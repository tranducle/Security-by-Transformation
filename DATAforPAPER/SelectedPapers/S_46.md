### ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the relationships between environmental, social, and governance performance and the cost of capital in the European context. Using data from 489 publicly listed companies in the STOXX Europe 600 index over an 8-year period (2015–2022), comprising 3317 firm-year observations, we analyze variations in this relationship over time. Our findings indicate that companies with strong ESG performance tend to enjoy lower costs of debt, reflecting favorable borrowing conditions perceived by debt financiers. Conversely, we observe a positive relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity, suggesting higher expected returns for equity investors due to perceived long-term risk. Furthermore, temporal analysis reveals that the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital became more pronounced from 2020 to 2022, potentially driven by heightened attention to sustainability practices and regulatory interventions. This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of the evolving role of sustainability in financial markets and its implications for corporate finance decisions.

## 1 | Introduction

In recent years, the integration of environmental, social, and governance factors into corporate decision-making processes has gained significant traction globally. This trend reflects a growing recognition among businesses, investors, and policy makers of the importance of sustainable practices for long-term value creation and risk management. As such, understanding the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital has become a topic of increasing interest in academic research and financial practice.

The significance of ESG factors in financial decision-making has been underscored by numerous studies highlighting their impact on firm value, risk exposure, and access to capital markets (Nirino et al. 2021; Wellalage and Kumar 2020). In this sense, various authors have shown (Attig et al. 2013; Ghouma et al. 2017) that companies demonstrating strong ESG performance tend to experience lower risks associated with reputational damage, legal liabilities, and regulatory compliance costs, leading to more stable cash flows and favorable financing conditions. However, the results are still debated since environmental concerns may increase default risk perception, thereby increasing the cost of debt (Kumar and Firoz 2018).

Despite the expanding body of research on the relationship between ESG performance and firm performance, the impact of ESG performance on risk (Nirino et al. 2022) and the cost of capital, particularly in the context of the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), remains underexplored (Priem and Gabellone 2024). While some studies report a negative relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of equity, attributing this effect to reduced perceived risk and a broader investor base (El Ghoul et al. 2011), others present mixed or inconclusive findings, potentially due to variations in regional regulations, industry-specific ESG sensitivities, or methodological differences in measuring ESG performance (Knoll 2002; Barnett 2007). Similarly, research on the cost of debt and WACC has yielded conflicting results, with some studies suggesting that strong ESG performance leads to lower financing costs by mitigating risk and enhancing transparency (Eliwa et al. 2021), whereas others highlight positive or negligible relationships, indicating that ESG benefits may not be universally recognized by creditors and investors (Atan et al. 2018; Fandella et al. 2023).

However, the literature often presents these discrepancies without thoroughly examining the underlying mechanisms that drive them. Several factors contribute to these divergent findings, including variations in ESG integration within financial decision-making processes, sector-specific risk profiles, and differences in regulatory frameworks across regions (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). For example, in industries with high levels of environmental risk, such as energy and manufacturing, strong ESG performance may significantly reduce financing costs by mitigating perceived regulatory and operational risk (Erragragui 2017). Conversely, in sectors where ESG factors are less material for financial performance, the impact on capital costs may be negligible (Fandella et al. 2023). Moreover, the extent to which investors and creditors incorporate ESG considerations into risk assessments varies, influencing the degree to which ESG performance affects debt and equity costs (Eliwa et al. 2021). Markets with well-developed sustainability disclosure requirements, such as those in the European Union, tend to reward firms with high ESG transparency by offering more favorable financing terms (Sharfman and Fernando 2008). However, in regions where ESG integration in investment decisions remains limited, the cost-saving benefits of ESG may not materialize to the same extent (Priem and Gabellone 2024).

Given these complexities and the inconsistencies in prior research as reflections of contextual dependencies and methodological disparities, by focusing on the European market, where ESG regulations are more stringent and investor awareness is greater, this study aims to provide a clearer understanding of the relationship between ESG and capital costs in a structured regulatory environment. Furthermore, previous studies have largely overlooked the evolving nature of the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital across different sectors, as noted by Morri, Colantoni, and De Paolis (2024); Morri, Dipierri, and Colantoni (2024). Many early analyses relied on static assessments, failing to account for the gradual adaptation of investors to ESG disclosures and regulatory shifts. The growing significance of ESG factors has only become more pronounced in recent years, indicating that markets may need time to fully integrate and react to these insights (Dallocchio et al. 2024). This study seeks to bridge this gap by adopting a more dynamic approach, capturing the shifting influence of ESG performance on capital costs over time and across industries, thus providing a more comprehensive understanding of its financial implications. Interestingly, in some sectors, such as real estate, ESG performance has a positive and significant effect during the pre-COVID-19 period; however, this effect diminishes in subsequent years (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). This pattern suggests that in periods of economic prosperity, well-executed social policies leading to high ESG scores are rewarded by the market. Conversely, during times of crisis, market interest in ESG standards tends to wane. As regulatory landscapes evolve, societal expectations shift, and global challenges such as climate change intensify, understanding how the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital evolves over time is crucial for both investors and corporate decision-makers.

In light of these considerations, this paper aims to investigate the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital in the European context. Specifically, we analyze a sample of 489 publicly listed companies included in the STOXX Europe 600 index over an 8-year period from 2015 to 2022, comprising a total of 3317 firm-year observations. By examining variations in this relationship over time, we seek to provide insights into the evolving role of sustainability in financial markets and its implications for corporate finance decisions.

Therefore, this paper contributes to the expanding literature on ESG integration in financial decision-making by analyzing the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital within the European context from 2015 to 2022. This time frame captures a critical phase in the evolution of sustainability practices, including the implementation of the EU's Nonfinancial Reporting Directive, approved in 2014, and the global disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, both of which significantly influenced investor behavior and regulatory priorities. Focusing on Europe—where ESG regulations are among the most stringent—allows this study to offer unique insights into how regional regulatory frameworks, shifting investor expectations, and corporate governance reforms impact financing dynamics. Moreover, the inclusion of the years 2020–2022 provides an opportunity to analyze the impact of unprecedented global challenges, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital (Dallocchio et al. 2024). As a consequence, unlike many previous studies that rely on static, cross-sectional data, this research takes a temporal approach, accounting for the gradual and delayed financial effects of ESG adoption (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024).

The findings reveal a differentiated impact: while firms with strong ESG performance tend to experience lower costs of debt—likely because creditors perceive them as less risky—they simultaneously face higher costs of equity, possibly reflecting equity investors' concerns about the upfront costs and uncertain returns associated with sustainable investments. Furthermore, the study identifies a clear shift in the financial relevance of ESGs from 2020 to 2022, when the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital became more pronounced, underscoring the heightened focus on corporate resilience and social responsibility in the context of the pandemic. By excluding the years 2023 and 2024, this analysis remains centered on a period of intense regulatory and investor-driven transformation, avoiding confounding factors from postpandemic normalization.

Our analysis reveals several key findings regarding the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital in the European context. First, we find that companies with strong ESG performance tend to enjoy lower costs of debt, suggesting that debt financiers perceive sustainable practices as indicators of reduced risk. This perception may stem from the belief that firms with high ESG ratings are more likely to demonstrate strong governance, operational efficiency, and resilience to environmental and social risks, thereby reducing the likelihood of default (Dallocchio et al. 2024).

Conversely, our results show a positive relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity, indicating that equity investors may view sustainable initiatives as signaling increased long-term risk or reduced short-term profitability. From a theoretical perspective, this reaction can be attributed to concerns about the upfront costs and uncertain returns associated with ESG investments. Equity investors, who typically require compensation for greater perceived risk, may interpret these initiatives as potential constraints on financial flexibility or as diverting resources from immediate shareholder returns (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). Furthermore, our examination of temporal variations in this relationship highlights notable changes over the study period. While the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital was not significant over the entire period, it became more pronounced from 2020 to 2022, contrary to previous results (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). This shift may reflect a growing recognition of sustainability as a determinant of firm value and the cost of capital, driven by heightened attention to ESG factors among stakeholders and regulatory interventions during this time. These findings underscore the need to consider the differing risk perceptions and priorities of debt financiers and equity investors, as well as the evolving market and regulatory landscape, when assessing the financial implications of ESG performance.

## 2 | Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

In recent years, ESG criteria have evolved into essential components of corporate financial assessments, with their importance notably increasing, even at the regulatory level, as illustrated by initiatives such as the European Action Plan and the NFRD, which have introduced a requirement for certain large companies to include a nonfinancial statement as part of their annual public reporting obligations. This statement enables them to convey their principles, accomplishments, and dedication to sustainable development to various stakeholders (Barros et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2022). Understanding the ramifications of CSR is a pivotal theoretical and empirical concern in fields such as business ethics, economics, and strategic management. Despite ample evidence demonstrating a positive association between financial performance and ESG practices, scholarly discourse on corporate social responsibility remains divided on its impact on financial outcomes (Lee et al. 2022; Hsu and Chen 2024). Some scholars contend that CSR involvement adversely impacts a company's financial performance due to the associated additional costs (Donaldson and Preston 1995; Wright and Ferris 1997). Conversely, others posit that CSR can attract critical resources, such as a favorable corporate image and skilled personnel, potentially enhancing a firm's financial performance (Waddock and Graves 1997).

Drawing from stakeholder theory (Freeman 1984), the integration of CSR principles within a company is considered pivotal for achieving financial prosperity by addressing and resolving conflicts among diverse stakeholder interests. In the context of this study, the primary stakeholder conflicts revolve around balancing the expectations of debt financiers, equity investors, employees, customers, regulators, and communities. Debt financiers prioritize stability, risk mitigation, and predictable returns, whereas equity investors often seek higher returns and may perceive CSR initiatives as potential risks to profitability (Tron et al. 2024). Simultaneously, employees and customers increasingly demand socially and environmentally responsible practices, and regulators enforce compliance with sustainability standards (Calcaterra et al. 2024).

The integration of CSR principles facilitates conflict resolution by aligning corporate objectives with broader stakeholder expectations. Proactive CSR engagement reduces perceived risk for debt financiers by demonstrating a commitment to governance, transparency, and long-term sustainability, which in turn lowers the cost of debt (Dayanandan et al. 2018). For example, effective environmental management systems can reduce operational risks, such as regulatory fines or environmental disasters, thereby reassuring creditors (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). Similarly, addressing employee well-being and customer satisfaction through CSR initiatives strengthens corporate reputation and operational resilience, benefiting all stakeholders (Husse and Pippo 2021). Therefore, stakeholder theory highlights the role of CSR in resolving conflicts among diverse stakeholder groups by aligning their interests, thereby contributing to reductions in the risk and cost of capital.

From the perspective of agency theory (Jensen and Meckling 1976), potential conflicts arise when managers, acting as agents for shareholders, prioritize personal agendas or CSR initiatives that may not directly increase shareholder wealth. Critics argue that CSR investments could divert resources from projects that generate immediate financial returns. However, the integration of CSR principles can mitigate these agency conflicts by demonstrating that CSR initiatives align with long-term value creation (Nirino et al. 2022). For example, fostering sustainable supply chains or increasing employee engagement can drive productivity, innovation, and market competitiveness, ultimately benefiting both shareholders and other stakeholders. Agency theory not only underscores the challenges and potential misalignment of managerial incentives but also emphasizes the importance of demonstrating how CSR investments contribute to long-term value creation.

However, some studies suggest that corporate commitment to sustainability practices may not necessarily translate into tangible financial benefits, as socially responsible firms may experience lower profitability than their less environmentally or socially conscious counterparts (Cerciello et al. 2023), owing to the increased costs associated with implementing such initiatives. These costs can manifest both as explicit expenditures and opportunity costs, as managers opting for ESG activities may overlook potentially more lucrative investment avenues. These divergent perspectives underscore the intricate dynamics within businesses and underscore the importance of concurrently considering a range of variables when evaluating the impact of corporate responsibility on a company's financial success.

### 2.1 | Cost of Debt

The literature consistently highlights a negative relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt, suggesting that companies with sustainability-oriented behaviors benefit from lower financing costs (Apergis et al. 2022). However, differences in findings across studies indicate the need for a deeper theoretical examination to understand the underlying mechanisms and contextual factors driving these results (Husse and Pippo 2021). For example, research in Europe (Eliwa et al. 2021) has shown that companies with higher ESG performance and transparency are able to secure more favorable borrowing terms, as creditors value not only a company's sustainability efforts but also its accountability in ESG disclosures. This underscores the role of transparency in shaping debt-financing conditions.

One theoretical framework that helps explain these findings is stakeholder theory, which posits that companies addressing the interests of diverse stakeholders—such as employees, customers, and investors—gain trust and legitimacy, reducing perceived risk for creditors (Attig et al. 2013). In particular, companies that show strong CSR performance often signal stability and sound resource management, which enhances their financial reputation and reduces the cost of debt. Similarly, adequate CSR disclosure can mitigate issues such as informational asymmetry and moral hazard (Ge and Liu 2015; Anis and Utama 2016), which in turn reassures debt investors by signaling reduced uncertainty and increased reliability (Ferri et al. 2023).

However, the relationship between individual ESG dimensions and the cost of debt is more complex. For example, environmental concerns such as high carbon emissions can increase the perceived risk of default, leading to higher borrowing costs (Erragragui 2017). This finding is consistent with risk perception theory, which suggests that ESG risk—such as environmental damage—directly affects investors' assessment of default likelihood. On the other hand, strong labor relations, as highlighted by some studies, can reduce debt costs by lowering perceived investment risk (Chen et al. 2012). This suggests that the impact of specific ESG factors on debt costs may vary, with some dimensions playing a more prominent role in reducing financial risk than others do. Furthermore, studies have noted that while effective environmental risk management can improve financing conditions, higher financial leverage resulting from such efforts can increase debt costs. This emphasizes the importance of considering financial leverage as a moderating factor in the relationship between ESG practices and debt financing (Sharfman and Fernando 2008).

Social initiatives, particularly those focusing on reputational capital, have also been found to reduce the cost of debt (Apergis et al. 2022), as companies with strong social practices are viewed as more stable and virtuous. This finding aligns with the concept that social engagement enhances a company's reputation, ultimately lowering perceived risk for creditors. However, some studies have highlighted the "governance paradox," where governance strengths lead to lower debt costs, but weaknesses do not necessarily result in penalties (Ghouma et al. 2017). This paradox suggests that creditors may give more weight to governance strengths rather than penalizing companies for governance shortcomings.

Therefore, despite the vast amount of research on this topic, the results are still mixed. Differences in findings can be attributed to variations in regional regulatory environments, sector-specific risk profiles, and different methodological approaches (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). For example, the increased focus on environmental concerns in certain studies may reflect regional sensitivities to climate-related risks, whereas the governance paradox may stem from creditors prioritizing transparency and board effectiveness.

In summary, stakeholder theory provides valuable insights into the mechanisms through which ESG practices influence the cost of debt. By addressing stakeholder expectations, reducing informational asymmetry, and mitigating specific risks, companies can enhance their creditworthiness. However, the varying emphasis on ESG dimensions, mediating factors such as financial leverage, and contextual influences necessitate an understanding of the relationship between ESG quality and the cost of debt.

Based on these theoretical considerations, the following hypothesis is proposed:

**H1.** *The ESG score is negatively correlated with the cost of debt of companies.*

### 2.2 | Cost of Equity

The literature provides diverse perspectives on the relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of equity (Galluzzi et al. 2023). However, these findings remain inconsistent, and their applicability across different regional contexts, particularly in Europe, requires closer examination. This section critically evaluates these differences, integrating theoretical insights to illuminate the contested findings and their relevance to this study (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024).

A general consensus exists that sustainability practices can influence the cost of equity, but the direction and strength of this effect remain debated. In the United States, a negative relationship was found (El Ghoul et al. 2011), and this reduction was attributed to lower perceived risk, an expanded investor base, and a decrease in litigation risk. These findings are consistent with signaling theory, which suggests that strong CSR performance signals a company's stability and long-term value to investors. However, the applicability of these results to Europe is uncertain, given the distinct regulatory landscape and heightened stakeholder engagement with ESG issues across European markets (Arnell 2023).

On the other hand, some scholars contend that CSR initiatives can result in competitive disadvantage. In this sense, directing corporate resources to CSR activities might divert wealth away from shareholders, reducing financial returns (Barnett 2007; Knoll 2002). This perspective aligns with agency theory, which suggests that managers might prioritize CSR activities that do not directly increase shareholder value, thereby increasing perceived risk for equity investors (Lee and Faff 2009). However, studies in this field often overlook the evolving role of CSR in European markets, where increasing regulatory pressure and investor demand for sustainability may offset the competitive disadvantages observed in earlier research.

Examining individual ESG dimensions reveals additional complexity. In the environmental domain, polluting companies face higher capital costs because they are excluded from "green" investors, which reduces their investor base and increases their cost of equity (Heinkel et al. 2001). Although effective environmental risk management can enhance resource efficiency, increased financial leverage may offset these benefits (Sharfman and Fernando 2008).

In the social dimension, a positive relationship between social factors and stock returns was observed (Marsat and Williams 2011) because investments in human capital create intangible assets that enhance a company's reputation and market value. However, Richardson and Welker (2001) argue that the costs associated with public disclosure of social initiatives can outweigh these benefits, particularly in markets where high disclosure costs are less tolerated.

In terms of governance, transparency plays a critical role in determining the cost of equity. Research (Hughes et al. 2007; Lambert et al. 2007) shows that higher disclosure quality leads to lower capital costs by reducing information asymmetry and investment risk. However, Easley and O'Hara (2004) warn that insufficient transparency can increase perceived risk, thereby increasing expected returns from investors.

Despite the growing body of research suggesting that ESG practices can influence the cost of equity, the results remain inconclusive, as variability across regions and industries continues to challenge a consistent understanding of this relationship. Differences in regulatory frameworks, investor preferences, and the measurement of ESG performance contribute to the inconsistencies observed in the literature (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). This study aims to address these gaps by investigating the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity, specifically in the European context, while considering regional regulations and market-specific dynamics.

Given that the majority of studies indicate a negative relationship between ESG ratings and the cost of capital (Hampl and Vágnerová Linnertová 2024), we believe that companies that score highly on ESG factors tend to be viewed as more stable, responsible, and forward-thinking. This can lead to greater investor demand for their stocks, as institutional investors, funds, and even individual investors increasingly seek to align their portfolios with ethical and sustainable investments.

Moreover, investors are increasingly prioritizing sustainability and responsible governance, often associating better ESG performance with lower risks related to regulatory, reputational, or operational issues. Consequently, these companies are less likely to experience high costs of capital, as investors demand lower risk premiums (Dallocchio et al. 2024).

Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

**H2.** *The ESG score is negatively correlated with the cost of equity of listed companies.*

### 2.3 | WACC

Although research on the impact of ESG factors on the cost of equity and debt is well established, few studies have focused on their effects on the WACC (Priem and Gabellone 2024). Among the measures of cost of capital, WACC serves as a more comprehensive metric than does analyzing debt or equity costs in isolation, as it reflects the overall cost of financing a firm's operations by incorporating both sources of capital. Unlike the cost of equity or debt, which provides a partial view of financing expenses, the WACC accounts for the proportional weight of each financing component, offering a comprehensive perspective on how ESG factors influence a firm's total capital structure. This is particularly relevant for firms making strategic financing decisions, as a lower WACC can increase firm value and investment attractiveness.

Among the few studies specifically investigating WACC, a positive correlation between corporate social irresponsibility and risk in firms with high media exposure was found (Kölbel et al. 2017). This increased exposure could lead to reputational damage and financial penalties (Atan et al. 2018; Priem and Gabellone 2024). In contrast, Mariani et al. (2021) reported a negative relationship between the environmental component and WACC, suggesting that capital markets reward companies with strong environmental practices by lowering their cost of capital. Additionally, research suggests that fluctuations in the cost of capital depend on the specific risk factors a company faces, with improvements in corporate social performance potentially reducing both systematic and idiosyncratic risk (Sassen et al. 2016; Lee and Faff 2009).

However, the results are focused mainly on the United States, and findings across regions and sectors highlight the importance of tailoring ESG strategies to local regulations, market conditions, and stakeholder expectations (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). Markets in regions more attuned to ESG issues, such as the United States and Europe, demonstrate a clear link between ESG factors and a reduced cost of capital, whereas results from other regions remain inconclusive (Fandella et al. 2023). In emerging economies, stakeholders have not yet developed the trust necessary to integrate ESG factors into risk assessments, with environmental and social components increasing the cost of capital. In contrast, the governance aspect appears to facilitate access to financing, which could lower the cost of capital. This can make it challenging to establish a clear, consistent understanding of the impact of ESG performance on the cost of capital, leading to a more generalized or underdeveloped analysis in the literature.

The broader literature suggests that effective sustainability practices reduce exposure to risk, mitigate its perception in capital markets, and ultimately lower the cost of both debt and equity, leading to a decrease in WACC (Priem and Gabellone 2024). This forms the basis for the third hypothesis:

**H3.** *The ESG score is negatively correlated with the WACC of European listed companies.*

## 3 | Methodology

### 3.1 | Data

This analysis focuses on empirical data concerning various components of the cost of capital and ESG scores for a sample of European companies from 2015 to 2022. The sample comprises companies included in the STOXX Europe 600 index, managed by STOXX Ltd., which in 2022 represented a total capitalization of approximately 12 trillion euros. This index was chosen because of its broad representation of the European economic landscape, serving as a globally recognized benchmark for institutional investors. It consists of 600 companies of large, medium, and small capitalization listed in 17 European countries: Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom.

The European context was chosen because European stakeholders tend to favor investing in companies that adhere to ESG standards, encouraging firms to adopt more sustainable practices (Eliwa et al. 2021). Moreover, Europe stands out as a leader in sustainability, particularly in areas such as environmental protection and climate change mitigation (Parker et al. 2017).

Consistent with prior studies, such as Johnson (2020), the sample excluded 102 companies operating in the financial sector. The exclusion of financial companies is justified by their unique characteristics and specific operating environment. Financial firms typically exhibit distinct capital structures and risk profiles, with financial leverage and exposure to specific risks differing significantly from those of nonfinancial companies. Moreover, they operate within a more stringent and complex regulatory framework, impacting their capital requirements and financial metrics, thus making direct comparisons with nonfinancial companies challenging. The impact of ESG factors on financial firms also differs significantly, as their sustainability performance is often tied to financing activities rather than commercial or production operations. Hence, the valuation models and data used for financial firms are not directly comparable to those of other sectors owing to the differing risk–return profiles, potentially leading to inconsistencies in regression analysis.

### 3.2 | Data Description

This research considers both the WACC and its individual components, namely, the cost of equity ($k_e$) and the cost of debt ($k_d$) as dependent variables.

Relevant data were extracted for the aforementioned sample from the LSEG DandA database, a global provider of financial market data and infrastructure based in the United States and the United Kingdom (Del Giudice et al. 2024). The cost of debt is calculated as the weighted sum of short-term and long-term debt costs. Specifically, the cost of debt at 1 year and 10 years is utilized from a designated credit curve.

The cost of equity is estimated by LSEG DandA using the capital asset pricing model (CAPM), which adds the risk-free interest rate to the company's beta multiplied by the market risk premium:

$$k_e = r_f + \beta(r_m - r_f)$$

where $r_f$ represents the risk-free rate, $r_m$ denotes the market portfolio return, and $\beta$ signifies the systematic risk measure of the company. The beta measure is computed through a market regression between the returns of the individual company and the returns of the reference market index, which is based on a 5-year period and utilizes monthly closing data. As a proxy for the current risk-free rate ($r_f$), LSEG DandA employs the yield of 10-year government bonds of the locally considered country.

The impact of the ESG score on the overall cost of capital is then analyzed, with WACC used as the dependent variable. For its calculation, LSEG DandA utilizes the following formula:

$$WACC = \left( \frac{E}{E + D} \right)k_e + \left( \frac{D}{E + D} \right)k_d(1 - t)$$

where "E" represents the market value of equity and "D" denotes the market value of the debt of the company. "t" represents the corporate tax rate.

In our analysis, we incorporated two key ESG-related variables. First, the ESG score, obtained through the Datastream software of LSEG DandA, offers a comprehensive assessment of a company's environmental, social, and governance performance. This score is derived from an extensive array of indicators covering diverse aspects, such as environmental impact, social responsibility, and governance practices. These indicators are obtained from various sources, including corporate reports, NGO publications, regulatory filings, and media coverage, providing a holistic view of a company's sustainability practices. Second, we consider the ESG controversies Score, which reflects a company's exposure to controversies surrounding environmental, social, and governance issues. This score serves as an indicator of reputational risk, reflecting how negative events or controversies reported in global media outlets may affect investor perceptions and, consequently, the cost of capital for the company.

In addition to these ESG-related metrics, we included several control variables in line with previous studies (Downs 1957; Alberts and Archer 1973; Jensen and Meckling 1976; Shleifer and Vishny 1986; Grabowski 2018):

*   **Size:** This variable reflects a company's size, typically measured by market capitalization. Compared with smaller firms, larger firms may benefit from economies of scale and greater access to capital markets, potentially leading to a lower cost of capital.
*   **Free float:** This variable represents the percentage of shares available for trading in public markets, excluding those held by insiders. It accounts for agency costs arising from conflicts of interest between management and shareholders, with a higher free float suggesting greater liquidity and potentially lower agency costs.
*   **Leverage:** This variable measures the company's financial leverage, calculated as the ratio of total debt to equity. Higher leverage indicates a greater reliance on debt financing, which may affect the cost of capital through factors such as interest expenses and perceived bankruptcy risk.
*   **Return on assets (ROA):** ROA reflects a company's profitability relative to its total assets. Higher ROA implies more efficient asset utilization and stronger financial performance, potentially leading to lower perceived risk and, consequently, a lower cost of capital.
*   **Industry:** Finally, we account for industry effects using dummy variables, recognizing that the cost of capital may vary across different sectors due to industry-specific factors such as the regulatory environment, competition, and market dynamics.

This suggests that including all these variables in a single model could lead to multicollinearity issues, hence the need to consider them separately. For the control variables, the average company size is €8.94 billion, with a mean free float of 74.114%, indicating insider participation below 30%. Companies exhibit an average profitability of 7.7% in terms of ROA and an average financial leverage of 26.6%.

### 3.3 | Methodology

The analysis of the relationship between the ESG score and the components of the cost of capital is conducted using linear regression models between the variables. For the data analysis organized in panels, two possible econometric models are considered: the fixed effects model and the random effects model.

The fixed effects model incorporates constant individual variations over time, capturing systematic differences between the entities under examination. This approach assumes that each entity possesses a specific effect that remains constant during the analysis period. Conversely, the random effects model considers individual effects as stochastic and uncorrelated with the independent variables. In this case, individual effects are assumed to be random variables, allowing for random variations among the analyzed units. To determine which of these two models is more appropriate for the analysis, a Hausman test was performed, as shown in Table 4.

The equations below therefore include the intercept $\alpha_i$, which reflects variations in the specific characteristics (e.g., country of origin) of individual firms, which remain constant over time. In addition, the error term $u_{i,t}$ is introduced, which includes both the generic error component of the model and that attributable to unobservable specific individual factors. To test Hypotheses H1 and H2, the following regressions are applied:

$$k_{d_{i,t}} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 ESG_{i,t} + \beta_2 Controversies Score_{i,t} + \beta_3 FREE FLOAT_{i,t} + \beta_4 LEVERAGE_{i,t} + \beta_5 ROA_{i,t} + \beta_6 INDUSTRY + u_{i,t}$$

$$k_{e_{i,t}} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 ESG_{i,t} + \beta_2 Controversies Score_{i,t} + \beta_3 FREE FLOAT_{i,t} + \beta_4 LEVERAGE_{i,t} + \beta_5 ROA_{i,t} + \beta_6 INDUSTRY + u_{i,t}$$

To test Hypothesis H3, the impact of the sustainability score on the WACC is observed.

$$WACC_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 ESG_{i,t} + \beta_2 Controversies Score_{i,t} + \beta_3 FREE FLOAT_{i,t} + \beta_4 LEVERAGE_{i,t} + \beta_5 ROA_{i,t} + \beta_6 INDUSTRY + u_{i,t}$$

We subsequently proceed with a breakdown of the ESG score to observe whether some individual dimensions of sustainability have a greater effect than others do. Since the ESG score is a composite measure of three different pillars, it is interesting to analyze the relationship between the score of each ESG pillar and the cost of capital. Below are the models that examine the impact of the E, S, and G pillars on $k_d$:

$$k_{d_{i,t}} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 E_{i,t} + \beta_2 Controversies Score_{i,t} + \beta_3 FREE FLOAT_{i,t} + \beta_4 LEVERAGE_{i,t} + \beta_5 ROA_{i,t} + \beta_6 INDUSTRY + u_{i,t}$$

$$k_{d_{i,t}} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 G_{i,t} + \beta_2 Controversies Score_{i,t} + \beta_3 FREE FLOAT_{i,t} + \beta_4 LEVERAGE_{i,t} + \beta_5 ROA_{i,t} + \beta_6 INDUSTRY + u_{i,t}$$

This methodology is applied similarly for the dependent variables $k_e$ and WACC.

To validate the regression model, we perform a variance inflation factor (VIF) test to assess multicollinearity. The results of this test revealed no significant multicollinearity, as all the VIF values were less than 2, as shown in Table 5. Additionally, we evaluated the normality of the data distribution using a normal probability plot. Furthermore, we examined the residual plot, along with fitted values and standardized residuals, and found no notable issues.

### 4.1 | Robustness Test

To ensure the reliability and validity of the findings, a robustness test was conducted to reinforce the earlier results. For this purpose, all analyses were reperformed using a pooled OLS regression model. This approach allows for a cross-sectional assessment that does not account for individual or time-specific effects, providing an alternative perspective on the relationships examined. The results of this robustness check are presented in Tables 9 and 10.

Notably, the findings remain consistent with the original analysis, confirming the robustness of the relationships between the key variables. These consistent results across different modeling approaches strengthen confidence in the conclusions of the study and highlight the stability of the observed effects. The use of pooled OLS as a complementary method ensures that the outcomes are not overly reliant on any single modeling framework, offering broader validation of the research hypothesis.

## 5 | Discussion

### 5.1 | Cost of Debt

With respect to the cost of debt, the results of this study are consistent with previous findings in the literature, particularly with respect to the negative relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt. This aligns with prior research by Attig et al. (2013) and Ghouma et al. (2017), indicating that companies demonstrating strong ESG performance tend to be less exposed to risks (and associated costs) related to reputational, legal, or regulatory issues arising from socially irresponsible behavior. In the perception of financiers, such companies exhibit more stable cash flows and thus have a greater ability to repay debt, leading to positive outcomes in terms of a lower cost of debt, in line with previous literature suggesting that sustainability-oriented behaviors lead to lower debt costs. Moreover, this study confirms the negative relationship between the controversies score and the cost of debt, which is consistent with prior research. This suggests that controversies or issues related to social responsibility can increase the cost of debt for companies. Furthermore, this study echoes previous research findings regarding the impact of social and environmental practices on the cost of debt (Chen et al. 2012). This analysis reinforces the notion that robust social practices contribute to lower financing costs, aligning with the observations of Apergis et al. (2022). Likewise, effective governance practices—particularly those involving board composition and high-quality disclosures—are associated with reduced debt costs, as demonstrated by Ghouma et al. (2017). However, the findings also highlight a "governance paradox": while strong governance practices are rewarded with lower financing costs, governance shortcomings do not appear to significantly influence the cost of debt. This finding suggests that creditors tend to focus more on incentivizing exemplary governance rather than penalizing deficiencies in governance standards.

### 5.2 | Cost of Equity

With respect to the cost of equity, the results of the study diverge from some previous findings regarding the relationship between ESG factors and the cost of equity. Hypothesis H2, which anticipated a negative correlation between $k_e$ and ESG performance, was not supported by the results, which aligns with the arguments of Knoll (2002) and Barnett (2007). Interestingly, that study revealed a positive relationship between the governance pillar and $k_e$, which stands in contrast to some earlier research findings. For example, studies by Hughes et al. (2007) and Lambert et al. (2007) have suggested that enhanced disclosure quality is associated with a lower cost of capital, implying that increased transparency in corporate governance reduces $k_e$. However, the findings of the current study indicate a different dynamic, suggesting that higher governance scores are instead positively correlated with $k_e$. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of the relationship between governance practices and equity financing costs, which may vary based on contextual factors such as regional regulatory frameworks or investor expectations. Investors may perceive strong governance practices as indicative of management prioritizing long-term sustainability over short-term profits, and they may demand a higher risk premium for holding the company's equity. This perception could arise from concerns that the company's focus on governance and sustainability may limit its ability to maximize immediate returns, leading to increased uncertainty about future profitability.

### 5.3 | WACC

With respect to the WACC, the results of this research indicate a lack of a statistical relationship between ESG performance and the WACC, but with individual pillars of ESG performance having differing impacts on the cost of capital, they offer insights that align with and diverge from those of previous studies.

The observation that the social pillar has a negative impact on the WACC, thereby reducing the cost of capital and increasing firm value, is consistent with previous studies, such as Mariani et al. (2021). This suggests that firms with strong social practices may enjoy lower financing costs, potentially due to reduced risk perception and increased stakeholder trust.

The research also highlights a positive effect of the governance pillar on the WACC, suggesting that strong governance practices enable firms to access more borrowing opportunities. However, this finding contrasts with those of some prior studies. For example, Priem and Gabellone (2024) identified a positive correlation between ESG scores and WACC, which they attributed to a lack of trust in corporate ESG initiatives in emerging economies. This divergence underscores the role of contextual factors, such as differing legal frameworks and variations in investor perceptions, in shaping the relationship between governance practices and the cost of capital. Furthermore, this study revealed no significant impact of the environmental pillar on the WACC, which aligns with the findings of Fandella et al. (2023), who similarly reported that ESG scores do not strongly influence the cost of capital in emerging economies. This outcome suggests that while environmental sustainability is increasingly prioritized globally, its direct effect on capital costs remains limited in certain contexts. The contributing factors may include the maturity of regulatory frameworks, the relative weight of environmental concerns in investor decision-making, and the degree of enforcement of sustainability standards.

The overall lack of a statistical relationship between the ESG and WACC observed in this research is consistent with the findings of Fandella et al. (2023). This suggests that while individual pillars of ESG may have varying impacts on the cost of capital, when considered collectively, their influence on WACC may not be statistically significant. This could be due to the complex interplay of different ESG factors and their varying importance across different contexts.

## 6 | Conclusions

### 6.1 | Contribution

In conclusion, this study explored the complex interplay between ESG performance and the cost of capital within the evolving economic environments of European countries, which were early adopters of stringent ESG regulations influenced by policies such as the NFRD. Drawing on a comprehensive dataset spanning from 2015 to 2022 and employing robust econometric models, the empirical analysis provides valuable insights into the financial dynamics of companies embracing sustainability practices.

The empirical findings shed light on several key points from which to extract contributions. First, our findings contribute to the debate on whether ESG issues are value-enhancing or value-neutral by showing that an improved ESG score can increase firm value by reducing a firm's cost of equity capital (Chen et al. 2023). This would mean that ESG practices represent a part of a firm's communication tools to decrease information asymmetries between managers and investors (Reverte 2012). In other words, we test how ESG practices are called to produce a better market valuation of a firm.

Second, despite the existence of mature theoretical models on corporate social responsibility and the increasing significance of global objectives such as sustainable development goals (SDGs), considerable heterogeneity in ESG scoring standards remains. This not only poses a challenge for standardization but also significantly influences the perception of risk associated with business activities by both investors and financiers.

Of particular interest is the empirical analysis of the relationship between corporate sustainability efforts and the cost of capital. On the one hand, such efforts translate into a reduction in the cost of debt, suggesting that sustainable practices are currently perceived by financiers as a risk-reducing factor, leading to more favorable financing conditions for companies with a strong sustainability orientation. On the other hand, sustainability efforts appear to have the opposite effect on the cost of equity, which increases linearly with sustainability scores. This increase seems to reflect higher return expectations accompanying shareholders' perception of increased risk associated with long-term sustainability strategies.

The significant divergence in perspectives between debt capital providers, on the one hand, and shareholders, on the other hand, poses a challenge for corporate managers, who are tasked with carefully balancing sustainability initiatives and the allocation of corporate resources, considering the expected effect on the cost of each capital source. Furthermore, this study highlights that the relationship between ESG scores and the cost of capital components is not consistent throughout the entire observation period, with a more pronounced relationship observed in the period 2020–2022.

### 6.2 | Practical Implications

The practical implications for investors, regulators, and policy-makers globally become apparent when examining the significance of ESG controversies in the context of market value across different countries. Our research highlights the importance of these findings for investors, who should consider ESG controversy data when making investment decisions. As competition intensifies in international capital markets, the differing impacts of ESG controversies on valuations across nations should motivate market regulators to enhance ESG performance. Moreover, it is extremely important that policy-makers understand how capital markets price ESG activities and regulate them accordingly.

### 6.3 | Theoretical Implications

This study makes important contributions to the theoretical understanding of ESG performance by providing insights into the fields of behavioral finance, stakeholder theory, and corporate finance. Our findings support the growing view that ESG performance can act as a risk-reducing signal to debt capital providers, aligning with studies suggesting that strong ESG practices enhance transparency, reduce information asymmetries, and ultimately lower perceived risk (Chen et al. 2023). In line with Dasgupta (2022), we emphasize that ESG initiatives should not merely be reactive tools for repairing reputational damage following controversies but should also be viewed as strategic, preventive measures designed to mitigate future risks and enhance firm resilience (Nirino et al. 2021; La Rosa and Bernini 2022). The divergent effects of ESG performance on different components of the cost of capital—reducing debt costs while increasing equity costs—introduce complexity into the traditional view that ESG practices are unilaterally value-enhancing. This dynamic may reflect equity investors' perceptions of ESG strategies as long-term investments that entail opportunity costs or delayed profitability, which is consistent with behavioral finance perspectives on risk and return expectations (Morri, Colantoni, et al. 2024; Morri, Dipierri, et al. 2024). Furthermore, the heterogeneity in ESG scoring systems and the evolving nature of stakeholder expectations raise important theoretical questions about how ESG information is interpreted and priced in financial markets, reinforcing the need for future models that capture these evolving dynamics.

### 6.4 | Future Research and Limitations

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, it is essential to acknowledge several limitations that may impact the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. However, these limitations can be overcome by further studies, as follows. First, the data were collected from the LSEG DandA database; combining our data with other data gathered from Refinitiv Eikon would be useful (Fandella et al. 2023). Second, the sample of companies included in the STOXX600 index has uniformly high ESG scores, limiting the analysis to cases that are not representative of severe sustainability issues. Third, the empirical analysis focused exclusively on European industrial and commercial companies, excluding banks and financial sector firms, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, this study focused on European countries, and the results may not be directly applicable to other regions or countries with different economic structures and regulatory environments, such as non-EU countries.

Future research could explore the nonlinear relationships between ESG scores and the cost of capital and investigate the long-term implications of sustainability performance standardization for capital costs to identify possible inflection or tipping points. Investigating additional mediating and/or moderating factors, such as management diversification and governance structures, that impact the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity could enhance our comprehension of these dynamics (Chen et al. 2024). In doing so, future contributions may focus on comparative studies of the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital in contexts characterized by less mature sustainability awareness (such as BRICS) (Chen et al. 2024; Fandella et al. 2023).
