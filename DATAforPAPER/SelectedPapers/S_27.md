# Impact of environmental, social, and governance disclosures on debt financing decisions: evidence from India

Poornima S¹ · Gopalakrishna B V¹ · Mithun Samanta²

Received: 22 August 2024 / Accepted: 28 July 2025
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2025

**Abstract**
In recent years, the incorporation of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) disclosures into corporate strategic planning has attracted global attention, yet its implications for firms’ capital structure decisions remain underexplored, particularly in emerging markets. In light of India’s dynamic financial and regulatory developments, examining the impact of ESG disclosures on corporate debt strategies is relevant. The study explores the association between ESG disclosure and corporate debt financing practices in the Indian context. Utilizing a panel of 692 listed firms from 2011 to 2022, the study employs panel regression and two-step system GMM techniques to investigate how ESG disclosure influences access to long-term debt, cost of debt, target leverage, speed of leverage adjustment, and financial constraints. The results indicate that companies that provide extensive ESG disclosures tend to secure long-term debt at lower costs, suggesting that lenders perceive strong ESG disclosure as a signal of lower risk. Furthermore, these firms maintain their target leverage and exhibit faster adjustments when they deviate from it, reflecting enhanced financial discipline and reduced adjustment costs. Finally, high ESG disclosing firms experience significantly fewer financial constraints, emphasizing the significance of ESG in improving creditworthiness and access to capital. The results remain consistent across various model specifications and alternative variable measures. The study contributes novel empirical insights on the impact of ESG on capital structure and offers practical implications to corporate managers, institutional investors, and regulatory authorities by underlining the prominent role of transparent ESG reporting in fostering responsible financial decision-making.

**Keywords** ESG · Capital structure · Agency theory · Pecking order theory · India

## 1 Introduction

ESG integration has become crucial in modern business strategies, driven by international frameworks like the Paris Agreement and the UN SDGs. ESG investments worldwide reached over $30 trillion in 2022 and forecasted to rise beyond $40 trillion by 2030, comprising over 25% of all assets under management (Bloomberg Intelligence Report 2024¹). Similarly, ESG-focused institutional investments are expected to grow by 84%, reaching US$33.9 trillion by 2026, and will account for 21.5% of global assets under management (PwC²). This rapid growth reflects the increasing prioritization of sustainability in global investment strategies. In India, ESG has Shifted from a niche concern to a core corporate focus, with ESG investments, estimated at USD 255.21 million in 2021, are anticipated to increase at 7.42% annually by 2030. The global emphasis on ESG issues is altering corporate debt-management practices, as ESG disclosures have become critical for creditors to assess risk and long-term sustainability. Firms with strong ESG records signal lower operational and reputational risks, which enhances creditor confidence and allows for lower-cost financing (Sharfman & Fernando, 2008). In emerging economies, ESG disclosure practices facilitate entry into international credit markets and attract international creditors (Dhaliwal et al., 2011; Cheng et al., 2014b), highlighting the global importance of ESG disclosure in debt financing.

Recent literature increasingly emphasizes the effect of ESG disclosure in shaping various financial factors, including firm valuation (Wong et al., 2021), financial performance (Zhou et al., 2022; Malik & Kashiramka, 2024; Jin, 2025; Sylos Labini et al., 2025), risk management (Menla Ali et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2024; Setiarini et al., 2023), credit ratings (Jang et al., 2020), investment efficiency (Donghui et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024; Liu & Tian, 2021), and digital transformation (Chen & Ren, 2025; Oliveira et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025). Thus, prior studies offer limited insight into the connection between ESG disclosure and firms’ capital structure decisions, specifically in emerging economies. Existing evidence on the ESG-capital structure relationship is predominantly documented in developed economies such as Europe (Gallucci et al., 2025; La Rosa & Bernini 2022; Krištofík et al., 2022) and the United States (Newton et al., 2023; Tawfiq et al., 2024). However, in many emerging countries, the ESG-capital structure nexus remains limited due to the absence of standardized disclosure frameworks, varying institutional environments, evolving regulatory regimes, and uneven capital market development (Malik & Kashiramka, 2024, 2025).

We address this critical gap by examining how ESG disclosure shapes debt structure in the Indian context. There are several compelling reasons to investigate the ESG–capital structure nexus within the Indian context. First, unlike developed economies with well-institutionalized ESG norms, India’s ESG reporting remains in a formative stage. Despite the implementation of mandatory “Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR)” in recent years, the standard of ESG disclosures varies significantly (Hasan et al., 2022), facilitating the examination of how firms adapt ESG disclosure practices amid evolving regulations. Second, India’s evolving regulatory landscape has been particularly dynamic, starting from the voluntary “Business Responsibility Report (BRR)” in 2012 to the mandated BRSR framework for the top 1000 listed firms beginning FY 2022–23. This shift reflects a growing commitment to aligning with global ESG benchmarks (Securities and Exchange Board of India, 2021³), offering a timely setting to assess whether increased disclosure obligations influence capital structure optimization and debt market access. Third, India’s corporate financing ecosystem is distinctively marked by bank-dominated debt markets, high ownership concentration, and varied degrees of institutional investor activism (Sarkar & Sarkar, 2008), transcending regulatory and reporting dynamics. Unlike market-based systems in developed economies, these elements influence financial flexibility and capital structure decisions. Finally, despite most of the current Indian literature focusing on ESG’s relationship with stock returns, firm value, or sustainability reporting (Kumar et al., 2021), empirical research examining its implications for leverage decisions is limited. In response to this research gap, the current study examines the influence of ESG reporting on debt funding dynamics by investigating four core research questions.

1. How does ESG disclosure affect firms’ access to long-term debt financing?
2. How does increased ESG disclosure affect a firm’s cost of debt financing?
3. Are firms with stronger ESG disclosure practices more efficient in achieving their target leverage?
4. Do firms with higher ESG disclosure scores face fewer financial constraints?

Building on these questions, the current study addresses the gaps by examining the impact of ESG reporting practices on long-term corporate debt borrowing behaviour in India. It examines whether entities with robust ESG disclosure policies have improved access to credit, lower cost of borrowing, and more effective target leverage attainment. The results will provide insights for companies, policymakers, and creditors by extending agency theory, pecking order theory, and stakeholder theory.

ESG disclosure policies are crucial in influencing debt financing by resolving concerns such as access to foreign capital, governance risk, and information asymmetry. In economies with less developed financial systems, creditors and Investors place significant importance on transparent and reliable ESG disclosures, as they mitigate information asymmetry (Cheng et al., 2014b). Improved disclosure also strengthens internal governance systems, thereby minimizing agency conflicts and integrating managerial actions with sustainable value creation (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Freeman 1984). Empirical result shows that companies with strong ESG disclosure face lower financial constraints (Cheng et al., 2014a, b) and experience reduced financing costs attributable to improved risk assessment perceptions among creditors (Eliwa et al., 2021; Lavin & Montecinos-Pearce, 2022). Specifically, proactive environmental practices have been linked to tighter credit spreads and reduced debt costs (Sharfman & Fernando, 2008), suggesting that ESG-disclosing firms are better positioned to secure long-term financing on favorable terms.

Next, to reinforce our first two hypotheses, we examine whether ESG disclosure enhances firms’ ability to access long-term debt financing at lower cost, which helps firms achieve their optimal leverage more effectively. Prior research suggests that enhanced ESG disclosure accelerates the speed of adjustment of capital structure by reducing the informational frictions that typically hinder leverage adjustment (Yang et al., 2018). Similarly, Ho et al. (2021) and Adeneye et al. (2023) confirm that companies with superior ESG disclosures reduce the adjustment costs and exhibit faster adjustment toward optimal capital structures. Therefore, it is hypothesized that enhanced ESG disclosure facilitates a more rapid adjustment to firms’ target capital structure. We validate our argument that disclosure of ESG practices facilitates easier and more cost-effective debt financing, helping firms achieve their optimal capital structure more efficiently by demonstrating that high ESG disclosure scores effectively reduce financial constraints in our last hypothesis. Our final hypothesis posits that a firm with high ESG disclosure practices mitigating financial constraints enhances its ability to secure long-term financing at reduced costs. This, in turn, allows the firm to manage its debt more effectively, thereby achieving its target leverage more efficiently. High ESG disclosure of firms contributes to more favorable financing conditions by lowering perceived risks and improving creditworthiness, ultimately supporting the firm’s capital structure objectives.

Examining 692 Indian firms from 2011 to 2022, we observe that enhanced ESG disclosure correlates positively with long-term debt, indicating that companies with better sustainability reporting benefit from improved access to long-term financing. Further, we identify a negative link between the level of ESG disclosure and debt financing costs, indicating cost advantages for firms with robust sustainability reporting. Our analysis also observed a positive coefficient, implying that robust ESG disclosure is connected to faster adjustment to target leverage, suggesting organizations with enhanced ESG disclosures often adjust leverage faster when they deviate from it. Lastly, we observed that these firms show reduced capital constraints, uncovering the confidence of creditors. These findings enhance our understanding of the integration of disclosing sustainability with established capital structure theories, offering insights into the strategic implications of sustainability for corporate finance.

Our analysis provides important contributions to the discussion on capital structure and ESG integration in developing markets. First, his study offers new empirical insights into the relevance of the pecking order theory across companies with varying ESG disclosure intensities. Existing literature presents conflicting evidence on the relevance of agency and pecking order theories in explaining corporate financing structure decisions among Indian firms, the study findings indicate that firms exhibiting robust ESG disclosure practices exhibit a clear preference for debt, aligning with the concept of agency theory and pecking order theory, as improved ESG disclose reduces agency issues, enhance financial discipline and reduce information asymmetry, thereby makes it easy to attract debt capital.

Second, we contribute to both theory and empirical evidence by exploring how transparent sustainability reporting relates to the cost of corporate borrowing. The findings suggest that greater transparency in ESG reporting is linked to lower debt financing expenses, reinforcing the argument that enhanced ESG disclosure practices mitigate the information gap, improve creditors’ trust, and reduce perceived default risk. This converges with existing empirical studies (e.g., You et al., 2025), demonstrating that ESG disclosure enhances creditor confidence and improves financing conditions.

Third, present research adds to the expanding research body on agency and stakeholder theory by showing that firms with higher ESG disclosure adjust their leverage structure more rapidly toward target leverage when deviated. This finding suggests that enhanced ESG disclosure improves financial flexibility, enabling firms to actively manage their capital structures in pursuit of an optimal debt-equity balance. It supports both agency and stakeholder theory as improved ESG disclosures reduce information asymmetry, strengthen internal governance mechanisms, and signal a firm’s commitment to a broad set of stakeholders, enhancing access to external capital and reducing the costs of debt by facilitating faster adjustment towards target leverage (Flannery & Rangan, 2006).

Finally, we advance the literature on financial constraints in emerging economies by demonstrating that ESG disclosure functions as a deliberate instrument to ease the capital constraints. Firms with robust ESG disclosure practices face fewer financing limitations, as their increased disclosure lowers perceived risk and boosts creditor confidence. These results align with recent empirical results (e.g., Guo et al., 2024; Gallucci et al., 2025) that link sustainability disclosure to reduced financing constraints. Notably, our comprehensive empirical framework—leveraging an extensive dataset and multiple interrelated hypotheses—offers a holistic view of the ESG–capital structure nexus, setting this study apart from existing research in the field.

In addition to contributing to theory, the research presents actionable implications for Indian policymakers, firm managers, and creditors. Specifically, the findings suggest that ESG disclosure can be utilized by managers to strategically enhance access to debt financing, minimize financing costs, and improve capital structure flexibility as lenders perceive these firms as low credit risk borrowers. Similarly, creditors can also benefit from ESG metrics by embedding them within risk assessment frameworks to facilitate better identification of low-default risk firms. Finally, policymakers, the results emphasize the necessity of implementing unified ESG reporting formats to improve informational transparency, reduce capital market frictions, and implement practices that promote sustainability.

## 2 Literature review

Modigliani and Miller assert that in a perfect capital market, a company’s value is independent of its debt-equity ratio. However, when market imperfections such as information asymmetry⁴, agency problems, taxation, and interest rates are considered, capital structure becomes a key determinant. This led to the development of theories like agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) and pecking order theory (Myers & Majluf, 1984), which emphasize the impact of these factors on corporate financing decisions. Agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) highlights stakeholder conflicts, where debt disciplines managers by limiting free cash flow but risks asset substitution. Pecking order theory (Myers & Majluf, 1984) addresses information asymmetry, prioritizing internal funds, debt, and equity to minimize adverse selection. Both theories underscore how governance and information dynamics shape capital structure in imperfect markets.

According to agency theory, market inefficiencies, particularly information asymmetry, arise when one participant (e.g., managers or firms) possesses enhanced information than another (e.g., investors, lenders). This disparity can lead to suboptimal financing decisions and higher capital costs (Myers & Majluf, 1984). According to agency theory, divergent interests among corporate managers and shareholders frequently give rise to agency conflicts that are relevant for financial decisions, where managers might avoid debt to reduce personal risk or pursue short-term personal goals that conflict with the company’s strategic focus on sustainable development and affect shareholders’ wealth (Jensen & Meckling, 1976).

Therefore, strong governance mechanisms, such as independent boards, institutional investors, and disclosing the sustainable activities, can help reduce agency issues and information asymmetry by ensuring management operates in favor of investors (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997). However, in the Indian context, where family-owned firms dominate and family members play key roles on boards, these mechanisms often become ineffective, as family interests may overshadow shareholder concerns (Jameson et al., 2014). In this scenario, integrating ESG disclosure practices reflect business’s dedication to long-term stability and responsible management. ESG disclosures enhance transparency, align management with stakeholder interests, and address agency problems. Specifically, for family-run businesses, ESG disclosure practices can signal a shift towards accountability and long-term value creation, improving both internal governance and external perceptions (Adeneye et al., 2023; Krištofík et al., 2022).

Additionally, the pecking order theory states that an organization’s financial structure decision depends on the Degree of disclosure gap of the financing source (Myers & Majluf, 1984). The theory states that managers use the capital in a specific order, first with retained earnings, then resorting to external debt, and lastly equity only when necessary. When a manager chooses debt first, this enhances the transparency as it has less information asymmetry (Grabinska et al., 2021). Further, firms disclose both financial and non-financial (ESG) information as a strategic move to minimize information gap between company and stakeholders (Oyewumi et al., 2018). Therefore, in the Indian context, where information asymmetry is particularly pronounced, ESG disclosures serve as an effective mechanism for corporations to increase visibility and decrease uncertainty among external capital providers. By disclosing ESG information, firms can lower perceived risk, improve their credibility, and gain improved access to debt funding.

Further extending to the stakeholder theory, it indicates that organizational success is assessed not solely on the basis of shareholder wealth maximization, but also on the corporation’s potential to adequately manage its relationships with a broader set of stakeholders, such as employees, consumers, lenders, suppliers, and the community at large (Freeman, 1984). Additionally, the theory suggests that the disclosure of sustainability-related information enhances corporate transparency and minimizes information disparity across the organization and its multiple stakeholders. This increased transparency fosters stakeholder confidence and contributes to enhancing an organization’s long-term competitive advantage (Adeneye et al., 2023). Moreover, enhanced ESG disclosure is linked with favorable debt financing conditions and better positioning for debt acquisition, thereby exerting a substantial impact on organizations’ financial strategies (El Ghoul et al., 2011; Gerwanski, 2020).

### 2.1 ESG, capital structure, and debt financing

Recently, ESG elements have been widely regarded as major promoters of sustainable value creation by equity investors, particularly those focused on sustainable investing. Strong ESG disclosure mechanism signals effective risk management, resilience to external shifts, and commitment to ethical practices, aligning managerial activities that balance the interests of stakeholders and minimize agency conflicts. Furthermore, robust ESG disclosure minimizes information imbalance enhancing investor confidence and lowering the cost of external financing. By addressing these key imperfections, ESG metrics improve governance and support sustainable capital structure decisions, enabling firms to balance agency costs and asymmetric information more effectively.

Despite the increasing focus on ESG factors among equity investors, research on how creditors perceive ESG disclosure practices remains relatively underexplored in emerging markets. In developed economies, studies show that creditors in markets with high transparency, like the U.S. and Europe, view ESG-disclosing companies as low-risk debtors, resulting in better credit conditions, including reduced cost of borrowing and extended repayment periods (Cheng et al., 2014a; Raimo et al., 2021). However, in developing economies, ESG ratings are not widely recognized and integrated by creditors while lending. In emerging markets, ESG’s role in shaping creditor perceptions is growing but remains less prominent due to limited regulatory frameworks, low transparency, and inconsistent reporting standards (Liang & Renneboog, 2020). However, as global ESG awareness increases, businesses with robust ESG reporting are viewed as lower-risk borrowers by international creditors and financial institutions (IFC, 2021).

The literature examining ESG disclosure’s effect on capital structure decisions reveals mixed evidence across developed and emerging economies. Some studies suggest that firms disclose social responsibility actions prefer lower leverage, favouring equity financing to maintain flexibility and mitigate financial risks (Ernst & Woithe, 2024; Mo et al., 2024; Tawfiq et al., 2024). Conversely, businesses with robust ESG disclosures are viewed as lower-risk borrowers by creditors, benefiting from better credit conditions, such as longer payback terms and lower interest rates (Lavin & Montecinos-Pearce, 2022; Raimo et al., 2021). Further, enhanced ESG disclosure reduces capital constraints by managing agency problems and information disparity, improving stakeholder trust and transparency (Cheng et al., 2014a, b). ESG disclosures further encourage debt utilization, offering cost advantages over equity financing and supporting sustainability initiatives (Wen et al., 2023; Benlemlih, 2017). Moreover, incorporating debt in the capital structure has enhanced ESG disclosure practices more significantly than equity funding (Al Amosh et al., 2022). Additionally, the quality of sustainability disclosure plays a critical role in determining debt costs, with greater transparency enhancing credibility and reducing borrowing costs (Eliwa et al., 2021). Engagement in disclosing environmental and social initiatives also influences borrowing terms and reduces the long-term debt costs (Ratajczak & Mikołajewicz, 2021). Conversely, poor ESG disclosures increase perceived risks, raising borrowing costs due to potential ESG-related liabilities (Apergis et al., 2022). Therefore, businesses that have robust ESG disclosure practices are more likely to opt for debt financing as a cost-effective and accessible option, particularly for long-term investments (Adeneye et al., 2023).

Further extending the literature on the independent ESG components, evidence on how disaggregated disclosure scores affect debt structure remains inconclusive. Governance disclosure constantly demonstrates an inverse relationship with the debt cost, as it enhances transparency, internal controls, and creditor protection (Shi et al., 2024; Dua & Sharma, 2024). The environmental pillar presents varied outcomes, while investments in green innovation are linked to lower borrowing costs (Yao & Bao, 2024; Attig et al., 2025), but such commitments cause initial restriction on access to debt in emerging markets due to high initial investment costs and uncertainty (Subhani et al., 2025). In addition to this, the social disclosure score shows mixed evidence on leverage structure. Zhao and Zhang (2024) report no significant role of social ratings on debt funding, whereas Tawfiq et al. (2024) reveal that social elements, such as employee engagement and community participation, positively influence debt structure, indicating that creditors and lenders do not associate social metrics during credit risk assessment as with environmental and governance factors. Hence, we present our first hypothesis is outlined below:

**Hypothesis 1** Firms with robust ESG disclosure practices have easier access to long-term debt financing due to enhanced transparency and stakeholder trust.

### 2.2 ESG and cost of debt

The increasing focus on sustainability has underscored the significance of ESG disclosures in forming borrowing costs. Lenders often perceive corporations with robust ESG disclosure as less risky debtors, resulting in more favorable debt terms. Empirical evidence consistently links robust ESG disclosure practices with reduced interest rates (Ratajczak & Mikołajewicz, 2021; Agnese & Giacomini, 2023). Further, organizations with superior ESG disclosure take advantage of cheaper borrowing expenses due to their perceived credibility (Eliwa et al., 2021; Raimo et al., 2021; Malik & Kashiramka, 2024). Similarly, You et al. (2025) confirm that companies with enhanced ESG disclosure significantly reduce the cost of borrowing, as these disclosures enhance the transparency and mitigate the risk. Additionally, He and Ismail (2024) demonstrate a substantial inverse link between ESG disclosure and debt funding cost, demonstrating that when a firm enhances its transparency in sustainability practices, it ultimately reduces the information asymmetry, leading to reduced borrowing cost.

However, emerging studies show that this relationship is not uniform across contexts. For instance, Tekin and Polat (2025) show that during crises like the COVID-19 epidemic, ESG disclosing firms in OIC countries were more likely to secure debt, whereas, in BRICS economies, ESG commitments initially limit access to debt in developing financial sectors (Subhani et al., 2025). These contrasting results highlight the significance of institutional quality, regulatory maturity, and macroeconomic context in the ESG–debt cost relationship. Despite these findings, ESG disclosure’s effects on debt funding remain underexplored in developing markets, where ESG frameworks are still developing. Hence, there is a need for more focused research on how ESG disclosure impacts debt financing across different regulatory setups. In light of the discussion above, we present our second hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 2** Companies with high ESG disclosure experience lower debt costs due to their reduced financial risk.

### 2.3 ESG and speed adjustment of target leverage

Modigliani and Miller (1958) proposed the concept of optimum capital structure, which refers to the appropriate debt-equity mix implied to maximize the market value of a business while lowering capital expenses. Firms reach this ideal structure when the advantages of debt offset the costs related to borrowing (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Recent studies, such as Chen (2024), indicate that many companies struggle to maintain an optimal capital structure, negatively impacting their financial health of the firms. Excessive debt can lead to poor liquidity, increased financial risks, and reduced profitability (Lv et al., 2018). Conversely, insufficient debt levels limit a firm’s ability to achieve higher returns and diversify risks effectively (Subramaniam & Hosen, 2023). Therefore, achieving and maintaining an optimal leverage ratio is crucial for lowering capital costs and improving financial stability (Ho et al., 2021). Stakeholder theory states that businesses need to balance the interests of both shareholders and stakeholders. ESG disclosure can help align these interests by enhancing a company’s competitiveness and risk management, signaling long-term value creation and stability to investors (Shakil, 2021). By disclosing ESG considerations, firms increase transparency, reduce information asymmetry, and reflect reduced risks and ethical practices, potentially lowering capital costs and improving debt management (Benlemlih & Bitar, 2018; Tang, 2022). Hence, entities with robust ESG disclosure are more equipped to secure and maintain their target capital structure, gaining shareholder and creditors’ support. As a result of the above discussion, we present the following hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 3** High ESG disclosures of firms positively relate to their ability to achieve target capital structure, as they have easy access to capital.

The costs of adjusting leverage affect the rate at which organizations achieve their target leverage. Firms initiate leverage adjustments only when the benefits outweigh the costs (Ho et al., 2021). Research suggests that sustainability disclosure practices reduce debt costs and facilitate debt capital acquisition, accelerating leverage adjustments (Eliwa et al., 2021; Nguyen & Phan, 2020; Huang et al., 2021). Additionally, improved ESG disclosure enhances the transparency, reduces information asymmetry, lowers agency costs, and speeds up adjustments towards target leverage levels (Aflatooni & Khazaei, 2020; Yang et al., 2018). Strong ESG disclosures attract creditors since they indicate lower risk and long-term prospects, increasing capital inflows further helps companies to adjust leverage more quickly (Chen, 2024). Ho et al. (2021) observed that entities with strong ESG disclosure incur reduced costs during leverage adjustments, leading to a faster process. Adeneye et al. (2023) discovered similar benefits in Southeast Asian companies, including reduced information asymmetry and quicker leverage adjustments. These findings show that companies with robust ESG disclosure are better able to adjust their leverage to attain an optimal capital structure (Adeneye et al., 2023; Aflatooni & Khazaei, 2020; Chen, 2024; Ho et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021).

However, recent literature highlights that the inconsistent ESG and leverage SOA relationship. Yang and Deng (2025) find that ESG disclosure disagreements across agencies significantly reduced the speed adjustment of capital structure, due to investor uncertainty, inefficient allocation of finance, and weak signaling credibility. In contrast, Tawfiq et al. (2024) report that firms with robust ESG disclosure, specifically environmental and social dimensions, adjust leverage more efficiently with better market access. In light of the discussion above, we present the following hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 3a** Firms with high ESG disclosure modify their target leverage more rapidly than those with lower ESG disclosure due to enhanced investor confidence.

### 2.4 ESG and financial constraints

ESG consideration is essential to alleviating capital constraints in the firms (Zhang et al., 2020). Prior literature indicates that a firm’s disclosure of sustainable performance can enhance company value through better financial performance and the mitigation of capital constraints (Cheng et al., 2014a). Stakeholder theory proposes that superior ESG metrics alleviate financing constraints. By enhancing information asymmetry and securing stakeholder trust through adequate ESG disclosure, firms can more easily acquire external funds (Hu, 2023). Similarly, when firms disclose their non-financial performance, it reduces information asymmetry, thereby enhancing the financing environment and mitigating financial constraints (Cheng et al., 2014a; Gao, 2023; Hu, 2023). As the ESG information quality improves, the financial constraints progressively reduce. Additionally, engaging in CSR activities allows firms to lower capital constraints, gain better access to finance, reduce cash flow volatility, decrease bankruptcy costs, and enhance their borrowing capability (Gao & Connors, 2011). Furthermore, companies with robust ESG disclosure are likely to decrease their cost of debt, thereby mitigating capital constraints (Gao, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Similarly, Donghui et al. (2024) support this argument, showing that ESG disclosure lowers costs for agencies and alleviates capital constraints and increases the effectiveness of investments, especially in stronger institutional environments. Zhao et al. (2025) also find that improved ESG disclosure mitigates financing constraints. Bridging capital market development and firms’ innovation capabilities. Thus, in light of the above findings, we outline our final hypothesis as follows:

**Hypothesis 4** Firms with higher ESG disclosure mitigate the financial constraints due to their better credit ratings.

Collectively, while the majority of the studies have analyzed the link between ESG disclosure and various corporate outcomes, its impact on capital structure decisions remains underexplored, specifically, in emerging markets. Existing studies are fragmented, predominantly focused on developed economies, and do not account for the dynamic financial and regulatory landscapes of developing nations. This study addresses these critical gaps comprehensively by analyzing the ESG disclosure’s effects on multiple dimensions of firms’ debt funding strategies in the Indian context, providing new empirical understanding of the importance of sustainability disclosure in optimizing capital structure.

## 3 Methods

This section explains the sample selection specifications, the variables employed, and the empirical methods used in the study.

### 3.1 Sample selection

This study encompasses all Indian companies listed on both the “National Stock Exchange (NSE)” and the “Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE)” from 2011 to 2022, utilizing data from two distinct sources: Bloomberg for ESG data and CMIE Prowess IQ for firms’ specific data. Additionally, we sourced macroeconomic data from the World Bank database for our analysis. Following rigorous filtration, which involved excluding firms in the utilities, insurance, and financial sectors because of their unique financial reporting norms and regulatory structures, our final dataset comprised 692 non-financial firms. To reduce the impact of outliers, all variables were winsorized at the top and bottom 1% of their distributions (Agnese & Giacomini, 2023; Eliwa et al., 2021). The reliability and authenticity of our reported data are ensured by the widespread recognition of all data sources among researchers.

### 3.2 Variable description

Table 1 outlines the definition of study variables. We employ four dependent variables aligned with our objectives, consistent with the prior literature on capital structure (Al Amosh et al., 2022; Javaid et al., 2021; Eliwa et al., 2021; Devos et al., 2017; Hu, 2023; Zhang et al., 2020, 2023). First, we use the long-term debt-to-total-assets ratio (LDTA) as a measure of capital structure. Corporate finance literature suggests that long-term debt serves as a more reliable indicator of leverage (Javaid et al., 2021). Moreover, since sustainable investments require long-term financing, LDTA is an appropriate metric in this context. Second, to measure the Cost of Debt (COD), we employed a ratio of the firm’s interest expenses on debt, as accounting-based measures capture the COD better than market-based measures (Orlitzky et al., 2003). Third, the target leverage values are obtained as the fitted values from Model 3.1, reflecting the firm’s optimal leverage level. Finally, to measure financial constraints, we employed two widely accepted proxies in corporate finance, the KZ Index and the SA Index, which capture firms’ challenges in accessing external capital (Hu, 2023; Zhang et al., 2020, 2023; Cheng et al., 2014a).

The independent variables consist of the ESG combined score (ESGC), an aggregated disclosure score across environmental, social, and governance dimensions ranging from 0 to 100, and individual scores for the environmental (E Scores), social (S Scores), and governance (G Scores) dimensions, each ranging from 0 to 100. These scores serve as proxies for a firm’s sustainability performance, capturing activities across all pillars (Gonçalves et al., 2022; Zahid et al., 2023a).

To control for business-specific and macroeconomic factors, we include key variables supported by the present literature. Where tangibility, the ratio of net property, plant, and equipment to total assets, reflects collateral value, enhancing borrowing capacity (Krištofík et al., 2022). Firm size, measured as the natural logarithm of total assets, positively correlates with leverage (Adeneye et al., 2023). Profitability, computed as operating income to total assets, reduces reliance on debt, consistent with pecking order theory (Moradi & Paulet, 2019). Tobin’s Q, defined as market value of equity plus book value of debt over total assets, serves as a proxy for financial performance, with higher values indicating lower debt dependency (Shi et al., 2024). Liquidity calculated as ratio between current assets to current liability, as firms prefer internal funds (liquid assets) to finance its activities, leading to reduced leverage (Al Amosh et al., 2022). Age of the firm, measured as nature log of firm age in years. Older firms have more retained earnings, reputation, and creditworthiness, making it easy for such firms to access capital at a lower cost compared to younger firms (Wen et al., 2023). Non debt Tax shield, calculated as ratio of accumulated depreciation/total assets. Adeneye et al., (2023) stated that companies with higher depreciation expenses issue less debt for tax shield purposes. ### 3.3 Methodology

To investigate the study's hypotheses, we employed two-step System Generalised Method of Moments (GMM) and panel regression methods. Figure 1 below presents the methodological framework, summarizing the statistical models used in this study.

To analyze our first hypothesis, we used Model 1, employing a two-step System GMM estimator to examine the ESGC-LDTA nexus. This method mitigates endogeneity problems, omitted variable bias, measurement error, autocorrelation issues, and heteroscedasticity (Padhan & Bhat, 2024; Zahid et al., 2023b; Xaisongkham & Liu, 2022) that are not accounted for in conventional panel estimators. Therefore, utilizing a two-step system GMM, that gives more accurate results compared to other GMM models (such as difference GMM, one-step GMM) (Roodman, 2009; Ashraf et al., 2022). In Model 1, LDTA is the dependent variable, $LDTA_{i, t-1}$ is the lagged dependent variable. ESGC is a combined ESG disclosure score, along with disagregated scores for environmental (E), social (S), and governance (G) factors, serving as the main independent variables of interest. Additionally, it also controls for Tobin’s q, tangibility, age, liquidity, firm size, profitability, non-debt tax shield, dividend, annual growth in nominal GDP, and the annual growth in inflation (INF). Additionally, the model integrates year, and industry fixed effects to accommodate variations across these dimensions.

**Model 1** $LDTA = \beta_1 LDTA_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 ESGC_{i,t} + \beta_3 Control_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t}$ (1)

Our second hypothesis investigates the role of ESG disclosure on firms’ COD using a two-step system GMM in Model 2, where COD is the outcome variable and $COD_{i,t-1}$ is the lagged dependent variable. We used the interest on long-term borrowing to the total long-term borrowings ratio as the measure to determine the COD (Miah et al., 2023). Similar to Model 1, this model incorporates the same explanatory and control variables along with industry and year fixed effects.

**Model 2** $COD = \beta_1 COD_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 ESGC_{i,t} + \beta_3 Control_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t}$ (2)

In our third hypothesis, we analyze the role of ESG disclosure on organizations’ target leverage using a two-step system GMM estimates. To examine this, we incorporated Model 3.2, where target leverage is the dependent variable, and all the explanatory variables and control variables are identical to those in Model 1. Additionally, the model integrates year and industry fixed effects to accommodate variations across these dimensions. Drawing from the current body of research, we conceptualize target leverage as being impacted by the dynamic attributes of the firm and industry-specific factors (Adeneye et al., 2023; Ho et al., 2021; Devos et al., 2017; Rajan & Zingales, 1995) Eq. (3.1).

**Model 3.1** $Lev^*_{i,t+1} = \beta X_{it} + \epsilon_{it}$ (3.1)

Where $Lev^*_{i,t+1}$ is the target leverage ratio for LDTA, and the X comprises a set of firm-specific factors including, size, tangibility, tax rate, growth, profitability, non-debt tax shield, dividend, and R&D. After predicting the target leverage, we incorporated the estimates into our model 3.2 to empirically test our third hypothesis.

**Model 3.2** $Lev^*_{t+1} = \beta_1 Lev^*_{i,t} + \beta_2 ESGC_{i,t} + \beta_3 Control_{i,t} + Year + Indus + \epsilon_{i,t}$ (3.2)

Furthermore, to analyze the significance of ESG disclosure on target leverage, it is vital to understand how quickly companies modify their capital structures to align with these targets. Therefore, model 4 is incorporated to determine the speed adjustment of capital structure using a panel regression fixed effect model, which helps control time and industry unobserved heterogeneity among firms (Baltagi, 2021), providing insights into the dynamics of how quickly firms realign their leverage ratios in responding to variations from the targeted leverage. By analysing the speed of adjustment, we can better comprehend the responsiveness and flexibility of firms in managing their capital structures, thereby contributing to a broader perspective of capital structure mechanisms.

**Model 4** $\Delta Lev_{i,t+1} = \beta_1 LevDev_{it} + \beta_2 ESG_{it} + \beta_3 LevDev_{it} * ESG_{it} + Year + Indus + \epsilon_{it}$ (4)

In the above equation, $\Delta Lev_{i,t+1}$, indicates the change in a company’s long-term leverage ratio from time $t$ to $t+1$. LevDev indicates the deviation across the present leverage ratio and the intended leverage ratio. ESG considerations can influence firms’ strategic decisions and financial policies, and we anticipate that the coefficient ($\beta_3$) on the link between the ESG disclosure and the deviation in capital structure adjustment reflects how firms manage their financial structures. Therefore, we hypothesize that stronger ESG disclosure could result in a more rapid adjustment in capital structure. As a result, we anticipate a positive coefficient ($\beta_3 > 0$) on the interaction between ESG disclosure and capital structure adjustment, emphasizing that businesses with better ESG disclosure respond more quickly by modifying their capital structure to address deviations.

Additionally, after analysing the role of ESG disclosure on various factors, it is vital to understand its effect on financial constraints. Therefore, we included our last hypothesis that entities with significant ESG disclosure mitigate the capital constraints using the two-step system GMM in Models 5 and 6. Strong ESG disclosure is believed to enhance reputation, reduce risks, and improve access to capital. By analysing this relationship, we aim to show how disclosing sustainable practices improves financial flexibility and resource access, contributing to a more refined insight into the advantages of robust ESG disclosure. Based on previous literature, we have formulated the capital constraints using the KZ Index and the SA Index (Benlemlih & Bitar, 2018; Cheng et al., 2014a; Hu, 2023; Zhang et al., 2020, 2023). Similar to Model 1, this model incorporates the same explanatory and control variables along with industry and year fixed effects.

**Model 5** $KZ = \beta_1 KZ_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 ESGC_{i,t} + \beta_3 Control_{i,t} + Year + Indus + \epsilon_{i,t}$ (5)

**Model 6** $SA = \beta_1 SA_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 ESGC_{i,t} + \beta_3 Control_{i,t} + Year + Indus + \epsilon_{i,t}$ (6)

Additionally, along with the ESG combined score (ESGC), all models incorporate individual scores for Environmental (E Score), Social (S Score), and Governance (G Score) factors as different explanatory variables to eliminate multicollinearity, thereby enhancing the robustness of our regression models.

### 4.3 ESG and debt financing

Table 4 reports the results of the two-step system GMM analysis, examining the association between ESG disclosure and businesses’ debt structure, proxied by LDTA. Our analysis reveals a statistically significant positive connection between the composite ESG score and firms’ capital structure. Particularly, in Column 1, the ESG composite score (ESGC) presents a significant positive association with LDTA (0.0534), indicating that businesses with better ESG disclosure are inclined to attract higher debt financing in the Indian context, supporting our hypothesis that strong ESG disclosure practices enhance the creditworthiness by reducing information asymmetry and enforcing stakeholder trust. Moreover, the individual components of ESG—E Score (0.0490), S Score (0.0819), and G Score (0.0630)—also exhibit significant positive effects on LDTA, as reported in Columns 2, 3, and 4, respectively. These results highlight the varying significance of each ESG dimension in determining corporate credit risk. Where governance and social factors directly impact the transparency and operational reliability, making them important for lenders, whereas environmental factors impact the long-term sustainability and have limited immediate impact on capital structure decisions. These results suggest that companies which disclose robust ESG initiatives have high access to debt financing, potentially reflecting creditors’ confidence in firms with strong stakeholder engagement and transparent governance structures. Firms engaged in disclosing sustainable initiatives face fewer challenges in securing debt financing, possibly due to lower operational and regulatory risks.

Further, control variables reveal significant relationships with debt financing. Firm size (0.0013) and Tobin’s Q (0.0019) are both positively associated with leverage, implying that larger companies along with higher growth opportunities have easy access to debt (Rajan & Zingales, 1995). Tangibility is positively significant (0.1905), supporting the view that firms with more tangible assets can secure debt easily (Myers, 2001). Profitability shows a negative relationship with leverage (−0.1705), in line with the principle of pecking order (Myers & Majluf, 1984). Additionally, the significant F-statistic confirms the combined relevance of the explanatory factors. Further, to assess the reliability of our results, we estimate the model with an alternate leverage proxy, total debt to total assets (TDTA), another broadly employed measure in the literature (Al Amosh et al., 2022; Javaid et al., 2021) and the findings aligned with those of the primary model (refer to Appendix 2), reinforcing the reliability and robustness of our conclusions across different leverage definitions. Overall, these findings align with agency theory and pecking order theory, suggesting that strong ESG disclosure lowers agency costs and information disparity., thereby lowering borrowing costs and supporting firms’ preference for debt financing. This extends current research by Uyar et al.(2024), Raimo et al.(2021), and Bai and Ho (2022), providing strong evidence confirming the ESG disclosure in facilitating access to debt capital.

## 5 Discussion

Our research investigation discloses a significant positive link between ESG disclosure and long-term debt to total assets (LDTA), suggesting organizations with superior ESG disclosure have more leverage capacity. Recent empirical work, such as that by Uyar et al (2024) and Bai and Ho (2022), also supports this conclusion, suggesting that corporations with robust ESG disclosure can easily access debt funding as ESG disclosure enhances transparency and builds stakeholder trust, enabling firms to easily access debt and increase leverage. Therefore, improved ESG disclosure increases capital access by lowering borrowing costs, enhancing credit evaluations, and promoting good stakeholder relationships, supporting this result. Specifically, each ESG pillar contributes positively to leverage, with social and governance factors exhibiting a more pronounced impact, as the lenders perceive the firms with strong governance and socially responsible practice as lower credit risk borrowers, whereas environmental metrics have no immediate impact as their benefits are often realised in the long run, emphasizing the significance of ESG disclosure in reducing credit risk and improving firms’ perceived stability and resilience. (Agnese & Giacomini, 2023). Further extending the analysis to COD, empirical results uncover a significant inverse association between ESG disclosure and COD, similar to Agnese and Giacomini (2023), Ratajczak and Mikołajewicz, and Raimo et al. (2021), who documented that businesses with robust ESG disclosure policies are considered lower-risk borrowers, allowing them to obtain funding under favorable terms. implying that improved ESG disclosure lowers borrowing costs by addressing agency conflicts, minimizing information asymmetry, and lowering risk perceptions. These results support the growing consensus that credit risk assessments are increasingly influenced by the quality and extent of ESG disclosures, driven by ESG-linked lending mechanisms, regulatory incentives, and lenders’ shifting preferences toward responsible corporate behavior.

Furthermore, It is observed that corporations with enhanced ESG disclosure show higher alignment with their target capital structure, implying more strategic and disciplined financial management. Easy recourse to external financing and lower agency costs enable these companies to achieve and maintain optimum leverage. This finding validates previous studies by Raimo et al. (2021) and Chen (2024), who emphasize that ESG-oriented entities increase stakeholder trust, enhance risk management, and strengthen financial planning. Importantly, we demonstrate that ESG disclosure exhibits a substantial positive correlation with the leverage adjustment. Firms with strong ESG disclosure practices rapidly adapt to their targeted leverage following deviations, as indicated by a significant interaction effect between ESG disclosure and deviations from optimal leverage. These findings are consistent with studies by Adeneye et al. (2023) and Do et al. (2023), who emphasised the significance of ESG disclosures in increasing stakeholder confidence and permitting flexible capital structure realignments. This adaptation reflects enhanced financial flexibility and proactive governance among ESG leaders as they navigate evolving market conditions and investor expectations. In addition, disaggregated ESG disclosure presented a varying impact, with social and governance factors having a high influence on SOA, followed by environmental factors. This variation reflects on the underdeveloped and non-standardized disclosure practice of environmental initiatives, whereas social and governance factors are more directly observable and align with the existing corporate governance norms, making them more relevant for creditors while lending. Furthermore, our investigation of capital constraints estimated by the KZ and SA indexes reveals that companies with strong ESG disclosure possess significantly reduced capital constraints. These outcomes correspond with prior studies by Zhang et al. (2023), Malik and Kashiramka (2024), and Gao (2023), who find that ESG disclosure serves as a reputational signal, minimising perceived risk and reducing frictions in capital market, thereby building creditor trust by promoting transparency and reliability, allowing for better access to external finance on beneficial terms. Overall, the empirical results affirm the notion that ESG disclosure transcends conventional perspectives of corporate responsibility and acts as a strategic measure that influences crucial financial outcomes such as financial accessibility, borrowing costs, and capital structure management.

This research enriches the literature by offering valuable insights. First, it offers novel insights that support the pecking order theory. It implies that businesses with stronger ESG disclosure easily secure debt, which is in line with the theory’s hierarchy of financing preferences. Second, we expand prevailing literature on how ESG disclosure reduces the debt cost, offering a robust explanation rooted in information asymmetry and credit risk mitigation. Third, we contribute empirical evidence of agency and stakeholder theory by demonstrating that ESG disclosure facilitates speed adjustment toward target leverage. Finally, the research enhances the current understanding of financial constraints in developing nations by demonstrating that ESG disclosure increases firms’ capacity to obtain external finance. Our extensive dataset and multidimensional empirical approach provide a novel and comprehensive insight into how sustainability disclosures relate to firms’ financial decisions, advancing the prevailing perspective on capital structure regarding the incorporation of ESG.

## 6 Policy implications

The findings of this research have practical relevance for corporate managers, creditors, and public policymakers based on our empirical findings on ESG disclosure and debt financing among Indian firms. The specific policy recommendations are outlined in the following subsections.

### 6.1 Implications for managers

The study reveals that corporations with enhanced ESG transparency benefit from reduced capital costs, greater access to long-term debt, reduced financial constraints, and faster adjustment to target leverage. These outcomes indicate that ESG disclosure should not be viewed merely as an obedience or ethical obligation but rather as a critical instrument for enhancing financial flexibility and optimizing capital structure decisions. For corporate managers, this highlights the significance of proactively embedding ESG metrics into core business and financing strategies. In particular, environmental dimensions, including adoption of renewable energy, efficient resource utilization, waste management, and carbon emission control, are often under-emphasized in current corporate disclosure but are increasingly scrutinized by creditors and investors. Strengthening these disclosures can help firms signal long-term risk mitigation, operational efficiency, and resilience to external shocks, thereby improving investor confidence, creditworthiness, and firm valuation. Managers should also institutionalize ESG reporting within governance frameworks to ensure consistency, responsibility, and compliance with worldwide sustainability benchmarks.

### 6.2 Implications for creditors

The study findings suggest that companies with robust ESG disclosure reflect high economic discipline and lower credit risk. For creditors, ESG disclosure can serve as a reliable proxy for assessing borrower quality. Integrating ESG metrics into credit assessment frameworks allows lenders and rating agencies to more accurately evaluate a firm’s risk profile, specifically in developing economies where information disparity is significantly high. Firms demonstrating robust ESG practices are more likely to manage their capital structure efficiently, adjust swiftly toward optimal leverage, and maintain stronger governance systems—all of which enhance repayment capacity.

Furthermore, these findings support the promotion of ESG-linked lending instruments, which align credit decisions with long-term risk mitigation and sustainable finance goals. Creditors are thus encouraged to incorporate ESG standards into investment plans to minimize exposure to financial and reputational risk while supporting broader sustainability objectives.

### 6.3 Implications for Indian policymakers and regulators

The findings underscore the need to strengthen ESG reporting frameworks through standardized, mandatory, and verifiable disclosure. When firms follow standardized reporting by following internationally standardised frameworks (e.g., GRI, TCFD, ISSB), it ensures consistency and global comparability. Furthermore, an independent third-party audit of ESG reports should be enforced to improve credibility and reduce problems of greenwashing. In addition, Policymakers can also offer benefits, including tax credits and subsidies, to encourage ESG implementation. Similarly, by promoting ESG-linked financial instruments, including green securities and debts associated with sustainability, to attract companies through regulatory and financial incentives, which can broaden the impact. Additionally, financial institutions and credit rating agencies should be encouraged to incorporate ESG risk assessment during credit evaluation and capital allocation processes. Finally, fostering regional cooperation through platforms such as BRICS and ASEAN can support ESG standardisation and enforce India’s leadership in sustainable finance by aligning domestic ESG policies with global sustainability agendas.

## 7 Conclusion

The present study provides robust empirical insights into How ESG disclosure influences corporate financing decisions, specifically in an emerging economy context like India, using a sample of 692 firms. By uncovering the influence of ESG reporting on major financial indicators, such as access to long-term debt, cost of debt, leverage adjustment, and financial constraints, the findings offer new insights into emerging corporate sustainability literature and highlight the managerial relevance of ESG initiatives in shaping corporate strategy to managers, creditors, and policymakers. Despite its contributions, this research presents directions for additional research. First, its geographic focus on India limits external validity. Cross-national analyses under varied institutional conditions can enhance our understanding of ESG-related financial effects. Second, the study relies on a single ESG rating source, i.e., Bloomberg, which may impact the comparability, as ratings across agencies vary due to different methods and data sources. Since Bloomberg relies on the voluntary disclosure data, which may lead to measurement bias. Therefore, future studies can employ multiple ESG data sources to enhance the robustness. Additionally, future studies can explore firm-level heterogeneity, such as ownership structure, board composition, and financial transparency, as moderators in the ESG–capital structure relationship, employing advanced econometric or machine learning techniques (e.g., FMOLS, random forest), and sectoral variation in ESG factors, further needs industry-specific analysis. Lastly, emerging research should examine behavioural and institutional factors, such as corporate culture, investor sentiment, ethical orientation, and perceptions of greenwashing, and their role in shaping ESG outcomes. These extensions would provide richer insights into the evolving interface between sustainability practices, capital structure decisions, and stakeholder behavior.
