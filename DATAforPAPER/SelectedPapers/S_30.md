# Heterogeneous Firms and Benefits of ESG Disclosure: Cost of Debt Financing in an Emerging Market

**Jaime F. Lavin $^{1,*}$ and Alejandro A. Montecinos-Pearce $^2$**

$^1$ Escuela de Negocios, Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez, Av. Diagonal Las Torres 2700, Santiago 7941169, Chile
$^2$ Escuela de Negocios, Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez, Av. Padre Hurtado 750, Viña del Mar 7561046, Chile
$^*$ Correspondence: jaime.lavin@uai.cl

**Abstract:** Current challenging environments pressure firms to improve their overall ESG performance. However, there is tension between the benefits of ESG disclosure and firm performance. We deepen the understanding of ESG disclosure’s contribution to firms’ cost of debt in an emerging economy context. This issue is critical in Latin America—a region with reduced financing, ample company heterogeneity, and scarce evidence associating ESG disclosure and firms’ debt financing. Using fixed effects models, for the 2015–2020 period, we study Chilean listed firms. We explore two association channels between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing. Through a direct channel, greater disclosure relates to a lower cost; however, through an indirect channel, disclosure interacts with growth opportunities—a proxy for firms’ prospective risk—and greater disclosure relates to a higher cost. Hence, evidence suggests that ESG disclosure affects the cost of financing in two opposite directions. Our results delve into how ESG disclosure encloses essential economic implications, particularly in countries that have recently adopted ESG practices: For regulators, on developing rules of ESG disclosure that consider their financial effects; for firms, on enhancing their ESG communications policies; and finally, for creditors, on the need to introduce ESG indicators in their financing decision-making.

**Keywords:** ESG disclosure; firm performance; debt financing; emerging markets; Latin America

## 1. Introduction

The worldwide concern about firms’ sustainable performance increases the pressure on corporations to improve their performance and their environmental, social, and corporate governance practices. Pressures for sustainable behavior emerge from various fronts. Stakeholders, regulators, policymakers, financial institutions, and in general, the business and social environment, encourage companies to make their corporate practices transparent through the traditional route of financial disclosure, as well as through nonfinancial disclosure [1–3]. Firms enhance the transparency of their behavior through non-financial disclosure, particularly in the context of capital markets; ESG disclosure complements traditional financial reports [4–6].

In the financial domain, a key contribution of ESG disclosure (ESGD) is to reduce the capital costs for companies [7] by minimizing information asymmetries in agency relations between firms and capital providers (stocks and debt), since it helps investors to better assess the risks of firms [8,9]. There is also evidence that ESGD has effects on corporate reputation, the competitive advantage of firms, and the decision-making of investors and capital providers of firms [10].

In addition, there is evidence suggesting ESGD impacts the behavior of investors in financial markets. Through the investment decision-making process, ESGD influences the buying/selling behavior of investors in financial assets [11]. Moreover, many professional investors such as institutional investors use ESGD as a proxy for the quality of management of issuers companies [12]. Combining environmental, social, and corporate governance information, ESGD aids investors in the task of the corporate analysis of future investment opportunities and their risk assessment, as well as in the evaluation of the level of transparency and future prospects of debt-issuing companies [13]. Thus, the study of the effects of ESGD on the performance of firms is a crucial point for improving firms’ strategic decision-making, particularly in developing economies where informational asymmetries and agency costs are broadly present in their financial markets.

However, most ESGD empirical studies focus on developed economies, where information is more abundant, ownership is less concentrated, and firms confront a more equitable and less volatile growth environment, all of which differs from the reality of any developing economy. Moreover, most studies assume that a greater ESGD affects firms analogously. In this paper, we study how firm heterogeneity affects the relation between ESGD and firm financial performance in a developing economy with a focus on the relationship between the ESGD of listed firms and their cost of debt financing. Thus, the current scarcity of evidence of how ESGD affects the cost of debt for companies in developing countries poses the question of whether the association between ESGD and the cost of debt financing (observed in developing economies) continues to hold in developing economies despite the existence of ample firm differences.

The emphasis of the ESGD literature regarding developed countries emphasizes the analysis of the relationship between ESGD and the equity cost of capital, and to a lesser extent, the cost of debt financing [14–16]. In developed countries, empirical results suggest that a higher level of ESGD reduces both the equity cost of capital and the cost of debt financing [13,17–22] to all firms alike. These results are essential not only for the financial costs of firms, but also for the intrinsic value of firms, and consequently the creating value process for shareholders and the rest of the stakeholders. A better understanding of this association is critical in emerging markets since its political, social, and economic particularities deter direct extrapolation from the evidence from the developed world [23].

The emerging markets context is a vibrant environment for studying the phenomenon of ESGD and the cost of debt financing for a variety of reasons. First, only recently has there been wider access to information on the environmental, social, and governance performance of listed firms. Second, the distinctively higher levels of ownership concentration in emerging economies imply larger differences between firms, which may distinctively affect the interaction between the different stakeholders of the firm. The latter is particularly relevant for Latin American firms where nowadays the interest in the ESG practices and ESG performance of firms increasingly affects their sustainability as well as generation new sources of risk. In particular, the expansion of the influence, scope, impact, and visibility of the operation of listed companies that significantly vary in size, liquidity, industrial sector, capitalization, and (therefore) stakeholders, exhibits new margins of risk exposure to investors beyond the mere generation of profits, which implies that ESGD heterogeneously affects the financial and economic performance of the companies.

In the latter context, we specifically contribute to deepening the understanding of the association between ESGD and the cost of debt financing by studying how firms’ heterogeneity in terms of size, sector, institutional investor participation, growth opportunities, and ownership structure affect the relationship between ESGD and the cost of debt financing. We argue that the reduction in risk provided by ESG information reduces the cost of debt differently for different firms depending on their features (size, sector, institutional investor participation, growth opportunities, and ownership structure). Thus, solely controlling by firm characteristics and studying the direct effect of ESGD on the cost of financing assumes that all firms are equally affected (usually benefited) by providing more information to the market regardless of how that information affects the risk profile of the firm. Hence, in the latter conditions, ESGD unambiguously and similarly affects different types of firms through a single direct channel. Nevertheless, we conjecture that the heterogeneity among firms in emerging markets generates different indirect responses to ESGD that may reinforce or mitigate the first-order direct effects of ESGD on the cost of debt.

Therefore, we empirically test the direct and indirect channels that drive the effect of ESGD on the cost of debt financing in an emerging market like Chile. In the main hypotheses of this paper (H2, H3, and H4), we test the conjecture that although ESGD helps to reduce the cost of debt financing through a direct first-order effect (H1), in the margin, ESGD would increase the cost of financing when more ESGD leads creditors toward a higher risk perception of the firm. Accordingly, we expect ESGD to have a heterogeneous association with the cost of debt financing caused by how the interaction of each firm’s characteristics and ESGD affects the firm’s risk perception in the market. Methodologically, first, we test the existence of the direct first-order association between ESGD and the cost of debt financing (H1). Then, we test the presence of indirect second-order relations between firm characteristics and the cost of debt financing by studying the association between the interaction of ESGD and three different aspects (H2, H3, and H4) of firms’ heterogeneity. For some firms, providing more information via greater ESG disclosure would reduce their information asymmetries with investors and the market’s perception of risk. However, for other companies, the opposite could happen. Since this extra information provided could be made explicit to the financial market, greater exposure to corporate risks not initially anticipated could occur, resulting in a marginal negative impact on the cost of corporate financing.

The Chilean context presents several attractive characteristics in our empirical study. First, it is a market that exhibits a combination of high-quality financial data coupled with the characteristic features of legal systems in emerging markets; namely, fewer regulations in corporate governance, high concentration of ownership, presence of control groups, and the participation of relevant institutional investors. Second, Chile is a member of the OECD (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development), which implies that the country has a high quality and wide availability of economic resources, and high-standard financial data. Third, it is not yet mandatory for listed companies in the country to disclose information regarding their ESG performance. Therefore, it is possible to observe a wide variety of corporate behaviors in relation to the adoption and disclosure of ESG performance. Fourth, in the Chilean market, there are significant differences in the size, sector, institutional investor participation, growth opportunities, and ownership structure between listed firms, which provides a promising environment to test our conjecture. Finally, the Chilean financial market is still similar enough to many other emerging markets where ESGD has not been deeply studied, especially in Latin America.

This research provides useful insights to companies in their effort to adapt to important environmental, social, and corporate governance regulations and information disclosure changes. These changes not only add new complexities to the strategic direction of firms, but also have implications for the economic value added by firms through their cost of capital, and particularly through the association with the cost of debt financing. Finally, from the Regulator’s viewpoint, our work contributes from an empirical perspective to help address and refine the legal frameworks that regulate the disclosure of ESG reports and that affect the decision-making of market agents with a look at contexts of developing economies.

Our data set considers the period 2015–2020 for the complete set of stocks belonging to the IGPA stock market index of the Santiago Stock Exchange. This dataset represents approximately 70% of the Chilean market capitalization. To study our hypotheses, we use time and firm fixed effects in a panel data model, which we now describe in further detail. First, to study the direct association between ESGD and the cost of debt financing, we study the existence of the direct first-order effect on the cost of debt financing related to ESG disclosure, using controls for the effects related to the dimensions of growth opportunities, institutional investor participation, and ownership participation of controlling shareholders. Second, we study the existence of indirect effects related to the heterogeneity of companies and their ESG behavior by testing the significance of the association of the interaction terms of the above firm heterogeneity dimensions and ESGD with the cost of debt financing of firms. Finally, we apply robustness tests to verify if our main models and results are consistent when using additional controls.

Our results show that greater ESGD is associated with a lower cost of debt for companies. More importantly, we find that firms with greater ESGD and higher growth opportunities have a greater cost of debt financing. The latter suggests that the benefits of ESGD on the cost of debt financing for the case of riskier firms would be reduced. Hence, our evidence indicates that the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing has two components: A first-order negative effect on the cost of debt financing, and a second-order positive effect of lesser magnitude than the first, but related to the existence of a positive relationship between growth opportunities and the cost of debt financing of firms.

Accordingly, our evidence supports that the heterogeneity among firms is a relevant aspect for assessing how ESG disclosure affects the cost of financing debt in a developing economy. More specifically, our results suggest that ESGD affects the cost of debt financing directly through the extent of ESG disclosure and also indirectly (with an opposite association) by providing more information about the risks associated with the firm’s growth opportunities.

The article is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the prior research and develops the hypotheses. In Section 3, we present the methodology, data, sample, and empirical strategy. Section 4 presents the results, Section 5 presents the discussion of the results, and finally, Section 6 exhibits the conclusions of our study.

## 2. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

### 2.1. ESG Disclosure in Emerging Markets Contexts

Worldwide firms are under a constant pressure to convincingly disclose and communicate to their stakeholders their environmental, societal, and corporate governance (ESG) performance. Thus, in a context of market accountability and discipline, ESGD helps firms to enhance their transparency. This greater and wider access to firm performance, nurtures a growing demand from stakeholders, managers, and investors. Thus, from a company’s perspective, ESGD enables managers to assess and compare their performance against a wider set of comparable peers [24]. Analogously, from the investor’s perspective, ESGD helps them to better assess the ESG activities of companies, improving the investors’ evaluation of the risks, and the inherent vulnerabilities as potential investable assets [25,26].

In emerging markets, ESG performance information also has a growing demand. That is the case for Latin American financial markets, where there is not only a high demand for sustainable business practices, but also great heterogeneity in terms of their business environments, regulations, board composition, ownership structure, legal rules, and protection of investors’ rights. These sources of firm heterogeneity generate a complex environment where the relation between ESGD and economic performance requires a deeper understanding of how ESG disclosure affects firms’ economic and financial performance.

There are two additional sources of firm heterogeneity in Latin America. First, the less regulated Latin American corporate environments imply fewer restrictions on the characteristics of the board of directors and the ownership structure of companies. There is evidence that these factors not only vary over time, but also provide favorable conditions to assess how these characteristics influence strategic decisions such as ESG measurement adoption [27] and ESG disclosure [28]. For example, regarding corporate financing, the authors of [29] find that legal enforcement in developing countries influences the ability of companies to raise capital. Second, the growing participation of institutional investors in companies issuing publicly offered securities such as stocks and bonds is another relevant aspect of emerging markets. Their presence provides the main entrance of minority shareholders to the Latin American directories, generating a connection between issuers and the general public’s preference for sustainable assets. Thus, the presence of institutional investors on the boards of Latin American companies [28,30] provides another source of heterogeneity in the strategic decisions of firms that facilitates the study of decisions such as ESG disclosure and its associated effects on firms’ performance.

The scarcity of evidence regarding ESGD effects in Latin America, combined with the distinctive attributes that enrich the complexity of the Latin American business environment, prevent a direct extrapolation of the evidence regarding the ESGD and economic performance reported in developed countries [23]. This lack of evidence for developing countries is crucial because there is recent evidence in developed countries of a complex relation between ESGD, different characteristics of a firm, the firm’s performance, and the firm’s corporate sustainability. For instance, using UK data, [31] study the moderating role of governance mechanisms on the relation between ESGD and firms’ financial performance. Similarly, [32] investigate the influence of the implementation of the EU Directive on non-financial information on the relevance of non-financial information on the value of firms. Furthermore, but in the context of a developing economy, using data from the Bombay Stock Exchange, [33] find a positive relationship between the profitability of a firm measured by its returns on assets (ROA) and the extent of ESG disclosure.

Finally, there is growing literature that suggests that ESGD has a positive impact on the corporate sustainability of firms in both developed and developing markets. For instance, studying listed companies in Asian countries [34], there is evidence that a good performance in terms of disclosing the implementation of environmental and social strategies strengthens corporate sustainability performance. As stakeholders exert pressure on firms for more sustainable behavior and a lower impact on society and the environment, adequate ESG behavior and proper disclosing policies become key elements of sustainability through adequate disclosure policies [2,3]. These studies highlight the impact of ESGD spreading beyond the borders of the firm, also positively improving the social value created by corporations. This social value creation is particularly relevant among emerging markets.

### 2.2. Corporate Disclosure and the Cost of Debt

The literature that studies the impact on the cost of debt of corporate disclosures cover different types of disclosure—namely: voluntary, financial, CSR, intellectual capital, integrated reporting, and more recently ESG.

Usually in the context of developed countries, most studies that focus on voluntary disclosure find that the cost of firms’ debt decreases with more disclosure. Hence, evidence suggests a negative relationship between corporate transparency and the cost of debt financing [9,20–22,35]. However, some studies challenge the inverse relationship between disclosure and the cost of debt. The authors of [36] find mixed results among UK firms after asking finance managers whether greater transparency lowers the cost of debt. In the context of developing economies, namely China, [37] do not find any association among Chinese listed firms. Considering the different business contexts, these mixed results illustrate that the relation between transparency and the cost of debt is more complex than what the studies in developed countries suggest.

A second strand of the literature analyzes the association between financial disclosure and the cost of debt. These results tend to confirm previous findings, indicating that greater financial disclosure generates a lower cost of debt. Behind this relationship is the argument that a greater disclosure reduces lenders’ and underwriters’ perception of the default risk of the disclosing firm. As a result, these firms face a lower risk premium in credit operations. The author of [38] finds that firms with high disclosure quality ratings from analysts enjoy a lower cost of debt, especially in situations where there is greater uncertainty about the firm. Similarly, [39–41] find that financial reporting quality in terms of its readability and content has a central role in assessing firm performance and ameliorating information asymmetries between lenders and firms.

More recently, a new strand of the literature analyzes the links between sustainability disclosure and the cost of corporate debt. The authors of [42] find that when Chinese firms were mandated to disclose their corporate social responsibility (CSR) reports, the major reduction in the cost of debt was among firms with longer reports and greater CSR scores. Similarly, there is evidence of a negative association between the cost of debt and the disclosure of sustainable practices among European firms [1,43], Australian firms [44], and Malaysian companies [45]. Analogously, in a recent study focusing on global firms, [16] find evidence suggesting that firms with greater levels of transparency in the dissemination of their ESG information are able to access financial resources at a lower cost.

Finally, there are studies that focus on the effect of intellectual capital disclosure and integrated reporting on the cost of debt. In this case, the findings also suggest that greater disclosure of information is associated with lowering the cost of debt for disclosing firm [40,46,47].

Thus, the literature provides evidence of the existence of a negative relation between the disclosure of information and the cost of capital. Theoretically, there is broad support for these empirical findings: information disclosure reduces information asymmetries among issuers and investors, and information disclosure enhances investors’ credit risk assessment of potential debt issuers [1,22,47].

In the case of fundraising via debt, the agency problem between lenders and firms coupled with the information advantage of the firms’ managers over the potential lenders regarding the performance of the companies causes lenders to highly value transparency and avoid opaque firms. Consequently, to reduce the lenders’ penalization, firms try to minimize their cost of issuing debt by providing detailed information that lessens agency costs and adverse selection problems. A crucial element in this process is the possibility that issuers hide essential information that could change the lenders’ willingness to participate in the credit operation [38]. Consequently, to better assess the issuer’s probability of default, lenders will collect all available financial and non-financial information on firms [48,49].

In the context of greater corporate scrutiny, ESG disclosure becomes a complement to the information that firms already disclose to the market and to its stakeholders. In this sense, ESGD provides additional information to financial markets that enrich credit risk analysis and the decision-making of lenders and other stakeholders. Thus, ESG information, thus its disclosure, is more frequently considered by professional investors in decision-making processes regarding firms’ assets and liabilities [11]. Hence, ESGD would have a positive effect on reducing information asymmetries because as a mirror of the execution of corporate strategy, it can serve as a tool to transmit information that is not fully included in the financial disclosure [16].

Therefore, we evaluate the following hypothesis that studies what we defined as the direct first-order effect.

**H1.** *There is a negative relationship between the extent of ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing for firms.*

### 2.3. Heterogeneity of Firms and Cost of Debt

The cost of debt financing of a firm depends on multiple factors, such as the industry to which a firm belongs, the financial robustness of the firm, and legal framework in which the firm operates. Specifically, the corporate finance literature indicates that the cost of debt is influenced by certain characteristics of firms related to their size, profitability, asset tangibility, growth options, and financial leverage, among others [50]. Likewise, the study of factors with a direct impact on the severity of information asymmetries in credit operations has also generated academic interest because it affects the cost of debt. In addition to the latter, ownership and corporate governance, accounting information quality, institutional ownership, and analyst coverage (among others) are other factors that have been studied as a complement to traditional corporate finance theories [51–54].

As the above literature suggests, in emerging economies characterized by high uncertainty levels and wide information asymmetries, there is a growing interest regarding the mechanisms that allow firms’ access to better credit terms. Some studies find evidence suggesting that institutional investors’ participation plays a key role in granting better financing access. By acting as a non-controlling shareholder or as a bondholder, institutional investors may force controlling shareholders to both enhance the financial disclosure and transparency of firms and improve their performance [29,52,53,55].

Another channel through which we conjecture ESGD affects the cost of financing is related to the risk embedded in the growth opportunities of firms. We argue that a firm’s growth opportunities influence their use of debt, their financial leverage, and consequently, their financial cost of debt. The theories of capital structure suggest that growth opportunities might be correlated with the cost of debt through changes in the mix of financing via equity and debt. Theoretically, highly leveraged firms are more likely to transmit profitable investment opportunities that depend on discretionary future investment because they are intimately related to their future availability of funds [56]. Therefore, firms that expect changes in their future growth opportunities should adjust their level of financing via equity, affecting their capital structure, and thus their cost of financing via debt. As suggested by [50,56], the market-to-book ratio is usually considered a proxy for the growth opportunities of firms. For their impact on the future capital structure of firms, the literature states that growth opportunities proxied by the market-to-book ratio have an impact on the risk perception of firms. Evidence indicates that high-growth firms could be associated with greater corporate risk, higher returns required on current and future debt issuances, and lower credit ratings [57].

The literature further suggests that ownership participation can also play a relevant role in the evolution of the cost of debt for firms. In conjunction with the role of institutional investors and their willingness to constrain managers and control shareholders to improve firms’ transparency, ownership also has a protagonist position. In companies whose capital is much dispersed, none of the owners have an incentive to invest in the control of the managers of the firm, as they will solely bear the cost of monitoring while all shareholders will benefit from this action. Accordingly, it is possible to observe a kind of “free rider” behavior among managers, and therefore they can unleash their opportunism in the management of the firm. In other words, large shareholders lead to better monitoring of managers [58–61].

Therefore, considering all of the above sources of firm heterogeneity, in addition to the direct first-order effect of ESGD on the cost of financing, we conjecture that the extent of ESG disclosure also affects the cost of firms’ debt financing indirectly through the interaction with elements of the firm’s heterogeneity. Hence, we considered interaction terms between the extent of ESGD and each of these cost of debt determinants (institutional investors’ participation, growth opportunities, and ownership participation of controlling shareholders). We refer to these interaction terms as mediating effects or indirect second-order effects. Moreover, to properly incorporate the latter elements from the corporate finance literature as factors of cost of debt financing, we included them as additional control variables in the econometric model of Section 3.2.

Accordingly, we consider evaluating the following mediating effects or second-order hypotheses.

**H2.** *There is a negative indirect second-order effect on the cost of debt financing between institutional investor participation and the extent of ESG disclosure.*

**H3.** *There is a positive indirect second-order effect on the cost of debt financing between growth opportunities and the extent of ESG disclosure.*

**H4.** *There is a negative indirect second-order effect on the cost of debt financing between the ownership participation of controlling shareholders and the extent of ESG disclosure.*

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1. The Data

We empirically test our hypothesis using hand-collected data for the period 2015–2020 from the main firms of the General Price Index (IGPA) of the Santiago Stock Exchange (SSE). By the end of 2016, the market capitalization of the IGPA was MMUSD 208,930, which represented almost 90% of the total market capitalization of the Chilean stock market. The dataset represents approximately 70% of the local market capitalization, and it is free from survivorship bias because we considered all the firms included in the index during the period of analysis. Moreover, the IGPA index accurately represents the main market trends since it is formed by the majority of the shares traded on the SSE. It is the oldest on the Chilean market, dating back to its creation in 1954, and since then it has become one of the main indicators of Chilean stock market activity.

We use three main data sources for the construction of the dataset. First, we gather the financial and stock market information of each company from the SSE website (www.bolsadesantiago.com, accessed on 18 October 2021) and its main financial market data services (www.sebra.cl, accessed on 18 October 2021). Second, for each firm belonging to the IGPA index, we obtain the financial information and the ESGD score indicators from the Bloomberg Terminal (www.bloomberg.com, accessed on 18 October 2021). And third, we collect information about locally listed firms’ boards and their property structure from the Chilean Commission of Financial Markets (CMF, for its acronym in Spanish). The CMF is the Chilean counterpart of the US Securities and Exchange Commission (www.cmfchile.cl, accessed on 18 October 2021).

### 3.2. Econometric Model

The model we use in this study aims to analyze the association between the ESG disclosure of listed firms and their cost of debt financing. Therefore, to properly capture the multivariate nature of the cost of debt phenomena, we follow [16,62,63], applying a panel data analysis with firm-fixed effects to capture any unobserved source of firm heterogeneity, and with year-fixed effects to capture any source of unobserved yearly shocks between the years 2015 and 2020. Although the literature applies a standard estimate of fixed effects, we apply Hausman’s specification test [64] to verify that this estimation is superior to that of random effects. The results show a Chi2 = 79.98 and a p-value = 0.000, which indicate that for our dataset and econometric specification, fixed effects are a better estimation method than random effects. Additionally, to increase the quality of the estimation, the model includes a set of control variables based on theoretical and empirical studies in order to adequately control for factors capable of significantly affecting the cost of debt financing of firms [16,47,62,65].

Accordingly, our baseline econometric model describes the relationship between the cost of debt financing ($ln\_cdebt_{it}$) of firm $i$ during year $t$ and the extent of ESG disclosure ($ESGD_{it-1}$) of firm $i$ during year $t - 1$, when controlling for the firm’s size ($ln\_assets_{it}$) of firm $i$ during year $t$, EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization) margin ($mg\_ebitda_{it}$) of firm $i$ during year $t$, the assets’ turnover of firm $i$ during year $t$ ($rotact_{it}$), financial leverage ($leverage_{it}$) of firm $i$ during year $t$, and interest coverage ($ebitdatoint_{it}$) of firm $i$ during year $t$.

Similarly, to capture the possible effects on the cost of debt financing by elements related to firms’ heterogeneity such as growth opportunities, institutional investor participation, and controlling shareholders’ participation, we also include them in the baseline econometric model as additional control variables, as presented in the tables. Accordingly, the variables we consider for each firm are the following: to consider the possible effect of growth opportunities, we added the price-to-book ratio ($pricetobook_{it}$) of firm $i$ during year $t$; to incorporate the effect of the institutional participation on firms, we added a dummy variable that takes the value of 1 if the firm $i$ has outstanding bonds (the presence of institutional investors like Pension Fund Managers and Mutual Funds in the Chilean market increases the probability that firms issue bonds since they improve corporate governance by inducing information disclosure, with institutional participation being mainly through debt and not equity [66]) during the year $t$ or 0 otherwise ($dum\_bond_{it}$); and finally, we controlled the effect of ownership participation of controlling shareholders on the cost of debt by including the percentage of ownership participation of the 12 major shareholders of firm $i$ during year $t$ ($control12_{it}$).

Hence, our baseline econometric model is:

$$ln\_cdebt_{it} = \alpha + \beta_1 ESGD_{it-1} + \beta_2 ln\_assets_{it} + \beta_3 mg\_ebitda_{it} + \beta_4 rotact_{it} + \beta_5 leverage_{it} + \beta_6 ebitdatoint_{it} + \beta_7 pricetobook_{it} + \beta_8 control12_{it} + \beta_9 dum\_bond_{it} + \sum_j \beta_j FIRM_{it} + \sum_k \beta_k YEAR_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}$$

To estimate this baseline model, we use the same measure of the cost of debt financing applied by [16,62,63]. This estimation is based on the annual weighted average cost of debt estimated by the Bloomberg database. However, in the case of our estimation, we work with the natural logarithm of the estimation of the cost of debt financing ($ln\_cdebt_{it}$).

Consistently with Bloomberg’s standards, the annual estimation of the cost of the debt corresponds to “the weighted average cost of debt, calculated using the government bond rates, a debt adjustment factor (AF), and the proportions of short- and long-term debt to total debt”. AF represents the risk-premium or the average yield above government bonds for a given rating class—this being an inverse relationship. AF is only used when a company does not have a fair market curve. When a company does not have a credit rating, an assumed rate of 1.38% (the equivalent rate of a BBB + Standard and Poor’s long-term currency issuer rating) is applied. The exact calculation of the debt adjustment factor is a Bloomberg proprietary calculation. The formal expression for the cost of the debt by the Bloomberg Professional Database is:

$$Cost\ of\ Debt = \left\{ \left( \frac{SD}{TD} \right) \times (CS \times AF) + \left( \frac{LD}{TD} \right) \times (CL \times AF) \right\} \times (1 - TR)$$

where SD: short-term debt; TD: total debt; CS: pre-tax cost of short-term debt; AF: debt adjustment factor; LD: long-term debt; CL: pre-tax cost of long-term debt; and TR: effective tax rate.

Following [67,68], we use Bloomberg’s ESG score ($ESGD_{it}$) as a measure of the extent of a firm’s disclosure on ESG performance. This measure ranges from 0.1 to 100, where 0.1 represents cases where minimal ESGD occurs and 100 represents the highest ESGD for each ESG dimension: environmental (E), social (S), and governance (G). Accordingly, the variable $ESGD_{it}$ is the sum in aggregate terms of the score for firm $i$ during year $t$ for the dimensions E, S, and G. We impute 0 to our dependent variable when there is no ESG disclosure (this treatment is consistent with the fact that ESG disclosure in Chile remains a voluntary action). This ESG score is calculated on the basis of public information by firms through sustainability reports and annual reports, press releases, direct communications, third-party research, and news items [4]. Bloomberg’s ESG score is based on 100 out of 219 raw data points that Bloomberg collects and is weighted to emphasize the most commonly disclosed fields (Bloomberg is a globally recognized institution in the financial and corporate world and widely used among the main international investment firms; therefore, its use as a data source allows the replication of these studies in other markets and countries. It also facilitates the improvement of companies’ data collection and reporting systems for the financial market. Likewise, Bloomberg as a global source of ESG indicators allows different studies to be compared globally since one of the main shortcomings associated with ESG indicators is their lack of comparability between different providers of international information).

Finally, as our study evaluates the possibility of multiple channels through which ESGD affects the cost of financing, we explore what we call the mediating effect of firm heterogeneity on the relation between ESGD and the cost of debt financing. For this purpose, and as indicated in Section 2.3, we study the indirect (possibly second-order) effects as stated in hypotheses H2, H3, and H4. That is, we test the extent to which ESG disclosure interacts with the participation of institutional investors (esgdtl1_dum_bond) (esgtl1_dum_bond = $ESGD_{it-1} * dumbond_{it}$), the ownership participation of controlling shareholders (esgdtl1_control12) (esgdtl1_control_12 = $ESGD_{it-1} * control12_{it}$), and the growth opportunities of firms (esgdtl1_pbook) (esgdtl1_pbook = $ESGD_{it-1} * pricetobook_{it}$) to affect the cost of debt financing. ## 4. Empirical Results

### 4.1. General Results

In the first stage of our analysis, we test whether the extent of ESG disclosure influences the cost of debt financing. Analogously to [36], we test the significance of ESGD as a single factor in the cost of debt. As Table 2, column 1 shows there is a negative and statistically significant relationship between the extent of ESG disclosure of the firms and their cost of debt financing. That is, this first stage of analysis provides evidence that coincides with the theoretical inverse relation between ESGD and the cost of financing—namely, a greater extent of ESGD by a firm reduces the firm’s financial debt costs. This general result is in line with evidence from the Chilean market indicating that some listed firms are issuing green and sustainable bonds in the local and international markets that had led them to obtain better rate conditions through a lower spread over the risk-free rate (for example, the firm CMPC was the first among Chilean companies to issue in 2017 green bonds, and more recently, bonds linked to sustainability objectives in both local and international markets, obtaining a lower cost of debt financing. Source: Sustainable Financial Report 2021. https://ir.cmpc.com/English/fixed-income/green-finance-impact-reports/default.aspx, accessed on 8 November 2022).

In Table 2, we additionally study the impact of ESGD controlling by the presence of other factors that the literature relates to debt costs. In Table 2, columns 2 through 5 depict the analysis that incorporates the influence of these other factors on the cost of debt financing: growth opportunities, ownership participation of controlling shareholders, and institutional participation. The evidence indicates that by controlling for the latter variables, ESGD sustains an inverse and statistically significant association with the cost of debt financing. This result cultivates the relevance and contribution of the ESG disclosure of firms as a factor that enhances their competitiveness in terms of their financial costs, and consequently, facilitates a greater value for companies.

Deepening the previous analysis, we explore whether the indirect second-order effects associated with the heterogeneity of the firms affect the ESGD-cost of financing relationship. In Table 2, columns 6 through 9 provide the main results. As can be seen, results continue to show evidence that suggests a dominant direct effect that produces a negative relationship between the extent of ESGD and the cost of debt financing. However, the presence of a statistically positive and significant relationship between growth opportunities and the extent of the ESG disclosure interaction term suggests the presence of a positive indirect second-order effect of ESGD through the growth opportunities of the firms. This evidence implies that when a firm improves the possibility of future growth (greater growth opportunities), and thus the firm’s exposure to risk increases, then the cost of debt financing rises in the margin. The latter occurs because in this case (ex-post higher risk profile) ESG disclosure would reveal previously unknown sources of risk. We further conjecture that this result suggests that ESGD contributes to a better assessment of the creditworthiness of firms and their future risk profile related to ESG dimensions.

Finally, we also studied the conjoint effects of ownership participation of controlling shareholders with ESG disclosure and institutional participation with ESGD. Our results indicated that both dimensions are not significant since with p-values greater than 12.56% and 26.28% respectively, these variables have a value other than zero (columns 7 and 8 in Table 2).

Therefore, our results confirm that the relationship between the cost of debt financing of firms and the extent of their ESG disclosure has at least two components. The first is a direct channel, which indicates the presence of a negative relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing. The second is an indirect channel that indicates a positive relation that mitigates the effect of ESGD on the cost of debt financing through the interaction between growth opportunities and the extent of ESG disclosure. Hence, these results highlight the relevance of considering the heterogeneity of firms in the analysis of the impact of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt financing, and that idiosyncratic aspects of a firm’s risk profile might affect its cost of debt financing when it decides to disclose its ESG information.

### 4.2. Robustness Analysis

Continuing our empirical strategy, we evaluate the consistency of the results presented in Section 4.1 through a robustness analysis. The goal of this section is to corroborate that our findings are robust to any peculiarity in the data during the period 2015–2020 not properly controlled by our main control variables. For this, we incorporate two extra control dummy variables. The first is *dum_djsi*, which takes the value of 1 if firm $i$ in year $t$ belonged to the Dow Jones Sustainability Chile Index during the period 2015–2020 and 0 otherwise. Similarly, the second variable is *dum_adr*, which equals 1 if firm $i$ in year $t$ is listed on the NYSE (New York Stock Exchange) as an ADR (American Depositary Receipts) during the period 2015–2020 and 0 otherwise. By adding these two control variables, the model captures the possible effects of common international shocks on Chilean listed firms.

In Table 3, columns 1 through 5 show analogous results to those presented in Table 2, but considering the possibility of international equity shocks to the Chilean firms’ cost of debt financing. As previously, there is still evidence of a negative relationship between the extent of ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing; moreover, this relation is robust to these additional controls. In addition, the existing evidence of indirect second-order effects through growth opportunities is also robust to these additional controls.

## 5. Discussion

Our paper studies the effect of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt financing among listed firms in the context of emerging markets. We find the presence of a non-homogeneous and negative relationship between the extent of a firm ESG disclosure and its cost of financing via debt. One possible explanation of this relates to the risk analysis involved in a credit operation. When creditors assess the creditworthiness of a debtor, they incorporate all of the relevant and available information into the decision-making process. This way, when non-financial—namely ESG—information becomes more available, the score of a firm regarding ESG disclosure complements its financial information and lets creditors improve their credit risk assessment. Consequently, our study provides evidence that supports the claim that ESG disclosure helps firms to reduce their financial cost during credit operations conditional on their risk profile. Thus, our findings support the thesis that ESG disclosure influences the cost of debt financing by reducing information asymmetries in a credit operation.

Moreover, we find evidence that suggests that firm heterogeneity affects the effect of ESGD on the cost of financing debt through the future risk perception of firms embedded in the growth opportunities of a firm. As the corporate finance literature documents, increasing the growth opportunities also increases the future risk that the companies will face. Therefore, the effect of the ESG disclosure on the cost of debt financing should increase with larger growth opportunities, which is what is reported in the coefficient of the ESGD growth opportunities interaction term in our econometric models. Thus, our evidence suggests that ESG scores help creditors to better differentiate the impact of the future risk prospect of firms revealed through environmental, social, and governance aspects and challenges of their operations.

In relation to previous studies analyzing the association between the cost of debt and ESG disclosure, our evidence confirms the results achieved by [1,43,44,74], revealing that ESG disclosure positively affects the cost of debt by lowering it. In line with the above first-order effects suggested by the literature, but in an emerging market context, our findings contribute with empirical evidence on Latin America, showing the existence of a first-order and negative relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing among listed firms. Crucially, in this paper, we also find evidence suggesting that in the margin, ESGD increases the cost of debt financing when the prospective risk of firms also rises. In this regard, these findings provide useful information to evaluate support programs for high-growth potential firms in developing economies as they provide the possibility of diversifying economic risk for emerging economies, thereby providing a positive externality for those economies.

Analogously, our evidence is consistent with literature that documents a negative first-order relationship between financial disclosure and the cost of debt financing [38–41], CSR disclosure [42,45,75], voluntary disclosure [9,20–22,35,76], environmental disclosure [8,77], carbon emissions disclosure, [78], social disclosure [79], intellectual capital disclosure [46], and finally, integrated reporting [40,47].

Despite the contribution of this study to the current literature, particularly by enhancing the study of ESG disclosure and its implications in emerging economies, this study has some limitations to consider. First, the data set refers only to listed firms. Thus, the question regarding how small businesses benefit (or not) from reducing informational asymmetries in terms of the cost of financing remains open—especially in the context of emerging economies where concentration drives small businesses to have the greater potential to generate disruptive innovations. Therefore, deepening the study of the relationship between ESGD and the cost of financing is a promising research direction in small and not listed firms.

Second, for the case of listed firms, although we find evidence of firm-heterogeneity in the (total) relationship between ESGD and the cost of financing, the latter heterogeneity does not change the sign of the relation in listed firms: this is to say, a greater extent of disclosure reduces the cost of debt financing. However, for the case of riskier, smaller, and innovative businesses, it is plausible to ask whether the heterogeneous elements of the relationship captured in growth opportunities could change the direction of the total ESGD-cost of financing relation. That is, when the heterogeneity of the growth opportunities in high, potential, and riskier smaller businesses is revealed through ESGD, ESG disclosure could consequently increase (in total) the cost of debt financing. The aggregate implication of the latter effect is that the growth potential of emerging economies could be circumscribed to the growth opportunities of mature firms, which are usually listed in developing economies.

Finally, the latter observation regarding the aggregate implications of ESGD in non-listed smaller firms opens another alternative for future research: what would be the aggregate effect on economic growth that ESGD produces in emerging economies that have less access to credit than firms in developed economies?

## 6. Conclusions

The main evidence of our article indicates the existence of a negative association between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing among listed firms in an emerging market context. Accordingly, it is worth asking whether these results could be directly extrapolated to other emerging economies that exhibit different conditions of financial development in their stock and credit markets, and different levels of advancement in the implementation of ESG norms and disclosure. Likewise, as we previously mentioned, our results support the thesis that ESG disclosure enhances risk analysis and helps firms in the task of reducing information asymmetries. A critical factor of success for this process relates to the condition that market participants are able to correctly apply this non-financial disclosure information of firms into their risk analysis decision-making. In other words, it is required that the financial market is used to and knows the ESG nomenclature and its interpretation.

Our results have several implications from the point of view of regulators, financial markets, and firms. First, our findings suggest for market regulators that both ESG disclosure and firm heterogeneity should be taken into consideration because of the potential trade-off between transparency and growth, given the risk profile of the regulator and the firms. Second, companies themselves should include the interaction of ESG disclosure and their growth opportunities in their financial management plans. The latter considerations might drive not only the cost of debt financing but the overall financing strategy from a long-term perspective. For firms, the relevance lies in improving their communication channels with ESG information users and promoting open channels of communication among them and their stakeholders. Third, for the financial actors, our findings suggest a broader perspective to assess the credit risk of firms, paying particular attention to the combined effect of ESG indicators as risk factors coupled with the heterogeneity elements that characterize debt issuers, sectors, and markets. Finally, in highly concentrated economies like developing countries, these results suggest that regulators should consider how to address the trade-off between the economic diversification of risk by promoting the growth of new riskier firms, and the reduction in the financing cost associated with fewer risky firms but a higher concentration in the markets.

Our study offers several extensions of research opportunities. First, spreading the analysis towards the mechanisms through which ESG disclosure affects the economic and financial performance of firms by considering the firms’ access to capital markets, internationalization, and type of shareholders, among other elements of differentiation. Second, widening the analysis to also consider non-listed firms that might harness greater growth opportunities. Third, evaluating the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing in emerging countries with less financial development. Finally, studying the aggregate growth impact of ESG disclosure in the context of emerging economies, where access to credit limits growth and total factor productivity improvements.
