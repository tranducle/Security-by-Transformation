### ABSTRACT
This paper analyzes whether there is any relationship between sustainability performance and corporate cost of debt. Using a sample of European listed companies, we find that high-ESG firms pay a lower cost on their debt, with the environmental dimension explaining most of this result and the governance dimension, instead, to have no effect in this respect. We also show that, consistent with the risk-reducing effect of superior ESG performance, a positive relation exists between ESG ratings and credit ratings. Our results are relevant to different groups of stakeholders, including policymakers, investors and firms themselves, suggesting them that they can benefit from being more socially responsible in terms of lower costs of financing.

## 1. Introduction

The importance of considering environmental and social issues into corporate strategies, as well as implementing sound governance mechanisms to foster communication with both minor shareholders and external stakeholders, is nowadays largely acknowledged among practitioners and researchers (Tripathi and Bhandari, 2014). So, Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) practices are no longer regarded as a burden by firms (following Friedman’s (1970) doctrine that the sole responsibility of business is to increase profits) but, instead, as a means to achieve a competitive advantage (Hao and He, 2022).

A primary reason for this is given by the regulatory environment. In fact, on one hand, policymakers across the globe are starting to explicitly forbid business practices endangering the safeguard of the natural environment (a prominent example is the EU’s ban of the sale of diesel cars from 2035). On the other hand, they are setting minimum environmental and social targets (in terms of CO2 emissions, pollution, workforce safety, etc.) that firms need to reach, in order to avoid incurring in sanctions and penalties (see Fig. 1).

However, regulation alone cannot explain the widespread adoption of ESG-driven behaviours by companies, since, in most of cases, these latter go well beyond such minimum regulatory thresholds (Attig et al., 2013). Why do private companies embrace sustainability despite it should be (ideally) demanded to the public sector?

This question has been addressed by extensive empirical literature, whose results seem to go in an opposite direction with respect to the Friedman’s doctrine, agreeing on the value-enhancing effect of superior ESG performance. A first argument is that firms engage in socially responsible investments (SRIs) to obtain a competitive advantage (Branco and Rodrigues, 2006), achievable by capturing the demand by consumers for “ethical” goods, i.e., goods compliant with green and social standards: as a consequence, customer awareness becomes a tool to achieve higher profitability and firm value creation through SRIs (Servaes and Tamayo, 2013; Aydogmuş et al., 2022). In fact, Uyar et al. (2022) point out that greater transparency, a stronger reputation and legitimacy, enhanced brand equity and increased loyalty from employees and customers are key channels linking ESG and financial performances. In this respect, the spread of ESG rating providers (e.g., Gregory, 2024) plays a crucial role.

As a result, those corporations engaging more in ESG activities are perceived as less risky (Meles et al., 2023; Palmieri et al., 2023). We then ask whether such lower risk stemming from more sustainability-oriented behaviours is acknowledged by external investors and ultimately translates into a lower cost of funding, thereby potentially representing a further incentive for firms to pursue ESG-oriented strategies. Therefore, in this study we analyze whether more ESG firms benefit from a lower cost of debt.

Actually, the literature seems to agree on the risk-reducing effect of being socially responsible. For example, Meles et al. (2023) observe that corporate environmental innovation reduces corporate default risk due to increased profitability and firm value. However, concluding that such reduced riskiness turns into a lower cost of debt is anything but obvious. In fact, despite some studies document a positive association between ESG and credit ratings (Attig et al., 2013; Palmieri et al., 2023), other issues (such as the exposure to ESG controversies or the market’s inability to understand the value-enhancing effect of ESG investments) still need to be considered. Not surprisingly, when it comes to the link between ESG performance and the cost of debt, existing evidence becomes mixed.

Some studies argue that the overall ESG performance contributes to reduce the corporate cost of debt (Li et al., 2024), while others find different results when disentangling the single ESG dimensions. For example, Agnese and Giacomini (2023) observe that the environmental dimension has no role in such respect, while Erragragui (2018), instead, find that the governance dimension is the nonsignificant one. The latter result is consistent with Fera et al. (2025) that only the environmental and social performances contribute to reduce the cost of debt (however, they uniquely look at the primary corporate bond market). A different result is provided by Goss and Roberts (2011), pointing out that banks “punish” socially irresponsible firms (by raising loan rates) but they do not “reward” socially responsible ones. In contrast, other studies do not find any significant evidence that sustainability performance contributes to reduce the cost of corporate debt (Hoepner et al., 2016; Gigante and Manglaviti, 2022; Veltri et al., 2023).

Against such contrasting evidence, having a clear understanding of whether firms can achieve lower cost of debt financing through enhancing their engagement in ESG practices is crucial to relevant stakeholders, such as policymakers, investors, banks and firms themselves. Thus, using a European sample of publicly listed firms, we investigate whether companies displaying a higher ESG performance (measured by ESG ratings by Refinitiv) pay lower interest rates on their debt. Our evidence confirms this prediction, as we observe a positive association between ESG scores and cost of debt. We also observe that the environmental performance contributes the most to explain such result, while we do not find any statistical significance for the governance dimension. Finally, we find that ESG performance is also positively related with credit ratings, suggesting that more ESG-committed firms are effectively perceived as less risky by banks and bondholders, thereby leading to lower cost of debt funding.

Our study provides a twofold contribution to existing literature. First, we provide novel evidence to the literature linking corporate ESG and how these affect the cost of capital (Agnese and Giacomini, 2023; Li et al., 2024, Fera et al., 2025), providing additional evidence about the beneficial effect of superior ESG performance in this respect. Second, by showing that higher ESG ratings lead to better credit ratings, we add to those studies exploring the risk implications of ESG investments (Meles et al., 2023; Palmieri et al., 2023).

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 explains the data and methodology, Section 3 presents the empirical findings, while Section 4 concludes.

## 2. Data and methodology

This study investigates the impact of ESG performance on the cost of debt on a sample composed of 671 listed European companies. Following previous studies (e.g., Wang and Sun, 2022), we collect data on ESG scores and governance variables from Refinitiv Eikon and we run generalized least squares (GLS) regression analysis.

We choose a European sample of firms for two main reasons. First, to have a large enough sample to not incur in generalization issues with our findings. Second, to ensure that all sample firms are subject to the same regulatory framework. In this latter regard, it is worth mentioning that, despite our sample encompasses firms from 13 member states, the regulation is harmonized when it comes to corporate sustainability issues, since it is set at the central level and then transposed in single states, even though some time lag may occur in the transposition in the national regulatory framework. Prominent examples are the Directive 95/2014/EU, regulating the duties of private companies with regard to nonfinancial disclosure, or the more recent European Regulation 2019/2088 (more commonly referred to as “Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation”), regulating the definition of “sustainable investment” in the nonfinancial disclosure of investment funds.

As a first step, we retrieve information on 950 European listed firms with an ESG score issued by Refinitiv. Of these, we exclude firms with missing relevant (financial and nonfinancial) data, gathering a final sample of 671 listed firms, covering a time window from 2013 to 2022, for a total of 6,710 firm-year observations. Table A1 in the appendix reports the sample distribution by country (Panel A) and by industry (Panel B).

To properly investigate our research question, we run a GLS analysis. To test our research question, we run a model (1), where the dependent variable is the cost of debt, measured by the ratio of the current fiscal year’s interest expenses to the average of the initial and final balances of both long-term and short-term financial debts (Callahan et al., 2012; Pittman and Fortin, 2004). We then estimate the following fixed-effects regression model:

$$Cod_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta_i ESG\_score_{i,t} + \sum_{u=3}^k \beta_u controls(governance, firm, country) + \tau + \gamma + \epsilon_{i,t} \quad (1)$$

where our main coefficient of interest is $\beta_i$, associated with the Refinitiv ESG score (and its respective sub-pillars). The score is standardized, ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate better overall performance in the environmental, social and governance dimension, whereas the weights of the three dimensions are defined according to the respective industry (the respective country, in the case of the “G” pillar). We also include a battery of governance, financial and country-level control variables. Five corporate governance factors that have been well documented to affect sustainability performance are included, namely: percentage of women on the board of directors, CEO duality, board independence, board size and the presence of a CSR Committee (Ting-bani et al., 2020; Elsayih et al., 2021).

Further, in line with prior studies (Nadem et al., 2020), seven firm-level variables that affect the environmental practice of organizations are included as control variables, notably: return on equity, leverage, firm sales (total book assets), total sales, Tobin Q and liquidity. Finally, in line with previous studies (e.g., Wang et al., 2023), our analysis includes three country-level variables as well: the rule of law index provided by the World Bank (Kuzey et al., 2023); the annual percentage growth rate of GDP at market prices based on constant local currency (Kuai and Wang, 2024); the log of real GDP per capita. Then, GDP per capita is based on purchasing power parity (PPP). All firm-level control variables are winsorized at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles to mitigate the effect of outliers.

A more detailed description of all the variables used in our analysis (along with related sources) is reported in Table 1, while Table 2 reports the summary statistics for the same variables. From Table 2, we can notice a consistent variability of the ESG score in our sample (the standard deviation is 20.321. This can be explained in light of the Refinitiv database’s depth, which makes this source ideal to deal with ESG measurement on a large sample of firms.

Finally, Table 3 shows the Pearson correlation matrix. As shown, the magnitude of the variable’s coefficients seems to exclude the occurrence of multicollinearity issues. A confirmation of this is also given by the maximum and average VIF (variance inflation factor) reported in Table 4, equal to 5.23 and 2.17, respectively. These values are well below the critical threshold (rule of thumb) of 10 suggested by previous studies (Gujarati, 1995; Kennedy, 1998). Overall, we can conclude that our analysis is free from any multicollinearity issue.

## 3. Empirical results

We then estimate our Eq. (1) to investigate the relation between corporate ESG performance and the cost of debt financing. Results are reported in Table 5. To mitigate the effect of heteroskedasticity in the empirical results, the analysis uses robust standard errors. We start from a baseline specification where we uniquely control for ESG performance and (year and firm) fixed effects (column 1). Then, we add financial controls, governance controls, and both (columns 2, 3, and full specification in column 4).

Nonetheless, our coefficient of interest is always positive and statistically significant (at the 1% confidence level in the full specification of column 4), suggesting that it is robust to a change in control variables. This result clearly corroborates our prediction that a higher ESG performance contributes to reducing the cost of corporate debt.

Other results from the estimations of Table 5 reveal that board gender diversity (percentage of women on the board of directors) also contributes to lower the cost of debt financing, as the coefficient to WoB is negative and statistically significant, consistent with the argument that a gendered board encourages the implementation of sustainable initiatives that are relevant factors to credit access. In addition, we state that a positive relationship between board independence and credit access exists. This means that firms with a higher percentage of non-executive independent directors are more likely to commit to better quality ESG performance because independent directors are much more concerned with stakeholder perceptions and needs, which, in turn, makes banks and other investors willing to charge lower interest rates. Among the financial controls, we observe a statistically significant coefficient (at the 1% level) only in correspondence of Leverage.

Our results are consistent with the argument that the inclusion of non-financial criteria in the decision-making loan process appears relevant because of the need to include economic, environmental, and social concerns within financial decisions (Artis, 2017; Serino and Campanella, 2024). Sustainability activities have become a recent central issue in banks’ credit lending practices (Birindelli et al., 2015) and the banks are more likely to incorporate environmental and social indicators in the decision-making process. In fact, some studies found how the right combination of financial and non-financial factors leads to more accuracy in the prediction of a default event positively influencing the credit risk portfolio of banks (Attig et al., 2013; Grunert et al., 2005).

In addition, our results show evidence of a significant relationship between some corporate and governance variables with the firm’s cost of debt, which is consistent with Campanella et al. (2021). The findings show that female directors are valuable resources in an organization since they possess the potential to promote ESG practices and disclosure (Khan et al., 2019). This means that boards with more female directors are more likely to influence firms’ ESG scores (Bhatia and Marwaha, 2022) so that they consider social and environmental engagement and are involved in sustainability activities.

We also find that the percentage of non-executive directors has a beneficial effect on the cost of debt. Consistent with (and in support of) the positive relationship between CEO duality and sustainable performance (Birindelli et al., 2015), our results show that the coexistence of the functions of the CEO and the chairman board contributes to lower interest rates on debt.

### 3.1. Additional evidence

In this section, we run two additional analyses aimed at verifying, on the one hand, which ESG dimension contributes the most to the cost of financing reduction and, on the other hand, whether the previous result is driven by the risk-reduction effect that previous empirical evidence attributes to ESG engagement (Attig et al., 2013; Meles et al., 2023; Palmieri et al., 2023).

First, we rerun Eq. (1) by decomposing, on the left-hand side (LHS), the ESG score in its three components (pillars), and we look at them separately. In addition to offer a deeper understanding of which one is more relevant in this respect (with existing literature providing mixed results; see, for example, Erragragui, 2018, Agnese and Giacomini, 2023 and Fera et al., 2025), this analysis also allows us to overcome a major criticism associated to each ESG rating, i.e., the rater’s discretion in weighting each single dimension across industries/countries. Results are reported in Table 6. We can clearly see that only the “E” and “S” pillars show statistically significant coefficients, while the “G” is not significant. This result is consistent with Erragragui (2018) and Fera et al. (2025), finding that the governance dimension does not contribute to lowering the corporate cost of debt. Moreover, in terms of magnitude, the “E” pillar shows both a greater statistical significance (at the 1% confidence level, while the “S” is significant only at 10 %) and a greater coefficient, suggesting that banks and bondholders care more about corporate environmental performance.

Second, are the previous results driven by a lower riskiness associated with a higher ESG engagement? While a positive answer may seem obvious, we still need to consider that the existent literature is not concordant in this regard. Moreover, the lower cost of debt may be also explained by another positive implication generally associated with ESG performance, i.e., higher corporate transparency (e.g., Nuskiya et al., 2021; Santamaria et al., 2021). It may be, for example, that, regardless of corporate risk exposure following an increase in ESG spending, banks agree to charge lower loan rates since ESG firms provide more nonfinancial disclosure and, thereby, they are more transparent: all other things being equal, lower asymmetric information should reduce the cost of debt.

We then test the “risk-reduction” argument by running Eq. (1) after replacing, on the LHS, the cost of debt with firm’s credit rating (by Fitch’s), measuring external perceptions about firm creditworthiness. As we can see from the results reported in Table 7, the coefficient of interest (associated with Esg_score) turns positive and statistically significant across all the specifications, going in support of the risk-reducing effect of ESG engagement. In other words, high ESG firms are perceived as less risky and more credit worthy, thereby increasing their credit rating and, ultimately, leading to lower costs of debt capital.

## 4. Conclusions

The primary aim of this paper is to gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of corporate debt in the European context.

Using a sample of 6,710 firm-year observations, our findings suggest that the level of ESG performance influences the overall cost of debt incurred by firms. The loan decision process is a selection process based on return considerations, financial conditions, and corporate risk assessment (Serino and Campanella, 2024). So, our results are consistent with the view of ESG factors as a tool to convey relevant information about the risk profile of borrowers to banks and other investors. Consistent with this, we also find that the environmental dimension of ESG provides the biggest contribution in this respect, and that higher ESG ratings are also associated with higher credit ratings.

If high ESG scores result in lower financing costs for a firm, it would necessitate a reevaluation of existing models. CEOs should then align their decision-making with a redefined risk-return framework that fully integrates ESG considerations. Additionally, regulators must act to enhance transparency and establish standardized ESG reporting practices, while policymakers should leverage these metrics as policy instruments to incentivize sustainable development.

So, our results have clear implications for multiple stakeholders, suggesting that sustainable initiatives play a key role in corporate financing choices. For banks, incorporating ESG performance into the credit evaluation process provides a more holistic view of credit risk, aligning lending practices with sustainable development objectives. This approach not only improves the precision of credit risk evaluations but also bolsters connections with socially responsible investors and stakeholders (Zampella et al., 2024). For businesses, especially those seeking financing, the findings emphasize the strategic value of prioritizing sustainability efforts and enhancing ESG performance to secure more favorable lending conditions. Policymakers can use these insights to create regulatory frameworks and incentives that encourage the integration of ESG criteria within financial systems, supporting a more sustainable economy (He and Liu, 2023). By aligning lending decisions with sustainable development goals (SDGs), banks can support projects and companies that contribute to responsible and environmentally conscious economic growth while simultaneously reducing negative impacts on the planet and society. Indeed, in accordance with prior studies (Attig et al., 2013) the introduction of sustainable criteria improves the credit evaluation for European firms and, consequently, their credit access, confirming that ESG engagement has a significant impact on firms’ financial performance reducing the default risk.

Future studies should extend this research to include the relations among more corporate governance characteristics, such as the audit committee effectiveness, the existence of foreign ownership, and ESG disclosure. So, they can replicate this study with additional measures of sustainability and governance. Another potentially exciting avenue for future research could be to disentangle the risk reducing effect typically associated with high ESG performance from the transparency effect, to observe what makes the bigger contribution to the cost of debt reduction and in which circumstances.
