### Abstract
We investigate whether environmental, social and governance (ESG) disclosure is related to default risk. Using a sample of US nonfinancial institutions from 2006 to 2017, we find that ESG disclosure is positively related to Merton's distance to default and is negatively related to the credit default swap spread, which suggests that firms with a higher ESG disclosure have lower default risk. Our analysis further indicates that the inverse effect of ESG disclosure on default risk is through increased profitability and reduced performance variability and cost of debt. We also document that the negative impact of ESG disclosure on default risk is existent only for mature and older firms. These results are important for all stakeholders of firms, including shareholders and bondholders to consider firm's ESG disclosure in conjunction with life cycle stage before making their investment decisions.

**KEYWORDS**
corporate life cycle, cost of debt, default risk, ESG disclosure, performance variability

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

This study has examined whether disclosures of environmental, social and governance (ESG) affect default risk in firms in the US context. Nonfinancial performance evaluated by ESG disclosure continues to attract the attention of practitioners, investors, customers, employees and suppliers, as well as scholars. All major stakeholders source information from ESG disclosure: about product safety (Deegan & Rankin, 1996), employee rights and welfare at workplace (Waddock, 2008) and firms' relationship with society.[^1] Growing empirical evidence regarding the influence of ESG disclosure on firm performance (e.g., Endrikat et al., 2014; Horvathova, 2010) suggests a positive impact on cash flows and a reduction in cost of capital (Plumlee et al., 2015). Extant evidence also suggests that ESG disclosure has an impact on firms' financial risk management (e.g., Plumlee et al., 2015; Limkriangkrai et al., 2017). However, the role of ESG disclosure in influencing default risk has received limited attention in the literature (e.g., Boubaker et al., 2020). Default risk is one of the important yardsticks of firm financial health (Rego et al., 2009).

Corporate default, generally defined as a firm that is failing to make contractual payments to debt holders (Valta, 2016), can lead to devastating results for stakeholders (e.g., investors, creditors, customers, employees and regulators).[^2] The likelihood of default depends on whether the firm's future cash flows are sufficient to cover both its debt service costs (interest payments) and the principal amount. In general, the likelihood of default increases when a firm experiences a downward shift of its average future cash flows or when the future cash flows become more volatile. Given the significance of default risk for investors and other stakeholders, it is imperative to examine whether ESG disclosure can help firms to mitigate default risk.

We use the resource-based theory (RBT) to conjecture the association between ESG disclosure and default risk. RBT (Barney, 1991) asserts that firms are capable of managing internal dynamic factors that affect their success. Firms with higher dynamic capabilities may combine ESG strength with other corporate strategies to successfully capitalize ESG benefits in their financial risk management (Teece et al., 1997). For instance, ESG disclosure is associated with brand value and with customer satisfaction that leads to higher sales and thus to profitability (Brown & Dacin, 1997; Luo & Bhattacharya, 2006). Such customer loyalty helps to ensure a stream of consistent cash flows, reducing a firm's financial distress. Moreover, in case of negative events, firms continue to enjoy stable future cash flows due to moral capital among customers. Further, disclosure of ESG information contributes to a reduction in information asymmetry (Cho et al., 2013), leading towards investors trust and loyalty. The symmetric flow of information increases the availability of debt capital and reduces the cost of funds (Cheng et al., 2014; El Ghoul et al., 2011; Waddock & Graves, 1997). We therefore argue that ESG disclosure reduces firms' default risk by increasing their level of profitability and by decreasing their performance variability and cost of debt capital.[^3]

We extend this baseline idea to examine if the ESG–default linkage is moderated by firm life cycle stages. Existing literature suggests distinct capabilities and resources, cash flow volatilities and policy decisions in firms at different life cycle stages (e.g., Faff et al., 2016; Miller & Friesen, 1984; Porter, 2008). For instance, firms in the early stages of their life cycle are financially constrained, lack liquid resources, may experience uncertainties about future cash flows and may have difficulty in raising additional capital, all of which raises the likelihood of default (Hasan et al., 2015). Younger firms, due to their implicit concerns about achieving financial objectives, are less likely to focus on ESG disclosure than on showing legitimacy with key stakeholders (Ramaswamy et al., 2007). Firms in their mature phase have greater competitive advantage, sufficient resources and more certainty of future cash flows (Hasan & Habib, 2017), leading to lower chances of default. In addition, mature firms are more likely to embrace ESG disclosure due to their reputational concerns and greater interaction with key stakeholders, including regulatory authorities (Hasan & Habib, 2017). Given this evidence, we expect to find that the inverse relation between ESG disclosure and default risk should be relatively stronger (weaker or insignificant) for firms at mature stage (younger and other stages) of the life cycle.

Using a panel of nonfinancial firms from 2006 to 2017, we find that ESG disclosure is negatively related to default risk, as proxied by Merton's distance to default and credit default swap spread in baseline estimations. This finding is robust to alternative variable specifications and to alternative estimation methods, including lagged independent variables, instrumental variable approach and propensity score matching (PSM). We further explore mechanisms through which ESG disclosure reduces default risk. Using the profitability mechanism, we find that the inverse effect of ESG disclosure on default risk is through increased profitability (measured through return on assets and return on equity) and reduced performance variability (measured through quantile-based risk metrics) (see, e.g., Konstantinidi & Pope, 2016). In addition, we examine whether ESG disclosure reduces default risk via cost of debt (measured as a ratio of interest expense divided by average interest-bearing debt). We find specific evidence that firms with a lower cost of debt have a lower default risk and that firms with a higher ESG disclosure have a lower cost of debt. Finally, we investigate whether the relationship between ESG disclosure and default risk relationship is magnified in different stages of a firm's life cycle following the Dickinson (2011) model. We find that the negative impact of ESG disclosure on default risk exists mainly in mature and older firms.

Our study makes two important contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the stream of research that explores the determinants of firms' default risk. This recent strand of literature has identified important determinants of default risk: for example, stock market liquidity (Brogaard et al., 2017; Nadarajah et al., 2020), incentive structure (Bennett et al., 2015), innovation performance (Hsu et al., 2015), debt maturity choice (Goyal & Wang, 2013), stock market return and volatility (Giesecke et al., 2011) and corporate governance characteristics including CEO compensation, board independence, gender diversity and ownership structure (see, e.g., Acrey et al., 2011; Ali, 2017; Ali et al., 2018, 2021; Chiang et al., 2015; Chiu & Wagner, 2012; Kabir et al., 2020; Miglani et al., 2015; Nadaraja et al., 2020; Schultz et al., 2017; Switzer & Wang, 2013; Vallascas & Hagendorff, 2013). We extend this literature by providing robust evidence regarding the role of aggregate ESG disclosure as a critical determinant of default risk for US nonfinancial firms. Prior studies investigate the effect of ESG on various firm-level outcomes including firm performance, cost of capital and financial distress (e.g., Boubaker et al., 2020; Endrikat et al., 2014; Horváthová, 2010; Plumlee et al., 2015). However, our study goes beyond cause-and-effect relationship between ESG and default risk and differs from existing studies in at least three ways. First, our data are sourced from Bloomberg, which uses the Global Responsible Initiative (GRI) and the United Nations (UN) guidelines in collating ESG disclosure information. Second, our default risk measures are based on market data, rather than on accounting information that lacks currency. Third, we examine various mechanisms through which ESG disclosure affects default risk, and document that higher profitability, lower performance variability and lower cost of debt are the channels through which ESG disclosure reduces default risk.

Second, we contribute to the firm life cycle literature. Previous literature on the firm life cycle has described its role in influencing dividend policy (DeAngelo et al., 2006; Grullon et al., 2002), equity offerings (DeAngelo et al., 2010), merger and acquisitions (Owen & Yawson, 2010), cash flow patterns (Dickinson, 2011), firm financial policies (Faff et al., 2016) and financial resources and corporate social responsibility (Hasan & Habib, 2017). We show that the corporate life cycle moderates the effect of ESG disclosure on default risk. Specifically, we find that firms in their mature stage have a significantly higher level of ESG disclosure and thus have lower default risk.

Overall, our study complements the current discussion regarding the importance of ESG disclosure for firms and for investors and stakeholders. This study offers new insights for policy makers into the business case for ESG disclosure (i.e., doing well by doing good). More specifically, the role of ESG disclosure in reducing default risk strengthens its importance for firms and regulators. Our findings also provide insights to shareholders and debtholders for considering a firm's disclosure about sustainability initiative before making investment decisions. Given our findings, policy makers aiming at sustainability should encourage firms to disclose their sustainability initiatives.

The rest of the paper is organized in seven sections. Section 2 reviews the literature then develops the hypotheses. The sample selection and summary statistics are discussed in Section 3. Sections 4 and 5 present the main empirical results. Section 6 shows additional analysis. Section 7 concludes the study.

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW AND HYPOTHESES

Default is an abrasive event in the life of a corporation. It causes disruptions in the productivity of firms through supply chain; it deters employee and customer retention; it increases legal and administrative costs (Brogaard et al., 2017). The risk of default increases if firm cash flows are shaky, and/or there is no access to market. This type of peril becomes a yardstick to evaluate a firm's financial health (Rego et al., 2009). The literature concurs that debtholders carry special interest in firms while shareholders are residual owners (Anderson & Mansi, 2009). This implies that debtholder will be mainly impacted in case of a firm's likelihood of financial distress. Given the importance of default risk in a firm's financial journey, it has received wider attention in the literature.[^4]

Default risk is considered to be an important factor in firm evaluation. Investors and debtholders take into account such risk in their investment screening process (Campbell et al., 2008; Dichev, 1998). As capital market is the largest source of external funding and to ensure availability of sufficient funds in future at relevant cost, managers endeavour to keep default risk lower (Anderson & Mansi, 2009). In turn, lower default risk may foster market confidence, resulting in a lower cost of capital ensuring smooth operations.

Prior research has found an array of factors associated with default risk, including a firm's operational and production efficiency (Becchetti & Sierra, 2003; Rendleman, 1978), cash flow uncertainty (Chava & Purnanandam, 2010), information asymmetry (Bhojraj & Sengupta, 2003), brand loyalty (Rego et al., 2009), customer satisfaction (Anderson & Mansi, 2009) and social factors (Allen et al., 2004). All those factors are associated directly or indirectly with future cash flows and firm performance. For instance, customer satisfaction and brand loyalty demonstrate the stability of future incoming cash flows. Such a manifestation is considered among major determinants of default risk (Acharya et al., 2006; Asvanunt et al., 2007; Merton, 1974). Moreover, business-related factors (e.g., business cycle and economic) and social factors both play an important role in default risk (Allen et al., 2004). In summary, default risk reflects market confidence and a firm's internal strength to minimize the risk associated with a number of factors, including those that are social, economic and business-related (e.g., life cycle).

ESG signals firm-specific information regarding environmental, social and governance initiatives towards society (Brown & Dacin, 1997). Those initiatives provide information about the firm revenue prospectus (Lev et al., 2010), including employee welfare and labour relations (Edmans, 2011; Waddock & Graves, 1997) and environmental and social compliance initiatives, based on firm operations (Brown et al., 2006). ESG disclosure also represents a firm's strategic investment towards its integrity (McWilliams et al., 2006). These societal initiatives improve the welfare of society (Davis & Blomstrom, 1975, p. 6). All those important activities help in creating a firm's integrity and its better linking with stakeholders and society at large. Therefore, ESG disclosure enhances a firm's ability to create reputation and to enjoy the benefits of such internal strength in its financial risk management. RBT embraces this argument: that a firm's internal strength, along with its corporate strategies, helps to capitalize the benefits for firms in their financial risk management.

However, investment on societal initiatives is considered to be an expense for shareholders. For instance, prior studies suggest that the main objective of a firm is to maximize its shareholders' wealth while focusing on profit making (e.g., Berle, 1930; Friedman, 1970), rather than spending their wealth on stakeholder orientation. To that end, some traditional studies offer support for shareholder-only orientation, while regarding societal and environmental consideration to be undesirable. However, earlier literature provides strong support for stakeholder orientation, contemplating that firms exist not only for profit making but also to offer social services (Dodd, 1932). More recent studies have shown a positive impact of ESG disclosure on firm outcomes, including on performance (Endrikat et al., 2014), on financial returns and risk-taking (Limkriangkrai et al., 2017; Sassen et al., 2016) and on cost of debt (Plumlee et al., 2015). This study, which focuses on the impact of ESG disclosure on default risk, is consistent with this area of the literature.

ESG disclosure may help firms in reducing default risk in several ways. First, it is directly linked with brand value and with the customer satisfaction that leads to higher sales turnover and results in higher profitability (Brown & Dacin, 1997; Luo & Bhattacharya, 2006). Prior literature concurs that propensity to default has a direct association with incoming cash flows because a reliable stream of liquidity provides support to operations and prevents firms from financial distress (D'Aveni & Ilinitch, 1992). ESG disclosure ensures consistency of cash flow and is therefore likely to affect the default risk.

Second, firms may experience default in cases of volatile cash flows because infrequent income flows create cash shortfall in critical operational requirements (Chava & Purnanandam, 2010). ESG disclosure helps in stabilizing financial performance through corporate image, reputation and better relationship with governments and financial markets (Carter, 2005; Cornell & Shapiro, 1987; McGuire et al., 1988). For example, in the case of a negative event, stakeholders are more likely to punish the firm; however, a firm with ESG known initiatives is less likely to have a negative effect on cash flows due to its image and its moral capital among stakeholders (Godfrey, 2005). ESG disclosure creates loyalty and trust, as well as brand equity among customers; thus, income stream and profitability are less volatile, even during the time of a crisis (Godfrey, 2005; Godfrey et al., 2009). In addition, performance variability is less likely to happen when there are stable cash flows. Therefore, ESG disclosure acts as an ‘insurance’ for firms, protecting them from default by ensuring stable cash flows. Prior literature has also supported the notion that socially responsible investors (SRI) are less sensitive than conventional fund providers to negative events (Bollen, 2007; Renneboog et al., 2011), suggesting a continuous supply of funds to firms with better ESG disclosure.

Third, ESG disclosure reduces agency cost and information asymmetry in firms (Cormier et al., 2011). The availability of symmetric information across the market mitigates several risks: regulatory, controversy, managerial and reputational. Investors evaluate firms based on the availability of nonfinancial information to determine default probability. The provision of such firm-specific information to the market builds investors' trust and loyalty, which increases the availability of funds at relatively lower cost and reduces the cost of capital (Cheng et al., 2014; Cormier et al., 2010; El Ghoul et al., 2011; Waddock & Graves, 1997).[^5]

In a nutshell, ESG disclosure represents important initiatives that create a valuable image, that increase cash flow, that decrease performance variability and that garner support for the easy availability of low-cost funding, leading towards a reduction in default risk. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows:

**H1.** ESG disclosure is negatively associated with a firm's default risk.

**H2.** ESG disclosure is negatively associated with a firm's default risk through increased profitability and reduced performance variability and cost of debt.

## 3 | DATA AND METHOD

### 3.1 | Sample and data
Our sample, for the period from 2006 to 2017, is sourced from Bloomberg and Singapore National Universal liquidity data for US firms listed in the New York and Chicago exchanges.[^6] First, we collect the ESG disclosure scores and firm characteristics from Bloomberg, disregarding the difference in industries, and receive 9455 firm-year observations.[^7] Since our research is not applicable to financial service firms, we receive 7412 firm-year observations. The selection of firms is also based on the availability of aggregate and individual ESG disclosure scores, as well as on firm characteristics. Second, we collect the default risk variables from the Singapore National University data for all the US firms and merge them with the ESG data. We eliminate the missing firm-year observations across all the variables. Finally, we receive 5206 firm-year observations as our selected sample. Appendix A presents the detail of our sample selection criteria.

### 3.2 | Measuring ESG
In order to measure a firm's ESG disclosure following prior literature (e.g., Cheng et al., 2014; Sassen et al., 2016), we use ESG disclosure scores as an umbrella measure provided by Bloomberg to examine the effect on the firm's default risk. ESG disclosure scores are provided in the form of numbers ranging from 0.1 to 100. Our additional analysis employed the individual scores of environmental, social and governance separately. These individual scores represent firm performance on each factor individually. For instances, environmental scores are based on firm performance relating to climate change policies, hazardous wastes, nuclear energy, and sustainability indicators. Social scores are based on firm performance relating to consumer protections, diversity, human rights, animal, welfare, child labour and employee health and safety indicators. Governance scores are based on firm performance of management structure, executive compensation and conflict of interest indicators.

### 3.3 | Measuring default risk
Our first measure of default risk, Merton's (1974) distance to default (DD), remains the most widely used market-based credit risk metric and performs better than accounting-based approaches for the estimation of default risk (Bharath & Shumway, 2008; Das et al., 2009; Duan et al., 2012; Duan et al., 2018; Hillegeist et al., 2004; Jessen & Lando, 2015; Miao et al., 2018).[^8] Merton's (1974) structural models used option pricing methods to compute a probability of default from the level and volatility of the market value of assets, with a strike price equal to the face value of the firm's liabilities (Bharath & Shumway, 2008). The larger the positive distance between firm value and firm liabilities, the lower would be the probability of default. In other words, the higher the DD, the lower the default risk, meaning more firm stability.

Our second proxy of default risk is the credit default swap (CDS) spread. CDS are credit derivatives that allow the transfer of the firm's default risk between two agents for a predetermined time period. In a typical CDS contract, the protection seller offers the protection buyer insurance against the default of an underlying bond issued by a certain company (the reference entity). In the event of default by the reference entity, the seller commits to buy the bond for a price equal to its face value from the protection buyer. In exchange for the insurance, the buyer pays a quarterly premium, called the CDS spread, which is quoted as an annualized percentage of the notional value insured. Therefore, by definition, the CDS spread is the pricing of the default risk (Das et al., 2009). The higher the default risk of the reference entity, the higher is the CDS spread. We extract the CDS spread from the National University of Singapore's Credit Research Initiative (CRI) database. In that database, CDS spread, or ‘Actuarial Spread’, is constructed using the traditional CDS design but without an upfront fee, on the assumption that market participants are risk neutral. Thus, actuarial spread has the same features as the standard CDS spread. This adjustment allows the calculation of CDS spread for a large number of firms.

### 3.4 | Measuring control variables
Following prior studies (e.g., Ali et al., 2018; Chiang et al., 2015; Schultz et al., 2017), we control for several variables which might potentially influence default risk. We first control for firm size (SIZE), measured as the natural log of total assets in millions of US$. In general, larger firms are more stable and consequently have less default risk; thus, we expect a negative relationship between firm size and default risk. Second, we control for leverage, the ratio of total debt to total assets (TLTA). Leverage reflects the capital structure of the firm: that is, how a firm finances its assets, as well as the ability of the firm to meet its financial obligations. Leverage increases the financial risk of firms and is thus expected to have a positive relationship with default risk (Chiang et al., 2015; Schultz et al., 2017). Third, we control for firm profitability using return on assets (ROA), which is equal to the ratio of net income to total assets. ROA, based on accounting information, indicates the ability of a firm to generate sufficient returns for the smooth functioning of the firm. Higher ROAs would indicate lower default risk. Fourth, we control for liquidity (LIQ), which indicates the ability of the firm to pay its short-term obligations. Default risk should be less in firms that have a higher liquidity ratio than in those with a lower liquidity ratio. We capture liquidity through the current ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities).

Our fifth variable, Tobin's Q (TOBINQ), considers the market information and is arguably a better measurement of both performance and growth opportunity. Tobin's Q, measured by summing fair market value and total liabilities divided by total assets, is expected to have a negative impact on default risk since better performance leads to higher stability (Chiang et al., 2015). The sixth control variable, the sales to asset ratio (SALES), is measured as the ratio of sales to total assets. SALES represents management efficiency and is expected to have a negative influence on the default risk (Miglani et al., 2015). Seventh, we control for tangibility (TANG), measured as the ratio of net property plant and equipment divided by total assets. Tangibility is found to be negatively related to firm value (Fukui & Ushijima, 2007; Nakano & Nguyen, 2013). Hence, we expect tangibility variables to be positively related to default risk (Chiang et al., 2015).

To reduce the influence of outliers in our estimates, we have winsorized all other control variables at 1%. We keep the key independent variable (ESG) at its original values as we observed no problem of outlier in the descriptive statistics. We also keep the dependent variables (DD and CDS) at their original values, as extreme values on DD and CDS indicate worst performance (bankruptcy).

### 3.5 | Estimation model
We estimate the following model to examine the effect of ESG disclosure on firm default risk:

$$Default\ risk_{it} = \alpha + \beta_1(ESG\_disclosure)_{it} + \beta_2(Controls)_{it} + \delta_3 \sum(industry\ effects)_i + \delta_4 \sum(year\ effects)_t + \epsilon_{it} \tag{1}$$

where dependent variable default risk is measured by Merton's distance to default (DD) and credit default swap spread (CDS). ESG disclosure is measured as the aggregate environmental, social and governance disclosure scores of firm $i$ in year $t$. Controls represents control variables as defined in Section 3.4. Industry effects represents the two-digit industry codes based on global industry classification standards (GICS), and year effects shows the sample years (2006–2017). See Appendix B for the description of variables.

### 3.6 | Descriptive statistics
Table 1 presents the summary statistics of various measures of ESG disclosure, default risk and firm-specific variables. The ESG disclosure variables show that, on average, the overall ESG score is 19.94, the environmental score is 22.49, the social score is 19.46 and the governance score is 52.29. The default risk variable shows that the average DD is 5.88 in the full sample. The mean DD of firms with high ESG disclosure (i.e., 6.60) is considerably higher than the mean DD of firms with low ESG disclosure (i.e., 5.24), suggesting that the firms with more ESG disclosure have lower default risk. The average size (SIZE) of the firm-specific variables is 11,899 million USD. On average, the sample firms have 2% profit (ROA), have 2.58 USD in current assets to repay 1 USD of current liabilities (LIQ), have 52% debt in their capital structure (TLTA) and have 0.97 sales as a proportion of total assets. Asset tangibility (TANG) and growth opportunities (TOBINQ) average 0.27 and 2.07, respectively. Those firms with high ESG scores are larger, more profitable and levered and have more tangibility. However, these firms are less liquid, with lower sales to total assets and lower growth.

### 3.7 | Correlation analysis
Table 2 presents the pairwise correlation among the variables used in empirical analysis. The correlation between CDS and DD is −.49, which is not very high, suggesting that these are the alternative proxies of default risk. In the relationship between ESG and default risk, the ESG scores have a significant negative (positive) correlation with the CDS (DD), as anticipated. These results suggest that for firms with a greater ESG score, the default risk is lower. However, the correlation analysis does not control other factors that affect default risk, so these results should be viewed with caution. For correlation between the control variables and default risk, we find that large firms with more profitability (ROA), firm value (TOBINQ), sales and liquidity (LIQ) have a lower default risk. However, leverage (TLTA) and tangibility (TANG) increase default risk. The correlation analysis also indicates that collinearity is generally moderate between the explanatory variables. As a rule of thumb, a correlation of .70 or higher in absolute value may indicate a multicollinearity issue. The highest correlation coefficient (.63) is between ESG and SIZE variables. Thus, multicollinearity may not be an issue in our analysis.

Figure 1 presents the industry-wide differences in the average DD between firms with high and low ESG scores. It is evident from the figure that the firms with high ESG disclosure have a lower default risk than the firms with low ESG disclosure for all industries. However, some industries benefit more from high ESG than others do. For instance, default risk reduction with high ESG disclosure is the highest in Health and Telecommunication Services (47% each) and is the lowest in Energy and in Consumer Discretionary (17% each). Overall, Figure 1 demonstrates that industries with high ESG disclosure have a lower default risk. Given this evidence, it is important to include this industry effect in the empirical analysis.

## 4 | ESG DISCLOSURE AND DEFAULT RISK (H1)

### 4.1 | Baseline results
Table 3 presents the OLS results of Equation 1 between ESG and default risk (as measured by DD or CDS). The results for DD and CDS are reported in Columns 1–3 and 4–6, respectively. ### 4.2 | Endogeneity bias
The relationship between firm default risk and the ESG may possibly be affected by endogeneity. Two of these possible sources of endogeneity (i.e., omitted variable bias and reverse causality) are likely to bias our main results regarding how ESG influences default risk. First, omitted variables (e.g., time varying and time unvarying; observable and unobservable) may simultaneously affect both ESG and default risk. It is difficult, if not impossible, to capture all the determinants of default risk in the empirical models, which leads to the omitted variable bias. Second, the direction of causation between ESG disclosure and default risk is not clear. For instance, Hermalin and Weisbach (2001) suggest that a firm's governance structure is endogenously determined. Bouslah et al. (2013) find a bidirectional causality between corporate social performance and firm risk. Given this, it is possible that ESG disclosure and default risk are determined simultaneously: that is, not only does ESG disclosure influence firm risk—firms also adjust ESG disclosure based on the current risk exposure. In such circumstances, current ESG disclosure is likely to be influenced by a past realization of default risk (i.e., reverse causality). Hence, as part of our identification strategy, we address these endogeneity concerns in three ways. First, we employ lagged independent variables to control for reverse causality. Regressions based on contemporaneous variables are susceptible to endogeneity bias due to reverse causality, whereas regressions based on lagged values of independent variables help to control for reverse causality and thus tend to be less susceptible to endogeneity effects (Harjoto & Jo, 2015; Luo & Bhattacharya, 2009). Second, we adopt an instrumental variable approach, two-stage least squares (2SLS), to address the possible endogeneity of the ESG disclosure score. Third, we execute PSM, which can help solve the reverse causality and self-selection bias.

As can be seen from these results, ESG is positively (negatively) related to DD (CDS), suggesting that the prior-year ESG inversely affects the current year's default risk. #### 4.2.2 | Instrumental variable approach
The instrumental variable approach requires an instrument that is correlated with the endogenous variable (i.e., ESG) but that does not have a direct influence on the dependent variable (i.e., default risk), except through the endogenous variable (Kennedy, 2003, p. 159). Specifically, a valid instrument must satisfy two conditions. First, the relevance condition requires that, after controlling for the set of variables in our model, the instrument correlates with ESG. Second, the exclusion restriction requires that, conditional to the full set of control variables, the instrument influences default risk only through its correlations with ESG.

Our instrument in this study, the industry median ESG, is computed as the average score of ESG of all the firms in a particular year except firm $i$'s score of ESG in that year.[^10] The intuition behind using this instrument is that a firm's ESG might be highly related to the industry peers due to their similar business mix and investment opportunities, but such an industry average is unlikely to directly affect a firm's default risk. Moreover, firm-level default risk may affect firm-level ESG, whereas it is less likely to affect industry-level ESG. Given these arguments, the industry median ESG should be a valid instrument, since it is related to firm-level ESG but unrelated to firm-level default risk.

Column 1 in Table 5 reports the first-stage regression results, where the dependent variable is the ESG. The explanatory variables include the instrumental variable and the same control variables as in Equation 1. The coefficient on ESG (IND) is statistically significant at the 1% level, suggesting that the instrument is valid because of its relevance and its statistical power to explain ESG. We also check the validity of the instrument. First, the reported F statistics of the first-stage regression is very high, which indicates that our instrument is not weak. Moreover, as the Cragg–Donald's Wald F statistic is greater than the critical value (at the 10% level) given by Stock and Yogo (2005), this again rejects the null hypothesis that the instrument is not weak.

Columns 2 and 3 of Table 5 present the second-stage regression results in which we regress the ESG (fitted) on default risk while controlling other firm-specific variables. Consistent with our earlier findings, the coefficient on ESG is statistically significant and negative (positive) for CDS (DD), indicating that a higher ESG disclosure leads to lower default risk. Therefore, our results are robust to the use of the 2SLS approach and seem not to be driven by an endogeneity bias.

#### 4.2.3 | PSM estimate
To further eliminate the endogeneity bias, we execute two-step PSM, in which firm-years with higher ESG are matched with firm-years with lower ESG, but with no significant differences in terms of all other variables (see, e.g., Atif et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2017). Specifically, in the first step, we create a dummy variable (high-ESG) that takes the value ‘1’ if ESG score is greater than the sample median and ‘0’ otherwise. Firms with ESG scores greater than the sample median are considered in the treatment group whereas firms with ESG scores lower than sample median are included in the control group. We run the logit regression for high-ESG with all the explanatory variables specified in Equation 1. The predicted estimates from the logit regression are used as the propensity scores for each firm-year observation. The logit regression results are reported in Column 1, Panel A, Table 6. The regression results suggest that firms with higher ESG are larger in size and have lower leverage, more tangibility, more sales and a higher firm value (TOBINQ). The pseudo-R² for the regression is high, with a value of 34.05%.

In the second step, we use the propensity scores to form one-to-one matched pairs, ensuring that the firms with ESG higher than the median (treatment group) are sufficiently similar to the firms with ESG lower than the median (control group). Specifically, each firm with higher ESG was matched to a firm with lower ESG using the closest propensity score. We employ matching with replacement, requiring that the difference in the propensity scores of the treatment and the matched firms does not exceed 0.1% in absolute value. With the application of these criteria, we are able to effectively match 1922 firm-year observations; hence, our treatment and control groups are nearly identical along all explanatory variables, with one exception: ESG.

We run two diagnostic tests to verify that the treatment and control groups are indistinguishable in terms of observable characteristics. In the first diagnostic test, we re-estimate the logit regression for the post-match sample. The results are reported in Column 2, Panel A, Table 6. Most of the regression coefficients are statistically insignificant and are smaller than those in the column pre-match, suggesting that both groups are almost similar in terms of observable characteristics. The pseudo-R² drops substantially from 34.05% for the pre-match sample to 0.1% for the post-match sample. This implies that the PSM removes all observable differences other than the difference in ESG.

In the second diagnostic test, we examine the difference for each observable characteristic between the treated firms and the control firms, reporting the results in Panel B, Table 6. Again, we find no significant difference in observable characteristics between the two groups. These results suggest that PSM removes other observable differences, increasing the probability that any difference in the default risk between the treated and control groups can be attributed to differences in ESG rather than to differences in other variables.

The PSM estimates and the multivariate results using the matched sample are reported in Panels C and D, Table 6, respectively. As evident in Panel C, Table 6, we find significant differences in both default risk measures between the treatment and control group.[^11] In particular, we find that the CDS spread is lower and distance to default is higher in the firms with higher ESG than in the otherwise indistinguishable firms with relatively lower ESG. Likewise, the multivariate results reported in Panel D, Table 6 show that firms with higher ESG have lower default risk. These results from the propensity match method suggest that the variation in default risk is attributable to the systematic differences in ESG. Hence, we infer that greater ESG leads to greater stability in firms.

Natural experiment is one of the state-of-the-art solutions to the endogeneity problem, as suggested by several empirical studies (see, e.g., Black et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2015; Gippel et al., 2015). However, the implementation of such a methodology requires a purely exogenous natural event, such as mandatory ESG disclosure reforms. In the context of our study, we are unable to use natural experimentation because of the absence of any mandatory ESG disclosure reforms in the United States during our sample period. Therefore, the disclosure of ESG is still at the discretion of the firm due to the absence of any credibly exogenous reform. Overall, we acknowledge that it is not possible to completely eliminate endogeneity. However, our results, based on lagged independent variables, an instrumental variable and the PSM technique, provide a certain degree of comfort that even when considering endogeneity due to omitted variable and reverse causality, the main findings regarding the relationship of ESG disclosure to default risk do not change.

## 5 | ROLE OF PROFITABILITY, PERFORMANCE VARIABILITY AND COST OF DEBT (H2)

In Section 2, we argue that profitability, performance variability and cost of debt/capital are the important mechanisms through which ESG disclosure influences default risk. In this section, we empirically test these assertions.

First, we investigate the relationship between ESG and default risk via profitability. We use two common measures of profitability: (1) return on assets (ROA), measured as net income divided by total assets, and (2) return on equity (ROE), measured as net income divided by common shareholder equity. Table 7 presents the interaction effect of ESG and profitability (using ROA and ROE) on default risk in Columns 1–2 and 3–4 for DD and CDS, respectively. As expected, we find that the coefficients on the interaction terms (ESG * ROA and ESG * ROE) are statistically significant, positive for DD and negative for CDS. These results suggest that firms with better ESG disclosure have higher profitability and thus have a lower level of default risk.

Second, we examine the association between ESG and performance variability. We measure performance variability using the quantile regression forecasts of the interquartile range of the distribution of profitability: that is, ROA (see, Konstantinidi & Pope, 2016). This approach, which relies only on cross-sectional fundamental characteristics, requires time series data for computation. We forecast performance in different quantiles using the following equation:

$$QUANT_q(ROA_{it} | X_{it}) = \beta_{0t}^q + \beta_{1t}^q ROA_{it-1} + \beta_{2t}^q LOSS_{it-1} + \beta_{3t}^q (LOSS_{it-1} \times ROA_{it-1}) + \beta_{4t}^q ACC_{it-1} + \beta_{5t}^q PAYER_{it-1} + \beta_{6t}^q PAYOUT_{it-1} \tag{2}$$

Our model resembles that by Correia et al. (2018), with the exception that we forecast return on assets (ROA), rather than return on net operating assets equity (RNOA). LOSS, a dummy variable, equals 1 if ROA < 0 and 0 otherwise. ACC is the difference between net income and operating cash flow, scaled by total assets. PAYER, a dummy variable, equals 1 if PAYOUT > 0 and 0 otherwise. PAYOUT is the ratio of dividend paid divided by net income.

First, we capture conditional dispersion in the future earnings distribution using the predicted interquartile range (IQR) = $Q_{i75} - Q_{i25}$, a commonly used measure of earning dispersion. The higher the IQR, the higher is the uncertainty in future earnings. Second, we estimate conditional skewness in the future earnings distribution as SKEW = $[(Q_{i75} - Q_{i50}) - (Q_{i50} - Q_{i25})]/IQR_i$, which captures the balance between upside risk relative to downside risk in future earnings within the two middle quartiles. Third, we estimate conditional kurtosis in the future earnings distribution as KURT = $[(Q_{i87.5} - Q_{i62.5}) + (Q_{i37.5} - Q_{i12.5})]/IQR_i$, which measures the density of the distribution close to Q25 and Q75, relative to the density close to Q50. Finally, we decompose the numerator of KURT to capture asymmetry in the relative densities of the distribution surrounding Q25 and Q75 as $UP_i = [(Q_{i87.5} - Q_{i62.5})]/IQR_i$ and $DOWN_i = [(Q_{i37.5} - Q_{i12.5})]/IQR_i$. The higher the SKEW and KURT, the higher is the uncertainty in the future earnings realization.

Table 8 presents the results between ESG and the five performance variability measures, IQR (Column 1), SKEW (Column 2), KURT (Column 3), UP (Column 4) and DOWN (Column 5). We find that the coefficients of ESG are negative and significant for all performance variability proxies except SKEW. Overall, these results provide empirical support to our argument that ESG disclosure enhances performance or earning stability and thus lowers the level of default risk.

Finally, we test the linkage between ESG and default risk via cost of debt. On the one hand, the firms experience default if they are unable to meet contractual debt payment (i.e., principal and interest or cost of debt). On the other hand, firms with more investment in ESG activities are considered safer by debtholders, and thus, they require less risk premium, which reduces the cost of debt (Eliwa et al., 2019). We tested if this is true for our sample. We obtain the cost of debt data for our sample firms from the Bloomberg database.

First, we test the effect of cost of debt on default risk, reporting our results in Table 9, with Column 1 for DD and Column 2 for CDS. We find a significant negative (positive) coefficient of cost of debt with DD (CDS), suggesting that firms have higher default risk if the cost of debt is higher. Second, we test the impact of ESG on cost of debt and reported the results in Column 3, Table 9. We find a significant negative coefficient on ESG with cost of debt, which implies that firms with higher ESG disclosure have a lower cost of debt. Taken together, these results indicate that ESG reduces default risk by reducing the cost of debt.

### 6.1 | Role of corporate life cycle
In this section, we report our exploration of the effect of ESG disclosure on default risk at various stages of the firm life cycle, as argued in the introduction. We use three alternative methods to classify firms into different life cycle stages. First, we adopt the cash flow-based method developed by Dickinson (2011), who argues that cash flow captures differences in firm profitability, growth and risk. Thus, one may use operating (CFO), investing (CFI) and financing (CFF) cash flows to classify firms into five stages: introduction, growth, mature, shake-out and decline. The firm is considered in the introduction stage if CFO < 0, CFI < 0 and CFF > 0; growth, if CFO > 0, CFI < 0 and CFF > 0; mature, if CFO > 0, CFI < 0 and CFF < 0; and decline, if CFO < 0, CFI > 0 and CFF ≤ 0 or CFF ≥ 0, with the remaining firm-years classified under the shake-out stage.[^12]

Second, we follow DeAngelo et al. (2006) and use retained earnings to total assets (RETA) as a proxy for the life cycle. RETA measures the extent to which a firm is reliant on internal or external financing. Firms with high RETA are mature or older, with declining investment, whereas firms with low RETA are in the young and growth stages. Finally, as life cycle stages naturally relate to firm age, we use firm age as a simple and natural proxy of life cycle. We measure firm age as a difference between the current fiscal year and the year of incorporation.

First, we investigate the direct effect of firm life cycle on default risk. The results are reported in Panel A, Table 10. All the control variables are the same as in Equation 1. Column 1 includes the regression results using the Dickinson (2011) life cycle proxy. Compared to the shake-out stage, the introduction, growth and decline stages are significantly negatively associated with DD, while the mature stage of the life cycle is positively associated with DD (p < .01). Columns 2 and 3 include the regression results using the RETA and AGE life cycle proxies, respectively. These results suggest that both RETA and AGE are positively and significantly associated with DD (p < .01). Overall, these results suggest that mature and older firms have lower default risk whereas introductory and younger firms have higher default risk.

Second, and more importantly, we investigate the interactive effect of ESG and firm life cycle on default risk. These results are reported in Panel B, Table 10. All the control variables are the same as in Equation 1. Columns 4–6 include the regression results using Dickinson (2011) life cycle stages, RETA and AGE life cycle proxies, respectively. The results suggest that the coefficient on ESG and Dickinson (2011) life cycle stages with DD is significant and positive only for the mature stage. Moreover, both the coefficients on the interaction between ESG * RETA and ESG * AGE with DD are positive and statistically significant. These results imply that firms with higher ESG disclosure reduce default risk for only those firms that are mature and older.

Finally, we use a split sample strategy to investigate the effect of ESG on default risk in different life cycle stages. For a Dickinson (2011) life cycle proxy, we run separate regressions for firms in introduction, growth, mature and decline stages. For RETA and AGE life cycle proxies, we split the sample into old firms, with RETA and AGE higher than the sample median, and as young, with RETA and AGE lower than the sample median. The unreported results indicate that ESG reduces default risk only during Dickinson's mature stage. ESG disclosure significantly reduces default risk for old age firms only and significantly reduces default risk only for high RETA firms (i.e., mature firms).

Table 11 reports the results between the components of ESG disclosure (environmental, social and governance) and default risk. These analyses are important to rule out the possibility that our results are driven by one of the components. Columns 1–3 of Table 11 show that ESG disclosures are positively and significantly associated with DD: that is, a lower default risk. ### 6.3 | Excluding GFC
For this section, we exclude the extreme observations during the global finance crisis (GFC) and then investigate the impact of ESG on default risk. We consider observations during 2008 and 2009 as GFC. Our untabulated results suggest that even when we exclude GFC years, our main inference does not change: that is, ESG disclosure reduces default risk. We also conduct pre-GFC and post-GFC analyses to check if the relationship between ESG disclosure and default risk is consistent across different periods. Our results reveal that the impact of ESG on default risk exists for both pre-GFC and post-GFC periods. Finally, we test the interaction effect of ESG disclosure and GFC on default risk and find that the interaction term is positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. These results indicate that firms with high ESG disclosure had lower default risk during the GFC period.

### 6.4 | Alternative measure of ESG
Our analyses so far have been based on the ESG disclosure data (instead of ESG performance) obtained from the Bloomberg database. Cho et al. (2015) argue that managers may engage in the deceitful behaviour of creating organizational facades for signalling purposes. With this in mind, we use an alternative measure of ESG from the Thomson Reuters Eikon (Asset4) database which is designed to transparently and objectively measure a company's relative ESG performance, commitment and effectiveness, in terms of environmental aspects (i.e., resource use, emissions and innovation), social aspects (workforce, human rights, community and product responsibility) and governance aspects (management, stakeholders and corporate social responsibility strategy). The results are reported in Table 12. Consistent with our baseline results reported in Table 3, we find that overall ESG performance and its factors (i.e., E, S and G) are significantly and positively (negatively) associated with DD (CDS), suggesting that the firms with higher ESG performance have a lower level of default risk.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

ESG performance (also termed nonfinancial or extra-financial information) has emerged as an important consideration for evaluating investment decisions. On the other hand, default risk has become an important yardstick of firms' financial health due to its severe consequences for firms. However, previous literature has not revealed how ESG disclosure influences default risk and through which mechanisms. We explore the effect of ESG disclosure on default risk for the nonfinancial US firms over the period from 2006 to 2017. Our results suggest that ESG disclosure is positively related to default risk as proxied by the distance to default (DD) measure and is negatively related to credit default swap spread (CDS). We also explore various mechanisms through which ESG disclosure reduces default risk. We find that the inverse effect ESG disclosure on default risk is through increased profitability and reduced performance variability. We also find that ESG reduces default risk via cost of debt. We specifically find that firms with a lower cost of debt have a lower default risk and firms with high ESG have a lower cost of debt. Finally, we document that the negative impact of ESG on default risk exists only for mature and older firms. Our findings are consistent when using alternative variable specifications and alternative estimation methods, including lagged independent variables, an instrumental variable approach and PSM.

Our study contributes to the stream of research that explores the determinants of firms' default risk. We extend this literature by providing robust evidence on the role of aggregate ESG disclosure as critical determinants of default risk for nonfinancial US firms. Moreover, our study extends the literature on the consequences of ESG by examining various mechanisms through which ESG disclosure reduces default risk. Finally, we contribute to the firm life cycle literature by providing empirical evidence that the corporate life cycle moderates the effect of ESG disclosure on default risk. Our findings are important for the potential of SRI to screen investment and to facilitate fund availability for firms with a better ESG outlook. Our findings also provide insights for debtholders to consider a firm's sustainability initiative before making decisions. Given our findings, policy makers aiming at sustainability should encourage firms to disclose their sustainability initiatives.
