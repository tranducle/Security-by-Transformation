# Environment, Social, and Governance Disclosures and Firm Performance: a Critical Review and Future Agenda

**Neha Saini¹ · Monica Singhania¹ · Preeti Roy² · Shalini Aggarwal³ · Ravita Kharb⁴**

Received: 1 July 2024 / Accepted: 10 May 2025
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2025

### Abstract
Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) is a framework for assessing and quantifying the sustainability performance of a business and the potential value of its ESG practices. ESG offers investors, regulators, and stakeholders a standardized method for evaluating corporate sustainability. The present study performs a meta-analysis to explain the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance by analyzing the combined effect of ESG disclosure and the individual effects of environmental, social, and governance disclosures. A sub-group analysis considers three different measures of firm performance: cost of capital (weighted average cost of capital, cost of equity, and cost of debt), accounting measures (return on assets, return on equity, and earnings per share), and market measures (Tobin’s *Q*). To address heterogeneity issues related to ESG disclosure and firm performance, the effects of different moderators (firm size and visibility) and control variables (ownership structure, research and development intensity, capital expenditure, sales growth, risk, firm age, publication quality, and ESG data source) are included. The meta-analysis includes 95 papers representing a sample of 60,247 firm-level observations. The result shows that ESG disclosure positively influences a firm’s performance, with pronounced effects of environmental and social disclosures. All three dimensions of ESG disclosure reduce the cost of capital and significantly increase firm performance. Our findings suggest that companies should prioritize the adoption of adequate environmental safeguards to enhance the quality of human and natural resources, the decentralization of ESG reporting practices at relevant levels, the adoption of mandatory measures for increasing transparency, the formulation of risk management portfolios, and the creation of new databases focused on separate ESG activities.

**Keywords** ESG disclosure · Firm performance · Meta-analysis · Subgroup analysis · Literature review

## Introduction

Businesses employ the environment, social, and governance (ESG) framework to take social, environmental, and corporate governance concerns into account when making decisions (Carroll & Shabana, 2010; Yu et al., 2024). ESG is less about a company’s retrograde control systems and more about its capacity to perceive and predict what it requires to thrive without endangering people and the environment (Brockett & Rezaee, 2012). To protect the rights and interests of shareholders or creditors, the market must be able to make informed decisions about an investment’s value (Ambec & Lanoie, 2008; Barnett & Salomon, 2012). This is achieved through the legal mechanism known as “ESG information disclosure,” which requires management to disclose a company’s ESG and financial information in a timely, accurate, and comprehensive manner (Yang et al., 2020). The United Nations Environmental Programme Financial Initiative (UNEP FI) first began urging financial institutions to consider ESG in decision-making in 1992 (Wahba & Elsayed 2015), and the global community has since steadily adopted ESG disclosure as a primary indicator of an economic entity’s capacity for sustainable development (Busch & Friede, 2018).

The European Union (EU) has set the standard for ESG with its double materiality approach (Kitzmueller & Shimshack, 2012), which requires businesses to consider how their operations affect the environment and society and how external factors such as climate change may affect their financial value (Ng & Rezaee, 2015). Through the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD), the EU mandates ESG disclosures by approximately 11,700 public interest entities (listed firms, banks, and insurers). Organizations engaging in ESG risk management and performance improvement may also enhance their financial performance by prioritizing the decarbonization of assets based on emissions intensity, creating environmentally friendly products and reducing the footprint of their current portfolios to boost sales, and implementing robust operational risk management initiatives to reduce incidents and encourage employee health (Brower & Rowe, 2017; Garcia et al., 2017; Margolis et al., 2009; Orlitzky et al., 2003; Wang & Sarkis, 2017). Investors, firms, management, and other stakeholders now recognize that ESG performance is a crucial means of increasing a company’s value (Friedman, 2007a, 2007b).

ESG is a collaborative endeavor that requires multiple corporate departments to gather and compile data, recognize and handle risks, and enhance overall performance (Testa & D’Amato 2017). Organizations must establish robust processes and allocate resources for software to facilitate the dismantling of organizational silos and foster the organized collaboration of various corporate stakeholders (Friede et al., 2015). Management must understand that integrating ESG performance into the firm’s managerial strategies can benefit the organization. ESG information benefits investors and society because financial markets play a major part in maintaining social activities.

Despite the perceived importance of ESG for firm performance, only four studies have specifically examined this relationship: Friede et al. (2015), Brooks & Oikonomou (2017), Zubeltzu-Jaka et al. (2018), and Huang (2021) (Table 1). The present study expands the scope of these studies by using a larger sample and incorporating the different aspects of ESG to establish a modest statistical relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance. The present study performs a meta-analysis to explain the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance by analyzing the combined effect of ESG disclosure and the individual effects of environmental, social, and governance disclosures. A sub-group analysis considers three different measures of firm performance: cost of capital (weighted average cost of capital, cost of equity, and cost of debt), accounting measures (return on assets, return on equity, and earnings per share), and market measures (Tobin’s *Q*). To address heterogeneity issues related to ESG disclosure and firm performance, the effects of different moderators (firm size and visibility) and control variables (Research and development (R&D) intensity, ownership structure, capital expenditure, sales growth, risk, firm age, publication quality, and ESG data source) are included. The meta-analysis includes 95 papers representing a sample of 60,247 firm-level observations. Exhibit 1 lists the variables examined by the hypotheses and their corresponding measures (Fig. 1).

## Conceptual Mapping and Hypothesis Development

ESG establishes a methodology for evaluating and measuring a company’s sustainability performance (Giese et al., 2019; Saini et al., 2022). ESG disclosure is an important tool for companies to communicate their environmental, social, and governance practices, ensuring that business operations are in line with the expectations of different stakeholders (Li et al., 2018; Lokuwaduge & Heenetigala, 2017). According to stakeholder theory, a company’s responsibilities extend beyond its shareholders to include employees, customers, suppliers, and communities (Freeman, 1984; Hasan et al., 2018). Transparent ESG reporting ensures that these various interests are addressed, which fosters trust and accountability. Similarly, agency theory emphasizes conflict resolution between shareholders (principals) and executives (agents) by ensuring that management’s actions are consistent with shareholder interests. ESG disclosures serve as a mechanism for increasing transparency, resolving agency conflicts, and promoting responsible decision-making (Abraham et al., 2024; Hussain et al., 2018). In addition, the corporate social responsibility (CSR) theory advocates that businesses prioritize societal and environmental well-being alongside profit generation (Aboud & Diab, 2018). ESG disclosures incorporate CSR principles, demonstrating not only ethical practices but also a company’s commitment to sustainable development (Duque et al., 2019; Dzomonda & Fatoki, 2020). Together, these theories highlight the importance of ESG reporting as a multifaceted.

### ESG Disclosures and Firm Performance

ESG practices are often associated with sustainability and can lead to long-term value creation (Aggarwal et al. 2025). Investors and stakeholders are increasingly focusing on ESG factors because of their strong association with organizational performance. Positive ESG practices contribute to a strong corporate reputation, which enhances customer loyalty, attracts talent, and influences customer behavior (Abdullah et al., 2020; Albitar & Gerged, 2020; Alareeni & Hamdan 2020). Based on these findings, we develop the following hypothesis:

*   **H₁: ESG disclosures have a positive effect on firm performance.**

### E, S, and G Measures

#### Environmental Disclosures
Orlitzky and Benjamin (2001), Chaudhry et al. (2021), and Al-Shaer and Hussainey (2022) acknowledged the importance of environmental performance for firm performance. Moreover, Albertini (2014) argued that firms with superior environmental performance can strengthen their competitive advantage, which enhances market perception and reduces litigation risk (Koh et al., 2013; Aggarwal et al., 2025), resulting in positive cash flows and market value (Kharb et al., 2024). Xie et al. (2017) further suggested that disclosure reduces the frequency of environmental regulatory intervention, reducing costs and improving operational efficiency. On the basis of the above argument, we formulated the following hypothesis.

*   **H₂: Environmental disclosures have a positive effect on firm performance.**

**Carbon Emission Reduction and Climate Change Policy** At the microlevel, corporate environmental performance is judged based on inputs, outputs, and compliance (Hussain et al., 2018). Specific environmental disclosures that have been found to influence firm performance include the disclosure of reduced carbon emissions (Dzomonda & Fatoki, 2020; King & Lenox, 2000) and compliance policy (intention and acts) on reducing global GHG emissions (Hussain et al., 2018; Jacob et al., 2023), giving rise to H₃:

*   **H₃: (a) Carbon emission reductions and (b) climate change policy initiatives positive effect on firm performance.**

#### Social Disclosures
Mathews (1997) adopted legitimacy theory to explain firms’ need to report social information. Treating the environment and society with respect gives firms a license to function in society. However, the discussion of social disclosures is incomplete without considering the resource-based view (Hart, 1995) and voluntary disclosure theory (Verrecchia, 2001) in this context. According to voluntary disclosure theory, firms with superior environmental or social performance accumulate resources and thus are incentivized to follow extensive disclosure practices, leading to H₄:

*   **H₄: Social disclosure has a positive effect on firm performance.**

**Human Rights Policy and Fair Remuneration Policy** Social disclosures include intangible assets such as stakeholder welfare (Jensen, 2001), and building a substantial reputation (Dowell et al. 2000). We consider two vital social policy activities, namely, human rights and fair remuneration (defined as equal opportunities without discrimination) (Hussain et al., 2018; Bhaskaran et al., 2019), as implicit components of social disclosure related to financial performance, leading to H₅:

*   **H₅: (a) Human rights policy and (b) fair remuneration policy initiatives have a positive effect on firm performance.**

#### Governance Disclosures
The two fundamental theories underpinning the relationship between corporate governance and firm performance are agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) and stakeholder theory (Freeman, 1984). According to Hussain et al. (2018), agency theory describes the presence of information asymmetry, conflicts of interest, and opportunistic behavior in the relationships of agents (managers) with principals (shareholders). This potential for harm may be controlled by implementing a corporate governance mechanism (Ho & Wong, 2001). Stakeholder theory claims that improving a company’s corporate social responsibility (CSR) participation may generate a variety of benefits, including a stronger reputation, a larger pool of competent staff (Jo et al., 2015), and greater shareholder value (Hasan et al., 2018), leading to H₆:

*   **H₆: Governance disclosures have a positive effect on firm performance.**

**Board Independence and Diversity** Agency problems can be resolved by incorporating board independence into the governance framework (Heracleous, 2001). According to resource dependency theory, the inclusion of women on the board aligns external resources with internal resources, enabling better supervision of management behavior (Ntim & Soobaroyen, 2013) and improved decision-making through quality discussions and empathetic presentation of different viewpoints (Adams & Ferreira, 2009). These benefits increase shareholder wealth, leading to H₇:

*   **H₇: (a) Board independence and (b) board diversity have a positive effect on firm performance.**

### Measuring Financial Performance
To evaluate the relationship between ESG and firm performance, we consider three measures of financial performance: cost of capital (cost of debt (CoD), cost of equity (CoE), and weighted average cost of capital (WACC)), profitability (return on assets (ROA), return on equity (ROE), earnings per share (EPS), and cumulative returns), and market value (Tobin’s *Q*).

#### Cost of Capital
Dhaliwal et al. (2011) suggested that financial and non-financial transparency reduces the cost of equity capital by lowering investors’ monitoring costs, which reduces their expected rate of return for holding stocks. In addition, Dhaliwal et al. (2011) observed a pronounced negative effect of CSR disclosure on firm performance in countries with a higher stakeholder orientation (Dhaliwal et al., 2014). These results indicate that the effect of disclosure depends on the measure of the cost of capital, leading to H₈:

*   **H₈: ESG disclosure is negatively related to different measures of the cost of capital.**

#### Accounting Measures
Orlitzky (2013) and Brooks & Oikonomou (2017) suggested that using accounting performance measures to evaluate the influence of CSR disclosures ensures better comparability and more robust results. ROA and ROE capture the historical profitability level of the firm and hint at management efficiency in business operations. If ROA is reasonable, with manageable debt levels, a higher ROE indicates greater management efficiency in increasing shareholder wealth. However, both measures are needed to obtain a clear picture of the effectiveness of management, leading to H₉:

*   **H₉: ESG disclosure is positively related to different measures of profitability, namely, ROA and ROE.**

#### Market Value Measure
Tobin’s *Q* considers investors’ past performance and future expectations based on fundamental or technical analysis (Bhaskaran et al., 2019; Chauhan & Kumar, 2019). It may even capture the effect of intangible assets but may be biased due to market anomalies (Daniel et al. 1998), leading to H₁₀:

*   **H₁₀: ESG disclosure is positively related to market value.**

### Moderator Analysis
Because heterogeneous effects of ESG disclosure on firm performance have been observed, it is necessary to evaluate the effects of moderators (Aguinis & Glavas, 2012). We consider two moderators: firm size and visibility in this study to capture the effect of heterogeneous effects.

**Firm Size**
Van de Ven (1986) and Youn et al. (2015) suggested that firm size is a potential moderator of the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance. Due to economies of scale, larger firms have greater resources, a significant competitive advantage, and a focus on better firm performance and legitimacy (King & Lenox, 2000; Wang et al., 2020). Larger firms also attract more public attention, greater scrutiny by regulatory agencies, and more pressure to disclose information, which boosts non-financial disclosure practices, leading to H₁₁:

*   **H₁₁: Firm size moderates the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance.**

**Visibility**
Visibility increases public monitoring and invites government regulations, enhancing stakeholder accountability. Yao and Liang (2019) found that increasing corporate visibility reduces the cost of capital and strengthens firm performance. Furthermore, greater disclosure increases the company’s popularity and reduces its risk of information asymmetry, increasing trading of its stock (Wang et al., 2018). It is argued that liquidity positively affects firm performance by enhancing oversight and minimizing agency costs (Abraham et al., 2024). Higher liquidity attracts block shareholding activities, mainly by institutional shareholders. leading to H₁₂:

*   **H₁₂: Visibility and liquidity moderate the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance.**

### Control Variables
The study also incorporates various control variables to address heterogeneity, improve the model fit, address publication bias, and strengthen the theoretical insights (Ross, 1988; Schwichow et al., 2016).

Financial leverage can be used by investors to amplify potential returns. Greater financial leverage is associated with lower profitability and market share of the firm. The presence of financial leverage is an estimate of the relationship between ESG and corporate financial performance (CFP) (Kumar & Firoz, 2022).

Ownership structure influences responses to government regulations concerning disclosure (Zeng et al., 2012). Although the presence of owners may boost ESG performance, owners may also exercise control over ESG information and use it for personal benefit at the expense of the wealth of other shareholders, thereby increasing the agency problem (Kao et al., 2018).

Sales growth is an important financial metric that supports and enhances ESG performance in various ways. Sales growth leads to higher revenue and provides companies with additional financial resources to invest in ESG initiatives (Patel et al., 2021).

Beta is used by investors as a measure of the risk associated with an investment. A company’s ESG measures affect its beta, which is used to estimate the CFP relationship.

Research and development (R&D) influences firm performance (McWilliams & Siegel, 2000). Hart and Ahuja (1996) found a positive link between R&D expenses and firm performance in the long run but a negative link in the short run. Capital expenditure (Capex) engages funds in long-term commitments whose benefits will be derived over time. Capex covers tangible and intangible expenditures and reflects the firm’s future growth potential (Trumpp & Guenther, 2017). On the basis of above argument, we formulated the hypothesis mentioned below.

*   **H₁₃: ESG disclosure affects firm performance through different control variables.**

## Methodology

In this study, a meta-analysis was performed to investigate the direct relationship between ESG disclosures and firm performance as well as the influence of various measures of the dependent and independent variables, potential moderators, and control variables.

### Sample Selection
The PRISMA methodology was employed as depicted in Fig. 2 to select the sample. A systematic literature review was performed according to the process suggested by Mehmood et al. (2023). An initial pool of 19,750 research studies was identified by searching Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, and Google Scholar using combinations of keywords (ESG, ESG and CFP, ESG disclosure and CFP, environmental disclosure and CFP, social disclosure and CFP, governance disclosure, and CFP). Exclusion and inclusion criteria were applied to systematically curate the final sample of 95 studies for the meta-analysis, as shown in Fig. 2. The inclusion criteria were as follows: articles that showed an association between ESG and corporate performance, articles only in the English language, articles from top-tier journals (ABDC: A, B, A*, and SCI journals) in Table 2, and studies reporting correlation coefficients (*r*), which are easily comprehended and scale-free, or standard regression coefficients when *r* values were unavailable. The exclusion criteria were studies not in the English language, book chapters, behavioral studies, conceptual and policy-based papers, conference papers, and studies that did not measure ESG as the independent variable and CFP as a dependent variable.

### Data Extraction and Coding
Although studies included in the meta-analysis used different measurement methods, their conceptual definitions and assessment items were largely consistent. To adopt a uniform coding pattern for recording the data, we first coded the primary constructs, such as ESG disclosure as an independent variable and firm performance as a dependent variable. We also collected data on the various components of ESG disclosure: composite ESG disclosure, E disclosure, S disclosure, G disclosure, climate change and emissions, remuneration, human rights, percentage of directors, and percentage of women directors. CFP was measured in multiple ways: cost of capital, accounting measures such as ROA, ROE, and EPS, and Tobin’s *Q*. Firm size and visibility were used as moderating variables. Ownership structure, Capex, sales growth, risk, firm age, publication quality, and ESG data sources were used as control variables for sensitivity analysis (Exhibit 1). A uniform coding pattern was used to record data on ESG disclosure and firm performance effect sizes, sample sizes, and inter-coder reliability. Effect sizes were initially taken using beta coefficients instead of correlation coefficients. Beta coefficients are regression coefficients converted into effect sizes (*r*) considering Peterson and Brown’s (2005) methodology. If the correlation value is not provided, it can be calculated using the following formula, where *r* is the correlation, $\beta$ is the betacoefficient, and 0.05 represents the *p*-value.

$$r = \beta + 0.05\delta \tag{1}$$
where $(\delta)$ represents signilue: if $\beta$ is positive, $\delta = 1$ and if $\beta$ is negative, $\delta = 0$.

For studies in which $\beta$ was not provided or was more significant than 1, it was directly considered for effect size (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). The effect of ESG disclosure was examined using different measures of firm performance; for example, Chauhan and Kumar (2019) and Alareeni & Hamdan (2020) reported the effects of ESG disclosure on ROA, ROE, Tobin’s *Q*, and CoE (Fig. 3). Therefore, coding was performed for profitability measures. Different accounting measures were considered as dependent variables for the purposes of sensitivity analysis.

Next, we performed moderator analysis. Two potential moderators were identified from the literature: firm size and visibility. Within firm size, four subgroups were created, and studies were coded based on the presence of parameters in these subgroups. Similarly, three subgroups of visibility were created. Studies were coded based on the presence of parameters in these subgroups.

Furthermore, the effects of control variables (ownership structure, capital expenditure, sales growth, risk, firm age, publication quality, and ESG data source) in explaining the dynamic relationship between ESG disclosure and CFP were recorded through the effect size measure. Finally, we coded the general features of each study, such as the quality of the publication journal and sources of data collection. Studies were ranked in four subgroups based on publication quality and six subgroups based on data source quality.

To avoid bias due to coding errors, the protocol suggested by Lipsey and Wilson (2001) was adopted. Agreement between two coders was determined at the full-text screening stage using the Kappa ($k = 0.817, p < 0.01$) measure (Cohen, 1960).

### Data Source and Publication Quality: Sensitivity Analysis

#### Data Source Analysis
The studies included in the meta-analysis used data from Bloomberg; Thomson Reuters; the Kinder, Lydenberg, and Domini Company database (KLD); the STATS database; content analysis; and stock exchanges. The Bloomberg database uses companies’ annual and sustainability reports, third-party research, and press releases (Nollet et al., 2016; Albitar & Gerged, 2020; Manita et al., 2018). The Thomson Reuters database reports 250 indicators classified into 18 categories under four dimensions, namely, economic, environmental, social, and governance measures, and scores a firm’s ESG performance on a scale of 1 to 100 (Ahmad et al. 2021). The KLD and STATS databases list positive and negative ESG performance indicators only for companies listed on stock exchanges. ESG scores in these databases are binary; a firm receives a value of 1 for a performance indicator if the associated assessment criteria are fulfilled and 0 otherwise (Brogi & Lagasio 2018; Servaes et al. 2013). Several studies performed content analysis of annual reports, such as Gerged et al. (2020) and Li et al. (2018). An advantage of content analysis is that a separate disclosure index can be created for each dimension of ESG according to the most helpful performance initiatives. In studies that used the survey method, a questionnaire was sent to chief executive officers, product and general managers, and R&D managers to capture their companies’ processes and strategies for CSR implementation. Companies included in these surveys were assessed for ESG disclosures (Wahba & Elsayed 2015; Aboud & Diab, 2018, 2019). Given this diversity of data sources, it is imperative to consider the effect of different approaches to ESG disclosure on the baseline results by performing a sensitivity analysis as per Huang et al. (2020).

#### Publication Quality Analysis
The empirical papers in Table 2 were either published in top-tier journals or in journals that may not be indexed. To account for publication bias, we characterized journals according to the ABDC indexing system, which ranks academic publications into four rating categories based on quality: A*, A, B, and C. The research studies included in our sample were either published in ABDC-listed journals or not. We conducted a sensitivity analysis to consider the effect of differences in publication quality on the baseline results following Bilal et al. (2018).

### Meta‑analysis Procedure
We chose the meta-analysis procedure adopted by Habib (2012) to perform the main effect testing (H01/H02), followed by the moderator and sensitivity analyses, as shown in Fig. 3. The first step was to assess the effect size between ESG disclosure and firm performance, which served as our baseline result. The effect size reflects the nature and magnitude of the relationship between two variables. The weighted average effect size was computed considering the sample size and reliability of independent variables *r* (Geyskens et al., 2009). One effect size was adopted per study. If a study reported more than one effect size, then the effect sizes were combined into a composite *r*, following Hunter and Schmidt (2004). Similarly, effect sizes were calculated for each category of overall ESG disclosure, i.e., environmental, social, and disclosure scores, and the different categories of environmental, social, and disclosure scores.

In the moderator analysis, the original effect size in the selected studies was employed for each of the two moderators, firm size and visibility, and their sub-groups, namely, total assets, total sales, number of employees, and market capitalization for firm size and analysts’ coverage, liquidity, and institutional shareholders for visibility. For the overall meta-analysis results, we employed one effect size per study. This strategy maintained the independence between observations as noted by Bilal et al. (2018); Hunter and Schmidt (2004); and Garcia et al. (2017). For the sub-group analysis, the original effect size was obtained for each firm performance matrix. After computing the effect size, the standard error (SE) was estimated as:

$$SEs = \frac{ES}{\sqrt{\sum ES * N}} \tag{2}$$

where *ES* is the effect size and *N* is the sample size. To test the hypotheses, the Z-test was computed as follows:

$$Z = \frac{\text{Mean ES}}{SE} \tag{3}$$

where *Z* is the *z*-statistic, mean *ES* is the average effect size in the study, and *SE* is the standard error for all studies. The *Z* values were compared at significance levels of 1%, 5%, and 10%.

After the moderator analyses, sensitivity analyses of the different measures of the study’s dependent, control, and bias variables were performed. To analyze the biases of the different variables, sensitivity analyses of the financial performance and control variables and publication biases were performed.

Study heterogeneity was further statistically checked with a *Q*-test. A “fail-safe number” was calculated as suggested by Rosenthal (1991) to address the publication bias inherent in meta-analyses. This problem is also called the “file drawer problem” and refers to the identification of an appropriate number of studies with significant results. If the relationship between the dependent and independent variables is significant, omitting relevant studies might generate results that contradict the findings from the current set of papers. This problem is solved by calculating the fail-safe number as described by Zaremohzzabieh et al. (2021) and Bilal et al. (2018). This number represents the number of additional studies with non-significant results that must be included to change the significance of the findings (Rosenberg 2005) and is calculated using the following set of procedures. First, the *z*-statistics are obtained and combined to get the unweighted *z*-statistic:

$$U_wZ = \frac{\sum z}{\sqrt{N}} \tag{4}$$

where *z* denotes converted *z*-statistics and *N* denotes the number of studies in the meta-analysis. Finally, the fail-safe number is obtained:

$$N_f = \left( s * \left( \frac{s * z^2 - 2.706}{2.706} \right) \right) \tag{5}$$

where *s* represents the number of studies and the combined *z*-value obtained from Eq. 4. Publication bias can be excluded only when $N_f$ exceeds the critical value (CV), i.e.,

$$CV = (5 * s) + 10 \tag{6}$$

## Sensitivity Analysis

### Sensitivity to Different CFP Measures
Table 6 presents the sensitivity analysis results. Only a few studies using WACC as a proxy for cost of capital were available; therefore, the effects of all ESG dimensions on firm performance measured by WACC were insignificant. However, the other two proxies of cost of capital were significantly and negatively affected by all ESG dimensions, except insignificant effects of social disclosure and ESG disclosure on CoE. The effect of governance disclosure was negative and significant for both CoE and CoD. Comparing the three proxies, CoD offered the best results regarding heterogeneity and significant effect size. Findings show that ESG disclosure improves firm performance by lowering the cost of capital, especially the cost of debt (CoD). While WACC provided limited evidence of this relationship, CoD emerged as the most reliable proxy, indicating a clear and substantial link. Align with baseline findings, the study highlights that ESG disclosure contributes to improved firm performance by reducing the cost of debt and equity, thus reinforcing its overall positive impact.

ESG disclosure improves firm performance by increasing key accounting metrics like ROA, ROE, and EPS. The significant impact of social, governance, and overall ESG disclosures on these metrics demonstrates their importance in driving financial success. Social and ESG disclosures increased EPS, indicating value creation for shareholders, whereas governance and ESG disclosures significantly influenced ROA, demonstrating their contribution to operational efficiency. ROE emerged as the most reliable of these metrics, reducing heterogeneity bias and reinforcing the consistent positive relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance.

Many studies have used Tobin’s *Q* as a measure of firm performance, and all ESG dimensions positively and significantly influenced Tobin’s *Q*. The effect sizes of Tobin’s *Q* on environmental and governance disclosures were more pronounced than those on social and ESG disclosures. However, all models had substantial publication bias when the effect of the firm performance was separately measured using Tobin’s *Q*. Regardless of this bias, the findings confirm that ESG disclosures improve firm performance by increasing market valuation, particularly through environmental and governance disclosures.

### Sensitivity to Different Control Variables
Table 7 presents the effects of different control variables used in the literature on the relationship between ESG disclosure and CFP. Ownership structure negatively influenced firm performance when interacting with the environmental and governance disclosure dimensions. This implies that, in some cases, ownership structure may undermine the positive effects of environmental and governance disclosures on firm performance. R&D intensity significantly and positively influenced the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance. Reiterating that innovation and research efforts improve the positive effects of ESG practices. This alignment demonstrates that firms with higher R&D intensity can better capitalize on the benefits of ESG disclosure, resulting in improved performance. The findings indicate that R&D intensity acts as a catalyst, enhancing the impact of ESG disclosure on firm performance by encouraging innovation and efficiency in sustainable practices. The relationship between governance disclosure and firm performance was positively impacted by both capex and sales growth, suggesting that the positive effects of governance disclosures on financial performance are amplified by investments in capital expenditures and sales growth. These factors, however, had a negative impact on the relationship between ESG disclosure and financial performance, indicating that although ESG practices that are focused on governance have a positive impact, more extensive ESG disclosures may not always result in immediate financial gains. This demonstrates how the effects of ESG disclosure on business performance can vary, with some aspects (such as governance) having a more significant and immediate influence on financial results than others (Table 8).

Financial leverage positively and significantly influenced the relationships of all ESG disclosure dimensions with firm performance. Reinforcing the notion that companies with greater leverage are better positioned to benefit from ESG practices. According to the findings, financial leverage enhances the impact of ESG disclosures on firm performance by increasing capital access and investor confidence. The effect size of financial leverage was substantial for the environmental dimension. Beta and audited financial reports did not significantly influence the relationship between ESG disclosure dimensions and firm performance. GDP significantly and negatively influenced the relationships of environmental, social, and governance disclosures with firm performance. Firm age significantly and negatively influenced the relationships of social and ESG disclosures with firm performance. Firm age had a significant and negative impact on the relationships between social and ESG disclosures and firm performance, implying that older firms may struggle to use these disclosures to improve performance. This could be because of established operational practices or a reluctance to implement new sustainability strategies. Despite this, the overall impact of ESG disclosures on firm performance remains positive, with the effectiveness of social and broader ESG disclosures possibly being less pronounced for older firms.

## Discussion

The present study performs a meta-analysis to explain the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance by analyzing the combined effect of ESG disclosure and the individual effects of environmental, social, and governance disclosures.

The first hypothesis states that ESG disclosures have a positive effect on firm performance (H1). The results also show a significant positive influence of ESG performance on firm performance. It was found that businesses that prioritize environmental, social, and governance aspects tend to achieve better financial outcomes. This is in line with the findings of the previous studies (Hamrouni et al., 2015; Nekhili et al., 2017; Brogi & Lagasio 2018). Moving ahead of the second hypothesis which states that environmental disclosures have a positive effect on firm performance (H2). The result shows a positive results as the customers appreciate those firms that disclose their environmental practices. Environmental disclosure has evolved into a symbol of corporate transparency and a driver of corporate reputation rather than a factor eroding enterprises’ competitive edge.

The third hypothesis states that (a) carbon emission reductions and (b) climate change policy initiatives have a positive effect on firm performance (H3). The result shows that carbon compliance policy and climate change policy initiatives had a positive impact on firm performance. According to Hussain et al. (2018), carbon emission reduction initiatives and climate change policy initiatives do not change investors’ perceptions of the firm and do not become valuable unless they are implemented as part of climate change policy. Also, fourth hypothesis states that social disclosure has a positive effect on firm performance (H4). This can be due to the fact that comprehensive and transparent social disclosures boost stakeholder trust, improve corporate reputation, and enhance financial performance.

Moving to the fifth hypothesis, it states that (a) human rights policy and (b) fair remuneration policy initiatives have a positive effect on firm performance (H5). The results of the study also give the same results. This can be due to the fact that companies’ involvement in defending human rights and fair remuneration sends a favorable message to the market about the ideal corporate culture and working environment, enhancing their reputation. This goes in line with the previous studies (Bhaskaran et al., 2019).

The next hypothesis is that governance disclosures have a positive effect on firm performance (H6). This hypothesis is not supported by the study. The seventh hypothesis states that (a) board independence and (b) board diversity have a positive effect on firm performance (H7). This hypothesis is not supported by the study. The reason for this is that governance consists of several pillars, including board diversity or board independence. This negatively influenced firm performance, as external members may distort company culture and may be too passive in their risk-taking approach, reducing corporate profitability (Arayssi & Jizi, 2018). Also, the board diversity has different opinions. This might create different viewpoint.

Moving to the eighth hypothesis, it states that ESG disclosure is negatively related to different measures of the cost of capital (H8). The hypothesis is not supported by our study. This is because better environmental disclosure informs investors about future risks and opportunities, which may be reflected in market value. Furthermore, environmental disclosure negatively influenced CoE and CoD, as greater disclosures allow firms to enjoy legitimacy from institutional actors and ease of access to capital at lower CoE or CoD (Dhaliwal et al., 2011).

The ninth hypothesis states that ESG disclosure is positively related to different measures of profitability, namely, ROA and ROE (H9). This hypothesis is supported by our study. Firm disclosures related to equity of pay, fair trade, consumer welfare, and social commitment practices lead to the retention of quality employees and enhanced productivity (Cormier & Magnan, 2013; Lokuwaduge & Heenetigala 2017). The influence of social disclosure was significantly higher for accounting measures, particularly ROA, as firms that focus on building trust among stakeholders lower their operational costs (lower employee retrenchment) and minimize distributional conflicts, resulting in efficient utilization of assets.

ESG disclosure is positively related to market value (H10). The results also state the same thing. Better corporate governance may lower the cost of supervision thereby increasing the market value of the company.

Firm size moderates the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance (H11). Firm size positively moderated the relationship between ESG disclosure and CFP for all three measures (Table 6), affirming the findings of Wang et al. (2020) and Youn et al. (2015). Visibility and liquidity moderate the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance (H12). This result suggests that the presence of institutional shareholders makes it imperative for firms to disclose all information, whether environmental or not. Visibility and liquidity moderated the relationship between governance disclosure and firm performance (Wang et al., 2020; Dhaliwal et al., 2011).

ESG disclosure affects firm performance through different control variables (H13). This hypothesis is supported by our study. Firms with higher R&D intensity and leverage had larger effects of ESG disclosure dimensions on CFP, whereas Capex, sales growth, firm age, data sources, and GDP negatively influenced the effects of ESG disclosure dimensions on CFP. This suggests that firms that are innovative and highly leveraged may use ESG disclosures to boost stakeholder trust and achieve better financial results.

## Conclusion, Implications and Future Research Scope

Integrating ESG factors into corporate reporting aligns with the ethical imperatives of sustainable and responsible business practices and supports superior financial performance. A positive impact of ESG disclosures on firm performance is evident. However, the extent of this impact varies depending on the industry, geographic region, and specific metrics used.

Based on this meta-analysis, we conclude that ESG disclosure is positively related to firm performance and that environmental and social disclosures significantly affect firm performance. Carbon compliance policy and human rights activities significantly influence investors’ market perceptions, stimulating firm performance. Of the two moderators, firm size as measured by total assets negatively moderates the relationships of governance and social disclosures with firm performance. The presence of institutional shareholders strengthens the relationship between environmental disclosure and firm performance. Firms with lower liquidity have improved firm performance when they have high governance disclosure. Of the several controls incorporated in our analysis, ownership structure, Capex, sales growth, and firm age negatively influenced the relationship between ESG disclosure and firm performance. We also investigated the effects of publication quality and different data sources on ESG performance to evaluate the robustness of the baseline results.

Our study of the relationship of ESG disclosures with firm performance incorporates new evolving dimensions and focuses on social and entrepreneurial phenomena, thereby providing useful insights and realistic implications for key stakeholders, including business managers, industry practitioners, market regulators, investors, academic scholars, and society. The findings suggest that firms that make ESG disclosures have better management of assets and employees, boosting company reputation and, consequently, firm performance. Sustainability practices may be perceived as a continuous commitment that enhances profitability, the life and quality of the labor force, and respect for human rights, leading to robust community development (Rajnoha & Lesníková, 2016). By adopting adequate environmental safeguards, firms can enhance the quality of human and natural resources. Companies must adopt a broader perspective on sustainability goals rather than looking for short-term market and accounting-based measures (Ullah & Nasim, 2021). According to the literature, while environmental disclosures positively impact firm performance, governance disclosures significantly influence external financers’ required rate of return. These results imply that managers must align environmental and governance disclosures to boost firm value. Managers should consider decentralizing such reporting practices at every level. Concerning regulatory aspects, the findings reveal that institutional shareholding enhances disclosure and firm performance. Firms perform well when institutional pressures are sufficiently high to overwhelm opinions against mandatory disclosure. Regulators must adopt mandatory measures to increase transparency and, in turn, the efficiency and resilience of financial markets. The findings of this study also suggest that companies should formulate risk management portfolios and create new databases focused on separate ESG activities.

By capturing the outcomes of ESG disclosures through three different sub-analyses, this meta-analysis makes a significant contribution to the field. This study covered a sample of large firms listed on their respective countries’ stock exchanges. These companies implement ESG activities to increase awareness or because it is required by law. Future work could examine small and medium enterprises (SMEs), in which ESG disclosure is still nascent due to a lack of awareness and integrated business models and organizational structures (Russo & Tencati, 2009). Delving into the ESG disclosure–firm performance relationship in the context of SMEs could provide insights for reducing information asymmetry and implications for business sustainability. In addition, future studies could compare the impact of ESG disclosures on the performance of firms operating in developed or emerging countries.
