### ABSTRACT
This study examines how sustainability performance affects the cost of debt financing in the hospitality and tourism industry and whether institutional environment quality moderates this relationship. Using a dataset of 2302 firm-year observations from 34 countries between 2010 and 2022, the results show that sustainability performance is negatively associated with the cost of debt financing, indicating that sustainability initiatives reduce lender-perceived risk. Notably, institutional environment quality strengthens this relationship, as countries with stronger governance frameworks and regulatory enforcement enable lenders to reward sustainability commitments more effectively. However, the association weakened during the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting how systemic uncertainty altered lender risk perceptions and curtailed the financial benefits of sustainability practices. The findings remain robust across alternative measures, different model specifications, and controls for endogeneity. The findings provide new cross-country evidence on how firm-level sustainability initiatives interact with institutional contexts to shape debt financing costs in the hospitality and tourism industry. This study extends neo-institutional and stakeholder theories by demonstrating how sustainability practices and national governance jointly influence the cost of debt. It also offers critical insights for managers, lenders, and policymakers seeking to align sustainability strategies with financial resilience.

## 1 | Introduction

In recent years, sustainability performance (SUSP) has gained increasing prominence in corporate decision-making, with firms facing growing pressure from stakeholders to integrate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors into their strategies (Global Witness 2021; Moneva et al. 2020). This shift is particularly significant in the hospitality and tourism industry, where businesses directly interact with natural ecosystems, local communities, and cultural heritage. Recognizing this, the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly Target 8.9, call for sustainable tourism policies as a pathway to promoting employment, cultural preservation, and environmental protection by 2030.

Hospitality and tourism, therefore, represent an especially salient case: the sector not only contributes significantly to global development but also faces acute sustainability challenges. In 2023, it accounted for 9.1% of global GDP and created around 27 million jobs, signaling a strong postpandemic recovery (WTTC 2024). However, these benefits are accompanied by significant environmental and social costs, including high carbon emissions, pollution, and ecosystem degradation (Davenport and Davenport 2006; Moneva et al. 2020). Unlike other environmentally sensitive industries such as energy or manufacturing, hospitality and tourism firms are both capital-intensive and highly visible to stakeholders, including local communities, regulators, and international customers. This visibility intensifies reputational and legitimacy pressures, making SUSP particularly important in shaping perceptions of risk and access to financing (Zhang et al. 2025).

This heightened visibility extends beyond reputational concerns to financial outcomes, since firms' credibility in sustainability can directly influence access to debt financing. The hospitality and tourism sector relies heavily on debt due to high fixed costs, seasonal demand fluctuations, and dependence on discretionary consumer spending (Ashraf et al. 2025; Yeh 2018). As a result, the cost of debt (COD) financing becomes a central determinant of resilience and competitiveness, particularly as firms adopt sustainability strategies to mitigate risks, strengthen resilience, and improve financial stability (Legendre et al. 2024). Moreover, vulnerability to external shocks, such as geopolitical disruptions and the COVID-19 pandemic, further highlights the critical role of debt financing in corporate survival and resilience (Gössling et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2024).

The growing literature on the financial implications of sustainability focuses primarily on firm performance (Hoyoung et al. 2025; Kim and Lee 2020; Velte 2017), stock returns (Liu et al. 2021), corporate reputation (Nardella et al. 2020), and the cost of equity (Salvi et al. 2024). However, the COD has received limited attention, even though it is critical for capital-intensive sectors such as hospitality and tourism. At the same time, investors and lenders are increasingly integrating ESG considerations into credit risk assessments, as demonstrated by the rapid growth of sustainability-linked bonds and green loans (Flammer 2021). Global sustainable debt issuances exceeded $1.6 trillion in 2021, driving the total market beyond $4 trillion (BloombergNEF 2022). The empirical evidence on the relationship between ESG practices and the COD remains inconclusive. While some studies report that stronger SUSP is associated with a lower COD (Apergis et al. 2022; Eliwa et al. 2021), others find no significant effect (Flammer 2021; Gracia and Siregar 2021; Hoepner et al. 2016). These inconsistencies may reflect industry-specific risk exposures, financial structures, and institutional contexts. Such issues are particularly pronounced in hospitality and tourism, where sector-specific conditions such as seasonality, demand sensitivity, and exposure to external shocks create financing dynamics that differ from those in other industries.

Beyond these sector-specific characteristics, the institutional environment in which firms operate may also shape how sustainability practices affect financing outcomes, particularly the COD. Previous studies (see, e.g., Eliwa et al. 2021; Houqe et al. 2020) indicate that national factors such as regulatory quality, enforcement mechanisms, legal traditions, and stakeholder orientation influence the extent to which financial markets reward sustainability practices. To build on this evidence, the present study employs the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI), a widely used framework that captures six dimensions of governance: voice and accountability, political stability, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption (Kaufmann et al. 2011). Unlike narrower governance proxies, the WGI facilitates systematic cross-country comparison and captures the broader institutional context in which firms operate (Allam et al. 2023; Moussa et al. 2022). Moreover, prior studies have often focused on single countries or regions, which limit the generalizability of their findings. Accordingly, this study addresses this gap by systematically examining how variations in institutional environment quality (IEQ), as measured by the WGI, moderate the relationship between SUSP and the COD financing in hospitality and tourism firms across multiple countries.

The COVID-19 pandemic further exposed the vulnerability of the hospitality and tourism sector to external shocks, with lockdowns and travel restrictions driving severe declines in demand and increasing firms' reliance on debt financing (Gössling et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2024). These disruptions raise critical questions about whether SUSP can mitigate financial distress by lowering the COD financing during crises. However, existing research provides little systematic evidence on how the sustainability–COD financing relationship varies across crisis and non-crisis periods. This lack of evidence limits our understanding of the resilience benefits of sustainability and constrains both corporate and policy-level responses to future crises (Ashraf et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2024; Yeon et al. 2021).

This study seeks to fill these gaps by investigating the impact of SUSP on the COD and the moderating role of (IEQ) in the global hospitality and tourism sector. Drawing on neo-institutional theory, we conceptualize sustainability as both an efficiency-enhancing practice that reduces risk and a legitimacy-seeking response to institutional pressures. Stakeholder theory further highlights how sustainability fosters ethical alignment and trust with lenders, thereby lowering financing costs. Based on 2302 firm-year observations from listed hospitality and tourism firms across 34 countries between 2010 and 2022, this study provides large-scale evidence on the interplay between corporate sustainability and institutional environments in shaping the COD financing.

This study makes several key contributions to the existing literature. First, it provides new empirical evidence on the relationship between SUSP and the COD in hospitality and tourism firms. While most studies focus on manufacturing or general services (Apergis et al. 2022; Eliwa et al. 2021), research on this sector remains limited despite its capital-intensive nature and environmental sensitivity. Second, it extends prior research by examining how IEQ moderates the relationship between SUSP and financing costs. Earlier studies typically emphasize individual institutional features or single-country settings (Eliwa et al. 2021; Houqe et al. 2020). Employing the WGI, this research offers one of the first cross-country analyses of how broader governance systems influence the financial benefits of sustainability in the hospitality and tourism sector. Third, it contributes to the sustainability–finance literature by analyzing a large international dataset of 2302 firm-year observations across 34 countries between 2010 and 2022. While earlier studies often focus on single-country settings (Ali et al. 2023; Apergis et al. 2022; Velte 2017) or regional samples (Eliwa et al. 2021; Gracia and Siregar 2021), cross-country evidence in this sector remains scarce. Spanning a long and recent time horizon, this study captures both long-term patterns and the effects of systemic shocks such as COVID-19, offering timely insights into the resilience of sustainable practices.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents the theoretical framework, Section 3 reviews the literature and develops hypotheses, Section 4 outlines the research methodology, Section 5 reports and discusses the findings, and Section 6 concludes the study.

## 2 | Theoretical Framework

This study adopts a multitheoretical framework that integrates neo-institutional theory and stakeholder theory to explain how SUSP influences a firm's COD, with a particular emphasis on the moderating role of IEQ. These theoretical perspectives provide a strong foundation for understanding why and how sustainability initiatives matter financially, especially in the hospitality and tourism sector, where firms operate under close regulatory scrutiny and strong societal expectations.

Neo-institutional theory posits that firms operate within environments structured by both formal rules and informal norms, which shape strategic decisions and organizational behavior (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Scott 2014). From an efficiency perspective, strong SUSP reflects disciplined management, reduced risk exposure, and efficient resource allocation. These characteristics lower lenders' concerns about default and signal long-term operational viability. In hospitality and tourism, where regulatory and stakeholder scrutiny is intense, sustainability initiatives directly address lenders' priorities by minimizing financial and operational risks (de Grosbois and Fennell 2022; Ibrahim et al. 2023; Khalaf et al. 2024).

A legitimization perspective within neo-institutional theory highlights how firms adopt sustainability practices to meet regulatory expectations and societal norms (e.g., Benamraoui et al. 2023; Judge et al. 2008; Scott 2014). In hospitality and tourism, legitimacy is crucial due to firms' reliance on environmental integrity, cultural preservation, and strong community relations. Actions such as achieving ecocertifications, engaging in conservation initiatives, and supporting local communities indicate alignment with stakeholder values. These visible commitments help build reputational capital, reduce information asymmetry, and improve lender trust, which can translate into reduced COD financing (Ibrahim et al. 2023; Masiero et al. 2023). Moreover, institutional pressures often result in firms mimicking peers who are perceived as legitimate and successful, further driving the adoption of sustainability practices across the industry (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Elamer et al. 2020a).

Stakeholder theory complements neo-institutional theory by emphasizing that firms depend on robust, trust-based relationships with diverse stakeholders to achieve sustained success (Elmarzouky et al. 2025; Freeman 1984; Jones 1995). This study draws on both normative and instrumental perspectives of stakeholder theory to articulate how stakeholder relationships shape the financial outcomes of SUSP. In the hospitality and tourism industry, stakeholders, including tourists, employees, investors, and local communities, actively influence firms' sustainability decisions.

The normative perspective posits that firms have ethical responsibilities to address stakeholders' needs beyond shareholder interests (Hillman and Keim 2001; Khalaf et al. 2024). For hospitality and tourism firms, ethical obligations include labor rights, environmental protection, and community engagement, which reduce reputational and regulatory risks. These commitments may further enable firms to access favorable financial instruments, such as sustainability-linked loans, reducing their COD (Hoepner et al. 2016).

The instrumental view regards stakeholder engagement as a strategic tool for enhancing competitive advantage, long-term performance, and financial reputation (Freeman 1984; Jones 1995). Firms that respond effectively to the interests of tourists, employees, investors, and communities are more likely to earn stakeholder loyalty, reduce operational disruptions, and present a favorable profile to lenders. These benefits make them more attractive to lenders and can lead to improved borrowing conditions (Jo and Harjoto 2011; Masiero et al. 2023). Strong stakeholder relationships also enhance the firm's resilience in volatile environments and improve access to critical resources, supporting financial stability over time (Moneva et al. 2020).

Taken together, neo-institutional and stakeholder theories suggest that SUSP is both a strategic asset and a normative imperative. In the hospitality and tourism industry, where firms are highly visible and their operations are closely tied to sensitive environmental and social issues, the implications of these theories are particularly pronounced. Neo-institutional theory explains how external institutional forces compel firms to adopt sustainability for legitimacy and risk reduction, while stakeholder theory explains the mechanisms through which stakeholder alignment contributes to financial trust and improved borrowing terms. The integration of these perspectives provides a strong theoretical basis for understanding how firm-level SUSP affects the COD and highlights the importance of institutional context in shaping this relationship (de Grosbois and Fennell 2022).

## 3 | Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

### 3.1 | SUSP and the COD

The COD reflects lenders' assessment of a firm's risk profile and financial reliability (Goss and Roberts 2011). In recent years, SUSP, typically evaluated through ESG criteria, has emerged as a crucial factor in credit risk assessment. This trend is particularly relevant in industries such as hospitality and tourism, where firms face elevated exposure to environmental, social, and reputational risks (Masiero et al. 2023).

Neo-institutional theory argues that firms adopt sustainability practices to gain legitimacy by aligning with formal regulatory frameworks and informal societal expectations, in addition to achieving operational efficiency (e.g., DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Scott 2014). By demonstrating alignment with these institutional pressures, firms send credible signals of lower risk, improving their perceived creditworthiness and potentially reducing the COD financing (Ibrahim et al. 2023; Masiero et al. 2023).

Complementing this perspective, stakeholder theory emphasizes that effective engagement in sustainability fosters stronger relationships with critical stakeholders, including lenders, regulators, and local communities, by enhancing transparency, trust, and corporate reputation (Freeman 1984; Jo and Harjoto 2011). Such proactive engagement mitigates reputational and litigation risks, reduces information asymmetry, and signals responsible management practices, all of which are positively regarded by lenders. Consequently, corporate sustainability serves as a credible signal of long-term stability and resilience, which lenders are likely to reward through a lower COD (Masiero et al. 2023; Theodoulidis et al. 2017).

Empirical studies provide substantial support for these theoretical predictions. Izzo and Magnanelli (2012) report that firms exhibiting superior environmental performance tend to enjoy lower debt costs, as they are perceived to better manage long-term environmental risks. Similarly, Jo and Harjoto (2011) reveal that strong CSR initiatives significantly strengthen corporate governance structures and reduce information asymmetries, which can, in turn, lower financing costs. Chava (2014) further finds that poor environmental records are associated with higher costs of capital, indicating that lenders penalize weak sustainability commitments. Other empirical studies also consistently document a negative relationship between SUSP and debt costs, showing that enhanced sustainability can improve debt ratings and capital access (Crifo et al. 2017; La Rosa et al. 2018). Moreover, recent studies from Asian contexts highlight that firms with inclusive governance structures, such as gender-diverse boards, are more successful at leveraging ESG activities for financial benefits (Mansour et al. 2024; Saleh et al. 2025).

Nonetheless, the existing literature also highlights mixed results. For example, Erragragui (2018) and Hoepner et al. (2016) find that SUSP does not always lead to lower financing costs, and in some cases, it may have no significant effect. Gracia and Siregar (2021) similarly report that in ASEAN countries (Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand), SUSP does not consistently translate into reduced debt costs. Such disparities may be attributable to variations in measurement methodologies, industry-specific factors, or diverse institutional contexts.

Within the hospitality and tourism industry, which is fundamentally linked to environmental integrity and social perceptions, the relationship between SUSP and debt costs is particularly salient. Given this industry's heightened exposure to sustainability-related risks and stakeholder scrutiny, hospitality and tourism firms are especially incentivized to exhibit robust environmental stewardship and proactive community engagement (Masiero et al. 2023). Such sustainability efforts can enhance lender trust and mitigate perceived financial risk, leading to potentially lower costs of debt. Drawing on these theoretical perspectives and empirical evidence, specifically within the context of hospitality and tourism, the following hypothesis is developed:

**H1. There is a negative relationship between a firm's sustainability performance and its cost of debt.**

### 3.2 | Moderating Effect of IEQ

IEQ, encompassing the strength of regulatory frameworks, enforcement mechanisms, rule of law, and overall governance effectiveness, plays a crucial role in shaping corporate sustainability behavior and influencing financial outcomes such as the COD (e.g., Detotto et al. 2021; Elamer et al. 2020a; Houqe et al. 2020). In the context of the hospitality and tourism sector, where firms operate in close contact with environmental, social, and cultural sensitivities, IEQ becomes especially salient in determining how sustainability initiatives are perceived and rewarded by financial stakeholders.

Drawing from neo-institutional theory, IEQ sets the formal and informal institutional boundaries within which firms operate (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Elmarzouky et al. 2025; Scott 2014). High-IEQ environments offer stable legal systems, reliable enforcement, low corruption, and effective governance, all of which reduce institutional uncertainty for both firms and lenders. According to the efficiency perspective of neo-institutional theory, such environments facilitate more accurate risk assessment and create systemic checks against opportunism or misreporting. As a result, firms operating within high-IEQ countries are seen as less risky, leading lenders to offer a lower COD (Detotto et al. 2021; Houqe et al. 2020).

From the legitimization standpoint of neo-institutional theory, firms adopt sustainability practices not only for operational gains but also to secure legitimacy in the eyes of regulators, communities, and stakeholders (Benamraoui et al. 2023; Judge et al. 2010; Scott 2014). In countries where sustainability norms are institutionalized, firms are incentivized to mirror these expectations. Especially within the hospitality and tourism industries, which are heavily dependent on community goodwill, environmental preservation, and public perception, conformance with these expectations fosters trust and legitimacy. This conformance also leads to isomorphic behavior, as firms emulate peers perceived as legitimate and successful (Elamer et al. 2020a; Ibrahim et al. 2023; Khalaf et al. 2024).

Stakeholder theory complements these insights by highlighting the ethical and strategic dimensions of stakeholder engagement. In high-IEQ contexts, stakeholders, including regulators, local communities, investors, and consumers, expect firms to uphold responsible environmental and social practices. Firms that meet these expectations benefit not only from ethical alignment (normative perspective) but also from reduced reputational risk and enhanced access to financial resources (instrumental perspective) (e.g., de Grosbois and Fennell 2022; Freeman 1984; Hoepner et al. 2016; Jones 1995). High-IEQ environments also reinforce these dynamics by ensuring that stakeholder voices are reflected in institutional rules, thus amplifying their influence on firm strategy.

Empirical evidence suggests that IEQ significantly shapes corporate outcomes, including SUSP and borrowing costs. Dhaliwal et al. (2014) find that CSR disclosures are more effective in reducing the cost of capital in stakeholder-oriented countries. Eliwa et al. (2021) show that the negative relationship between ESG performance and COD is stronger in jurisdictions that are more stakeholder-oriented. Similarly, Houqe et al. (2020) show that in countries with robust investor protections and transparency standards, SUSP is more likely to translate into financial advantages. In contrast, weak institutional environments often fail to reward sustainability efforts. In such contexts, limited legal enforcement and corruption can erode stakeholder confidence in reported ESG performance, undermining its role as a credible risk-reducing signal. Garmaise and Liu (2005) show that corruption increases the cost of capital, while Daske et al. (2008) and Manganaris et al. (2015) highlight that benefits from regulatory initiatives are contingent on the strength of institutional enforcement.

In the hospitality and tourism industry, where firms rely on natural, cultural, and human resources, the relevance of IEQ is magnified. The industry's global reach and visibility mean that institutional credibility significantly affects how sustainability practices are evaluated. According to Skare et al. (2021) and the WTTC (2024), tourism accounted for one in four new jobs globally before the COVID-19 pandemic and contributed over 10% to global GDP, highlighting its economic and social significance. In this setting, firms are particularly exposed to reputational and regulatory risks. A strong IEQ framework not only mitigates these risks but ensures that credible sustainability commitments are rewarded by capital markets.

Based on the above theoretical arguments and empirical evidence, we contend that IEQ may enhance the benefits of firm-level sustainability practices by legitimizing such efforts, reducing information asymmetries, and reinforcing lender confidence. In high-IEQ settings, sustainability signals are more credible and materially relevant to financial stakeholders, thereby strengthening the negative relationship between SUSP and COD. Thus, we develop the following hypothesis:

**H2. Institutional environment quality moderates the relationship between firm-level sustainability performance and the cost of debt.**

## 4 | Research Design

### 4.1 | Data and Sample

We start our analysis with an initial sample consisting of 3328 firm-year observations, covering all globally listed firms in the Hospitality and Tourism industry with accessible data on SUSP between 2010 and 2022. The dataset comprises firms from six industries: airlines, hotels and motels, travel and tourism, casinos and gambling, restaurants and bars, and recreational services. Excluding 295 firm-year observations due to inadequate data for COD calculation and an additional 731 firm-year observations for missing control variable information, our empirical analysis proceeds with a final sample comprising 2302 firm-year observations across 34 countries.

Data on SUSP indicators for each firm-year are collected from the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) Workspace database (formerly Refinitiv Eikon), which is widely recognized for its comprehensive and systematically validated ESG indicators (Haque 2017). Data on COD and firm-specific financials were retrieved from Bloomberg and LSEG Workspace. IEQ and macroeconomic variables were extracted from the World Bank databases.

### 4.2 | Variables Measurement

#### 4.2.1 | SUSP Measures

In this study, SUSP is proxied by firms' ESG scores obtained from the LSEG Workspace database. The ESG score is a comprehensive index that evaluates a firm's achievements, commitments, and effectiveness in implementing sustainability practices. It captures performance across three dimensions: environmental sustainability (resource use, emissions reduction, and innovation), social sustainability (workforce relations, human rights, community involvement, and product responsibility), and governance sustainability (management practices, shareholder relations, and CSR initiatives). Both the overall ESG score and its subdimensions are measured on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher values reflecting stronger SUSP. Consistent with prior research, ESG scores are widely recognized as a reliable proxy for assessing corporate SUSP (e.g., Apergis et al. 2022; Eliwa et al. 2021; Moussa et al. 2020).

#### 4.2.2 | COD Measures

Our methodology employs two distinct measures of COD, the accounting-based measure and the annual weighted average cost of debt (WACOD), to examine the effect of firms' sustainable performance on their financing costs (e.g., Caragnano et al. 2020; Elamer et al. 2020b; Eliwa et al. 2021; Gracia and Siregar 2021). This dual approach enriches our analysis by capturing both the immediate and strategic financial impacts of sustainable initiatives. The accounting-based measure, calculating the ratio of interest expense to average net debt, offers immediate insights into the financial consequences of sustainable practices (Gracia and Siregar 2021). It reflects the direct impact of historical ESG actions on borrowing costs, serving to evaluate the short-term financial outcomes of sustainable performance. The WACOD, on the other hand, provides a forward-looking perspective by incorporating the cost of different types of debt and adjusting for tax effects. This measure captures the broader, strategic implications of sustainability initiatives on a firm's financing strategy and overall COD (e.g., Caragnano et al. 2020; Raimo et al. 2021). This measure is calculated as follows:

$$WACOD = \left\{ \left( \frac{SD}{TD} \right) \times (CS \times AF) + \left( \frac{LD}{TD} \right) \times (CL \times AF) \right\} \times \{1 - TR\} \tag{1}$$

where WACOD serves as an indicator of the cost of debt; SD represents short-term debt; TD is total debt; CS is the pre-tax cost of short-term debt; AF stands for the debt adjustment factor; LD indicates long-term debt; CL is the pre-tax cost of long-term debt; and TR is the effective tax rate.

Utilizing both measures allows for a comprehensive analysis of the SUSP-COD relationship. While the accounting-based measure focuses on the direct, short-term effects, WACOD explores the long-term strategic benefits of sustainability initiatives. This comprehensive approach ensures that our analysis captures both the direct and indirect effects of sustainable performance on the COD.

#### 4.2.3 | IEQ

To measure IEQ, we employed the WGI developed by the World Bank, which provides a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of country-level governance quality (Kaufmann et al. 2011). These indicators consist of six key dimensions: “voice and accountability (VA), political stability and absence of violence/terrorism (PS), government effectiveness (GE), regulatory quality (RQ), rule of law (RL), and control of corruption (CC).” The IEQ¹ score is measured on a scale from −2.5 to +2.5, where a higher value indicates stronger institutional quality.

The selection of the WGI is justified by its widespread use in academic research and its ability to capture multiple dimensions of country-level governance (e.g., Daniel et al. 2012; Kaufmann et al. 2011). The indicators offer global comparability across over 200 countries, making them particularly suitable for our study. Unlike narrower governance metrics, such as Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index, the WGI provides a holistic view of governance, encompassing political, regulatory, and legal dimensions critical for examining the institutional environment's role in shaping firms' SUSP and financial outcomes. Moreover, prior research has validated the reliability and validity of these indicators, demonstrating their robustness for analyzing IEQ (Kaufmann et al. 2011).

To ensure the validity and robustness of the IEQ indicators, we conducted multiple analyses. First, we performed a correlation analysis, as recommended by Hair et al. (2018). Appendix A displays the Pearson correlation matrix, which shows that all correlation coefficients are both positive and statistically significant. This suggests a high level of internal consistency and reliability for the IEQ indicators used in this study. These findings align with prior research (Allam et al. 2023; Elamer et al. 2020a), further supporting the robustness of the indicators.

Next, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to develop a composite measure that captures the six dimensions of IEQ (Allam et al. 2023). PCA was used to reduce data complexity and enhance interpretability by identifying the underlying structure of the IEQ indicators. To clarify the factor structure, we utilized varimax rotation, a standard technique that maximizes the variance of squared loadings and minimizes cross-loadings, ensuring the factors are well-differentiated. According to Hair et al. (2018), varimax rotation is particularly effective in simplifying complex datasets, making it an appropriate choice for this study.

Appendix B presents the PCA results, including eigenvectors and diagnostic measures. The analysis revealed that all six IEQ dimensions loaded strongly onto a single factor, accounting for 86.3% of the total variance. This high percentage of explained variance highlights the robustness of the composite measure in capturing the multidimensional aspects of IEQ. Moreover, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.872, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Cerny and Kaiser 1977), confirming the dataset's suitability for PCA. Finally, we created a composite IEQ index by calculating a weighted average of the factor loadings from the first principal component. This approach ensures that the composite measure accurately reflects the relative contribution of each dimension while maintaining the overall integrity and multidimensional nature of the IEQ construct. Although each individual WGI dimension ranges from −2.5 to +2.5, the composite index produced through PCA is a statistical factor score. As such, its values can extend beyond the original bounds due to the nature of linear combinations of standardized variables. This is a standard outcome of PCA-based index construction and reflects the statistical nature of factor scores, rather than the original indicator scale.

#### 4.2.4 | Control Variables

Following previous literature (e.g., Abdelfattah et al. 2021; Allam et al. 2024; Caragnano et al. 2020; Duong et al. 2024; Tantawy and Moussa 2023), this study incorporates several control variables to account for both firm-specific and country characteristics that may influence a firm's COD. These variables are categorized into two main groups: (a) firm characteristics, which include firm size (FSIZE), measured by the natural logarithm of a company's total assets, profitability (PROF), calculated as the net income to total equity ratio, leverage (LEV), defined as the total debt to total assets ratio, cash flow from operations (CFS) measured as the percentage of cash flow from operations to sales, capital expenditure (CAPX) measured as the percentage of total capital expenditure to total sales, interest coverage (INT_COV) calculated as net income before interest and taxes divided by the firm's interest expenses, and market-to book-value (MTBV), calculated by the market-to-book value ratio; and (b) country characteristics, which encompass GDP per capita (GDP) calculated as the natural logarithm of average income per individual (in current US dollars), and unemployment rate (UNEM) measured as unemployment as a percentage of the total labor force. All continuous variables are winsorised at the 1% level to mitigate the impact of outliers. Table 1 provides definitions for all the variables used in this study.

### 4.3 | Empirical Models

To investigate the main and joint effects of SUSP and IEQ on COD, a three-way fixed-effects model is used that accounts for variations across countries, industries, and years.² This approach not only addresses variations and hidden factors that could affect the results but also ensures robust inferences by controling for unobserved heterogeneity at multiple levels (Wooldridge 2010). Specifically, the inclusion of fixed effects for countries, industries, and years helps mitigate the influence of imbalanced panel data by accounting for systematic differences across groups. Robust standard errors are clustered at the firm level to correct for potential heteroskedasticity and within-firm correlation, thereby improving the reliability and accuracy of statistical inference. The fixed-effects model improves the accuracy and dependability of the estimated relationships by addressing challenges associated with omitted variables and unobserved heterogeneity (e.g., Gujarati 2009; Wooldridge 2010). To test our hypotheses, we develop the following empirical models:

$$COD_{it} = \alpha_0 + \beta_1 SUSP_{it} + \beta_2 FSIZE_{it} + \beta_3 PROF_{it} + \beta_4 LEV_{it} + \beta_5 CFS_{it} + \beta_6 CAPX_{it} + \beta_7 INT\_COV_{it} + \beta_8 MTBV_{it} + \beta_9 GDP_{it} + \beta_{10} UNEM_{it} + \beta_{11} Country_i + \beta_{12} Industry_i + \beta_{13} Year + \epsilon_{it} \tag{2}$$

$$COD_{it} = \alpha_0 + \beta_1 SUSP_{it} + \beta_2 IEQ_{it} + \beta_3 SUSP_{it} * IEQ_{it} + \beta_4 FSIZE_{it} + \beta_5 PROF_{it} + \beta_6 LEV_{it} + \beta_7 CFS_{it} + \beta_8 CAPX_{it} + \beta_9 INT\_COV_{it} + \beta_{10} MTBV_{it} + \beta_{11} GDP_{it} + \beta_{12} UNEM_{it} + \beta_{13} Country_i + \beta_{14} Industry_i + \beta_{15} Year + \epsilon_{it} \tag{3}$$

Equation (2) is employed to examine the effect of SUSP on the firm's COD, as outlined in Hypothesis 1. Equation (3) is used to test the moderating role of IEQ in the relationship between SUSP and the firm's COD, as proposed in Hypothesis 2.

### 5.2 | Multivariate Results

#### 5.2.1 | Effect of SUSP on the COD

Table 5 reports the fixed-effects regression results examining the impact of SUSP on the COD in hospitality and tourism firms. In Model 1, the coefficient for SUSP is negative and statistically significant (β = −1.160, p < 0.01), indicating that higher SUSP is associated with lower COD financing. To assess the economic significance, we calculate the effect of a one standard deviation increase in SUSP on COD. The result shows that a one standard deviation increase in SUSP leads to a decrease of approximately 0.238 percentage points in COD.³ Given the mean COD of 0.065, this reduction is economically meaningful, demonstrating the strong impact of SUSP on firms' financing costs.

In Models 4 to 6 of Table 5, which disaggregate SUSP into its environmental (EVNP), social (SOCP), and governance (GOVP) components, the results remain consistent. Specifically, EVNP is negatively and significantly related to COD (β=−1.090, p<0.01), as is SOCP (β=−1.320, p<0.01) and GOVP (β=−0.531, p<0.05). The economic effects are also significant: a one standard deviation increase in EVNP, SOCP, and GOVP reduces COD by approximately 0.319, 0.290, and 0.122 percentage points, respectively.

These results strongly support Hypothesis 1 and align with the efficiency perspective of neo-institutional theory, which posits that sustainability initiatives signal disciplined management, efficient resource use, and lower operational risk, thereby reducing lenders' perceived default risk (Crifo et al. 2017; de Grosbois and Fennell 2022; Ntim and Soobaroyen 2013). They also support the legitimisation perspective, especially in hospitality and tourism, where regulatory scrutiny and public expectations around environmental, social, and cultural responsibility are high; credible SUSP enhances legitimacy, builds reputational capital, and reduces information asymmetries (Ibrahim et al. 2023; Khalaf et al. 2024). From a stakeholder theory perspective, robust sustainability practices foster trust-based relationships with lenders, regulators, employees, and communities (Freeman 1984; Jones 1995), lowering reputational and litigation risks and improving financing conditions. The results extend prior evidence linking sustainability to reduced financing costs (Eliwa et al. 2021; Houqe et al. 2020; Masiero et al. 2023; Theodoulidis et al. 2017) to the hospitality and tourism sector, where sustainability is both a strategic priority and a competitive necessity.

This result suggests that country-level governance effectiveness, regulatory quality, and rule of law contribute directly to reducing the COD by lowering systemic uncertainty, enhancing transparency, and strengthening the credibility of corporate disclosures capital (Cheng et al. 2014; Houqe et al. 2020; Ibrahim et al. 2023).

These results provide strong support for Hypothesis 2 and align with the legitimisation perspective of neo-institutional theory, which posits that alignment with societal norms and expectations enhances organizational legitimacy and stakeholder trust (Judge et al. 2008; Scott 2014). In high-IEQ contexts, the credibility and informativeness of sustainability disclosures are enhanced, making them more influential in lenders' credit risk assessments. This is particularly relevant for hospitality and tourism firms, where operational visibility and dependence on natural and cultural resources lead to heightened scrutiny from regulators, lenders, and the public.

From a stakeholder theory perspective, strong institutional contexts embed stakeholder expectations in enforceable rules and industry standards (Elmarzouky et al. 2025; Freeman 1984; Jones 1995). Firms meeting these expectations benefit from reinforced legitimacy, reduced default risk, and more favorable credit terms. These findings align with prior evidence showing that the financial benefits of sustainability depend on the institutional environment, including in the tourism and hospitality sector (e.g., Dhaliwal et al. 2014; Eliwa et al. 2021; Houqe et al. 2020; Masiero et al. 2023). Overall, the interaction between firm-level sustainability and country-level institutional quality emerges as a key determinant of financing conditions in the hospitality and tourism sector.

## 5.3 | Additional Analyses

### 5.3.1 | Effect of Strong Versus Weak National Governance Environments

We now examine whether the moderating role of IEQ is concentrated in jurisdictions with stronger institutions. Prior studies (e.g., Benamraoui et al. 2023; Dhaliwal et al. 2014; Eliwa et al. 2021; Houqe et al. 2020; Scott 2014) suggest that stronger institutional environments enhance public and regulatory scrutiny, strengthen enforcement, and improve the credibility and informativeness of corporate disclosures, thereby increasing the value that lenders attach to sustainability signals. In hospitality and tourism, where firms operate in close contact with local communities and natural and cultural assets, these external pressures are especially salient in credit assessments (Ibrahim et al. 2023; Masiero et al. 2023). To empirically test this, the sample is split at the median IEQ value into strong national governance countries (above median) and weak national governance countries (below median). The baseline moderation model from Equation (3) is then estimated separately for each subsample.

The results in Table 6 show that, in strong national governance environments, the coefficient on SUSP is negative and significant, indicating that higher SUSP reduces the COD financing when institutional quality is high. IEQ also remains negatively and significantly associated with COD. Most importantly, the interaction between SUSP and IEQ is negative and significant, confirming that the COD benefit of sustainability is greater when legal enforcement is reliable, disclosure standards are credible, and corruption risk is low. By contrast, in weak national governance contexts, the magnitudes of these effects are smaller and generally statistically insignificant, suggesting that weaker institutions dilute the translation of sustainability signals into tangible financing advantages.

These results align with the neo-institutional perspective, which argues that stable, well-enforced rules reduce uncertainty and enhance the credibility of sustainability disclosures in lenders' eyes (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Scott 2014). They are also consistent with stakeholder theory, which posits that institutionalized stakeholder norms increase the salience of environmental and social commitments for lenders, reinforcing trust and lowering perceived default risk (Freeman 1984; Jones 1995). In hospitality and tourism, where operational visibility and community integration are high, legitimacy and credible disclosure become central to lender evaluations (Masiero et al. 2023). Overall, the subsample analysis strongly supports H2, highlighting that the financial benefits of SUSP are contingent on the institutional setting in which firms operate.

### 5.3.2 | Effect of the COVID-19 Pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the global tourism industry on an unprecedented scale, halting mobility and exposing structural vulnerabilities in tourism-related businesses (Gössling et al. 2021; Uğur and Akbıyık 2020). Unlike earlier crises, COVID-19 triggered simultaneous demand- and supply-side shocks: Lockdowns, travel restrictions, and public health measures caused revenues to collapse, operations to halt, and liquidity pressures to intensify. The World Travel and Tourism Council (WTTC 2024) estimates that the sector suffered a $5.5 trillion GDP loss in 2020, highlighting its vulnerability to shocks due to reliance on discretionary spending, international mobility, and cultural and environmental resources (Zhang et al. 2021). Previous research (Gössling et al. 2021; Uğur and Akbıyık 2020) highlights that the pandemic's effects were both immediate and prolonged, undermining cash flow, employment, and investor confidence. As an additional analysis, we examine whether the relationships between SUSP, IEQ, and COD changed during the COVID-19 crisis (2020–2022), allowing us to assess whether extreme disruption altered lenders' sensitivity to sustainability and institutional quality.

Table 7 reports results for prepandemic and pandemic subsamples. Before COVID-19, SUSP, and IEQ are negatively and significantly associated with COD, and their interaction is also significantly negative. This indicates that lenders rewarded firms combining strong SUSP with operations in high-quality institutional environments. This is broadly in line with neo-institutional theory, which argues that alignment with societal norms, credible disclosure practices, and adherence to regulatory expectations enhance legitimacy and reduce perceived risk (e.g., DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Houqe et al. 2020; Ibrahim et al. 2023). The results are also consistent with stakeholder theory, which holds that transparent engagement with stakeholders fosters trust and reduces perceived default risk (Freeman 1984; Masiero et al. 2023).

During the COVID-19 period, however, these relationships weaken and cease to be statistically significant, although the coefficients remain negative. This suggests a shift in lender priorities under extreme uncertainty: lenders placed greater emphasis on immediate cash flow stability and liquidity buffers, deprioritizing longer term sustainability considerations. This behavioral reorientation is consistent with Acharya and Steffen (2020), who show that systemic shocks increase risk aversion and shorten decision horizons, and with Lin et al. (2024) and Qiu et al. (2020), who find that ESG signals lose salience during crises as credit risk assessments focus on short-term survival metrics. Such a shift reflects heightened uncertainty, compressed decision horizons, and elevated liquidity risk in credit markets during COVID-19, raising questions about the durability of sustainability-linked financing benefits under severe macroeconomic disruptions.

Overall, the evidence suggests that COVID-19 served as a real-time stress test of the financial relevance of sustainability and governance. While their short-term effects on COD were dampened under crisis conditions, their underlying contribution to perceived resilience remained intact. This suggests that integrating sustainability into corporate strategy, particularly in high-quality institutional environments, can enhance long-term creditworthiness even when short-term market dynamics shift during systemic shocks.

### 5.4 | Robustness Checks

We perform several analyses, summarized in Table 8, to test the robustness of our findings. First, we examine whether our results are sensitive to the COD proxy employed. Accordingly, we re-estimate our main models using the annual WACOD as an alternative measure. The results, presented in Models 1 and 2 of Table 8, remain largely consistent with those reported in Table 5, suggesting that our findings are robust to different COD proxies.

Second, to mitigate potential endogeneity arising from simultaneity or reverse causality, we use 1-year lagged independent variables (Models 3–4 of Table 8). This ensures that SUSP and IEQ precede COD in time, thereby strengthening the causal interpretation of our results (Larcker and Rusticus 2010). The results, shown in Models 3 and 4 of Table 8, remain stable and consistent in both sign and significance.

Third, we apply a two-stage least squares (2SLS) instrumental variable approach to mitigate endogeneity concerns arising from omitted variables and simultaneity. This approach is widely used in research to mitigate the correlation between the error term and the independent variables, ensuring more reliable estimates (Wooldridge 2010). In line with prior studies, we employed two instrumental variables to perform the 2SLS regression: the average country-year SUSP (Houqe et al. 2020) and a binary variable indicating whether firms reported losses in the previous year (Eliwa et al. 2021). These instruments satisfy the relevance and exclusion restrictions necessary for addressing endogeneity risks. The 2SLS regression results from Models 5 and 6 in Table 8, consistent with those in Table 5, indicate that our findings are robust against biases from omitted variables. This further strengthens the reliability of our empirical analysis.

Fourth, we also estimate a dynamic panel model using the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) to account for more complex forms of endogeneity, including unobserved heterogeneity, simultaneity, and reverse causality (Arellano and Bond 1991; Wintoki et al. 2012). This approach uses lagged values of the dependent and independent variables to generate GMM-type instruments that are statistically valid and uncorrelated with the error term. Specifically, the first differences of all independent variables are used as standard instruments, with lags from the second period onwards applied in the differenced equations. We performed two postestimation analyses. The Sargan test confirmed the validity of overidentifying restrictions, while the Arellano–Bond test indicated no significant serial correlation in first-differenced errors at order two (Wintoki et al. 2012). These diagnostic tests validate the robustness of the GMM model.

The application of the GMM-based dynamic panel regression reduces inconsistencies in standard estimators caused by correlations between the lagged dependent variable and unobserved panel-level effects (Arellano and Bond 1991). The GMM model effectively mitigates endogeneity concerns and enhances the reliability of our findings, particularly the relationship between SUSP and COD. The results, presented in Models 7 and 8 of Table 8, align closely with our main findings in Table 5, further supporting the robustness and consistency of our empirical analysis.

Finally, to ensure that findings are not driven by dominant country effects, we re-estimate the models after excluding the United States, the country with the largest number of observations (Models 9–10 of Table 8). This approach ensures that dominant countries do not drive the results (Raimo et al. 2021). The results remain materially unchanged, indicating that our results are not country-specific outliers.

## 6 | Conclusion

This study examined the effect of SUSP on firms' COD and the moderating effect of IEQ on the SUSP–COD nexus in hospitality and tourism firms. Using three-way fixed-effects regressions and robustness checks on 2302 firm-year observations from hospitality and tourism listed firms across 34 countries between 2010 and 2022, we provide novel evidence on the financial implications of corporate sustainability in a global industry context.

Our results show that SUSP is negatively associated with the COD, indicating that stronger SUSP enhances firms' credibility, mitigates perceived risks, and reduces the COD. We also find that IEQ lowers the COD financing directly and strengthens the negative relationship between SUSP and the COD. This implies that the benefits of sustainability practices are more pronounced in countries with effective governance frameworks where sustainability commitments are viewed as more credible. However, these benefits appear weaker during the COVID-19 crisis, when lenders prioritized short-term liquidity over long-term SUSP, indicating that the financial materiality of sustainability practices may fluctuate during systemic shocks.

Altogether, our evidence supports and extends both neo-institutional theory and stakeholder theory. Consistent with neo-institutional theory, our findings indicate that firms adopt sustainability practices as a response to institutional pressures, using them to secure legitimacy with financial stakeholders. Stakeholder theory is also reinforced, as sustainability initiatives reduce information asymmetry and align corporate actions with the expectations of lenders and investors. Importantly, the moderating role of IEQ highlights the need to extend these theories by considering cross-level interactions. National governance frameworks act as enabling conditions that strengthen the credibility and financial relevance of corporate sustainability, thereby shaping how firms capture value from sustainability investments.

Our evidence has several implications for practice and policy. First, the results confirm that SUSP has tangible financial value by lowering debt costs. Managers in hospitality and tourism should therefore view sustainability initiatives not merely as tools for reputational enhancement but as strategic levers for securing cheaper financing, thereby demonstrating that environmental and social strategies can yield tangible cost-of-debt benefits in a sector highly exposed to environmental and institutional pressures. To maximize these benefits, firms need to strengthen the credibility of their sustainability commitments through transparent disclosure, independent assurance, and the adoption of sustainability-linked debt instruments such as green bonds. Substantive improvements in operational practices, particularly in resource efficiency and credible certification schemes, can further reinforce this credibility. For firms in low-IEQ environments, where formal institutions are weak, managers can partially offset governance gaps by pursuing internationally recognized sustainability certifications, obtaining independent third-party assurance, and accessing cross-border green or sustainability-linked financing instruments. These governance mechanisms allow firms to build credibility with lenders even in the absence of strong institutional frameworks.

Second, the moderating role of IEQ highlights the interdependence between firm-level sustainability and national governance systems. Policymakers can reinforce the sustainability–finance nexus by strengthening sustainability disclosure standards, enforcing anti-corruption and rule-of-law measures, and providing sector-specific incentives such as tax relief for certified sustainable hotels or government-backed green financing programs. Such interventions not only reduce financing barriers for firms but also increase investor confidence in the sector's sustainability trajectory.

Third, for investors and lenders, the findings suggest that integrating both firm-level SUSP and country-level governance quality into credit risk models and bond rating systems will improve the assessment of long-term exposures. This approach enables more accurate pricing of sustainability risks and enhances resilience against systemic shocks such as pandemics or climate-related disruptions.

This study has several limitations that provide opportunities for future research. Our analysis relies on ESG ratings, which may not fully capture firm-specific practices or the qualitative aspects of SUSP. Future studies could employ textual analyses of sustainability reports, third-party audits, or survey data to provide more comprehensive measures. In addition, our empirical findings are based on publicly listed hospitality and tourism firms, which may not generalize to small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) that often face more severe financing constraints. Therefore, future research could draw on SME data to explore the links among sustainability practices, national governance systems, and the COD. Finally, the weakened effect of sustainability during the COVID-19 crisis highlights the need for further work on how global shocks alter the financial materiality of sustainability practices. Despite these limitations, our findings show that corporate sustainability and institutional environments are not only fundamental determinants of financing outcomes in hospitality and tourism but also vital mechanisms for enhancing financial resilience in an increasingly uncertain global economy.
