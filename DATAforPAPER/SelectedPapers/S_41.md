# ESG performance as an informational signal: dual effects on the cost of capital and price synchronicity

**Santi Termprasertsakul$^a$, Piyapong Pantipjatuporn$^b$ and Krisada Khruachalee$^a$**

$^a$ Faculty of Business Administration for Society, Srinakharinwirot University, Bangkok, Thailand; $^b$ Graduate School of Applied Statistics, National Institute of Development Administration, Bangkok, Thailand

### ABSTRACT
This study examines how firms’ environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance jointly affects the cost of capital and stock price synchronicity. Using firm-year data for companies listed on the Stock Exchange of Thailand (2015–2024). Fixed-effects and dynamic system GMM models linking overall and pillar-level ESG scores to the cost of debt, the cost of equity, the weighted average cost of capital, and the extent of firm-specific information in prices are estimated. The results show that stronger ESG performance lowers financing frictions – particularly in debt markets – and reduces synchronicity, indicating improved informational efficiency. A key insight concerns governance. Investors appear to interpret governance practices as broad signals of market credibility rather than as mechanisms that enhance firm-specific transparency, which limits governance’s ability to improve price informativeness. In contrast, the environmental and social pillars are the primary drivers of lower synchronicity. This study contributes to the ESG literature by providing evidence from Thailand that ESG transparency is financially material through both capital cost and information channels, suggesting that managers seeking to enhance price informativeness should prioritize substantive environmental and social disclosures along with formal governance practices.

### IMPACT STATEMENT
This study shows that ESG performance serves as an important informational signal in emerging capital markets. Evidence from Thailand indicates that stronger ESG practices—particularly environmental and social dimensions—reduce stock price synchronicity and improve market information efficiency, while environmental performance also lowers debt financing costs. These findings demonstrate that ESG disclosure affects both financing conditions and the quality of price formation. The results provide actionable insights for corporate managers, investors, and policymakers seeking to promote sustainable finance and transparent capital markets.

## 1. Introduction
Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) practices have shifted from voluntary reputation-building to financially material drivers of firm value. Evidence suggests that ESG performance influences both the cost of capital by shaping perceptions of risk and information asymmetry and the incorporation of firm-specific information into stock prices, determining how efficiently markets reflect fundamentals. For investors, understanding these relationships is essential because they affect not only the cost structures of portfolio companies but also the ability to identify mispriced securities and achieve superior risk-adjusted returns.

In Thailand, growing interest from investors and capital market regulators has prompted the widespread adoption of ESG practices. In 2015, the Stock Exchange of Thailand (SET) introduced benchmarks called Thailand Sustainability Investment (THSI) to track and promote sustainable firms. In 2023, the SET renamed the ‘THSI’ list to ‘SET ESG Ratings’, with the aim of providing investors with enhanced information to support their investment decision-making. The SET ESG Ratings recognized 192 listed companies, representing a combined market capitalization of over THB 11.8 trillion, or approximately 70% of the total market value of securities on the SET and Market for Alternative Investment (MAI).$^1$ This indicates that large-cap companies demonstrate a high level of readiness to embrace ESG principles.$^2$ Tirapat and Tanthanongsakkun (2025) find that Thai investors are more likely to choose securities with higher Globe Rating scores, implying that Thai investors place importance on sustainability. In addition, 74% of investors prioritize the Environmental (E) dimension, followed by the Governance (G) and Social (S) dimensions. However, Thai investors place less emphasis on sustainability compared to findings from international studies, and the majority are still unfamiliar with the Globe Rating or Morningstar Rating. Furthermore, the PwC 2021 Global Investor ESG Survey reports that foreign investors are increasingly incorporating ESG considerations into their investment decisions regarding Thai companies. However, domestic investors continue to place relatively less emphasis on ESG, with many perceiving it has not yet a direct determinant of their investment decisions.$^3$

The link between ESG performance and the cost of capital has been extensively studied in developed markets. Empirical studies consistently highlight that high-quality ESG disclosure or corporate social responsibility (CSR) reports can reduce a firm’s equity costs by mitigating the information asymmetry between managers and investors. For example, Dhaliwal et al. (2011) find that firms that initiate standalone CSR reports experience a significant decline in their implied cost of equity. Similarly, El Ghoul et al. (2011) report that firms with stronger CSR performance exhibit lower expected equity returns, suggesting that investors price the informational and reputational benefits of ESG engagement. Beyond the cost of equity, research shows that ESG performance affects the cost of debt. Goss and Roberts (2011) show that banks reward firms with superior CSR practices through lower loan spreads, while Cheng et al. (2014) demonstrate that firms with strong stakeholder relations enjoy better access to financing and reduced capital constraints. Conversely, other studies argue that ESG efforts may be viewed as additional costs or manifestations of managerial entrenchment, potentially increasing capital costs (Barnea & Rubin, 2010; Nofsinger & Varma, 2014). Although ESG integration has gained momentum, empirical findings on financial impact remain inconclusive.

Considering the growing prominence of ESG factors in investment decision making, this study extends the financial implications of ESG performance by focusing specifically on its relationship with the stock price synchronicity in the Thai stock market. Price synchronicity measures the extent to which a firm’s stock price moves with the overall market or industry, as opposed to reflecting firm-specific information. Lower synchronicity implies that stock prices incorporate more unique, company-level information, which benefits informed investors by creating opportunities for superior returns through fundamental analysis (Morck et al., 2000).

This study revisits the relationship between ESG performance and all components of the cost of capital, namely, cost of debt, cost of equity, and weighted average cost of capital (WACC) for the Thai stock market. The study extends the literature by examining the relationship between ESG performance and stock price synchronicity, an area that remains largely unexplored in the context of the Thai stock market. This study aims to provide deeper insights into the financial implications of ESG performance in emerging market. Using panel data from 2015 to 2024, this study offers new evidence from an emerging market perspective. The cost of capital and price synchronicity jointly capture the financing and informational channels through which ESG matters. Lower financing costs directly improve firm value, while reduced synchronicity creates opportunities for informed investors and strengthens market efficiency. Studying these outcomes together allows us to assess whether ESG operates as a credible signal that simultaneously eases financing constraints and enhances price informativeness, which is a particularly valuable contribution in emerging markets where ESG integration is still evolving.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 highlights prior research on the relationship between ESG performance, cost of capital, and stock price synchronicity. Section 3 describes the data sources and outlines the study’s empirical methodology. Section 4 discusses the empirical findings and provides insights into the impact of Thai listed firms’ ESG performance on financial outcomes. Finally, Section 5 concludes the article and suggests directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review
This study examines the effects of ESG performance on Thai listed firms, focusing on two key financial outcomes: cost of capital and stock price synchronicity. Accordingly, this section is organized into three parts. The first explores existing research on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital, while the second addresses the influence of ESG on stock price synchronicity. The final part reviews control variables employed in prior studies.

### 2.1. ESG and cost of capital
A growing body of empirical research has examined the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of capital, yielding evidence that generally supports the ability of strong ESG performance to reduce financing costs. Piechocka-Kałuzna et al. (2021a) provide a comprehensive analysis of U.S. firms, uniquely examining both aggregate ESG scores and their individual components on the cost of equity, cost of debt, and WACC. Using a large and diverse sample from the database from 2016 to 2020, their findings reveal that higher ESG ratings are significantly associated with lower WACC and cost of equity, although they observe no consistent relationship with the cost of debt. This study’s methodological rigor, including its time-lagged approach, highlights the delayed but tangible benefits of ESG disclosure for capital costs. Focusing specifically on the U.S. healthcare sector, Piechocka-Kałuzna et al. (2021b) address a notable gap by investigating ESG’s impact within an under-explored industry. Their results align with earlier findings, demonstrating that strong ESG performance, particularly governance, significantly reduces both the WACC and cost of equity, whereas the cost of debt remains unaffected. These findings underscore the importance of governance practices for mitigating equity financing costs in healthcare firms. Eliwa et al. (2021) analyze non-financial firms in 15 EU countries from 2005 to 2016, using pooled regressions integrating the country-level score of stakeholders orientation and a fixed effect panel regression. They found that enhanced ESG performance and ESG disclosure, particularly the environmental and social dimensions, correlates with a lower cost of debt. Notably, the effect is amplified in stakeholder-oriented countries where corporate governance and economic systems emphasize the alignment of diverse stakeholder interests, highlighting the conditional nature of ESG’s financial benefits.

However, ESG does not uniformly lower financing costs. Khanchel and Lassoued (2022) examine how the effects of ESG dimensions evolved among S&P 500 firms between 2011 and 2019, using panel data analysis for both fixed and random effects. Their analysis reveals a complex temporal pattern in which environmental disclosure initially reduces the WACC but this effect diminishes over time. Governance disclosure exhibits a diminishing effect that eventually reverses, whereas social disclosure consistently increases the cost of capital. This nuanced evidence suggests that the financial implications of ESG disclosure are multifaceted and depend on the dimensions and temporal dynamics considered.

Research in Thailand remains relatively scarce owing to the limited availability of ESG data. Most studies rely on corporate governance or CSR proxies rather than comprehensive ESG scores. In a cross-sectional study of 303 non-financial firms listed on the SET for the 2014 fiscal year, Ongklang et al. (2017) assess corporate governance quality across the five dimensions of shareholder rights, equitable treatment, stakeholder role, disclosure/transparency, and board responsibilities, and examine their effects on the costs of debt and equity and WACC. Multiple regression results indicated that stronger governance, particularly in shareholder rights and disclosure, significantly lowered both cost of debt and equity, and thus reduced overall WACC. In contrast, Jantadej and Wattanatorn (2020) use data from 2007 to 2016 on SET-listed firms to explore how board effectiveness metrics (meeting attendance, frequency, board size, and audit committee composition) influence the cost of debt. Their regressions, which address endogeneity via two-stage least squares, reveal a counterintuitive trend: enhanced board effectiveness is associated with a higher cost of debt, thus supporting the sub-optimal investment (agency of debt) hypothesis. Phoprachak (2018) examined approximately 370 SET-listed firms as of 2014, using a structural equation model to explore how firm size indirectly influences the cost of capital through CSR disclosure (according to GRI standards). The results demonstrate that CSR disclosure increases the cost of debt for small and medium-sized firms but reduces it for large firms; CSR has no significant impact on the cost of equity. Suttipun (2023) focuses on the MAI$^4$ from 2017 to 2021. ESG performance is measured by the content analysis scoring of annual reports, and financial risk is assessed based on the debt-to-equity ratio. Utilizing the multiple regression, their results showed that average ESG performance improves over time and that stronger ESG performance is significantly associated with lower corporate financial risk, in line with signaling theory.

Previous studies highlight diverse methodologies and nuanced findings, showing that the impact of ESG on financing costs depends on the dimension examined, type of capital, firm characteristics, and research design. In Thailand, research has mainly focused on governance or CSR disclosure, specific forms of capital, or alternative markets, often using cross-sectional data and narrow indicators. Owing to the limited availability of ESG data, no study has yet assessed how comprehensive ESG scores affect all components of the cost of capital – debt, equity, and WACC – within a unified framework. This study addresses this gap using panel data analysis to comprehensively assess how ESG scores influence each component of the cost of capital in the Thai stock market, thereby offering a more integrated and robust understanding of the financial implications of ESG.

### 2.2. ESG and price synchronicity
Stock price synchronicity captures the extent to which firm-specific information is incorporated relative to market information. Recent empirical studies have examined the relationship between ESG performance and stock price synchronicity, using diverse datasets and methodologies. For example, Potharla et al. (2024) investigate the financial viability of ESG data by examining their relationship with stock price synchronicity in the Indian equity market. These results indicate that higher ESG performance mitigates synchronicity in the Indian stock market, suggesting that ESG performance enhances the flow of firm-specific information into the market. Similarly, Ram (2023) analyzes Indian firms on the BSE-ESG Index from 2013 to 2019, confirming a robust negative impact. An increase in ESG score corresponds to a decline in synchronicity. When each ESG component is observed, the environmental (E) and social (S) dimensions of ESG are statistically significant; however their effects differ: E negatively affects price synchronicity, while S positively affects price synchronicity. Expanding on ESG data quality, Potharla et al. (2024) decompose the social (S) dimension of ESG into community engagement, human rights, product responsibility, and workforce satisfaction. Their findings reveal that superior social performance is associated with higher stock price synchronicity, indicating that social disclosures tend to move more closely with overall market trends. In China, Yin et al. (2026) investigate ESG rating disagreements and demonstrate that divergent ratings across agencies increase synchronicity by obscuring firm-specific information. Yang et al. (2023) analyze environmental information disclosure in the Chinese market and provide evidence of its association with reduced information efficiency, as indicated by higher stock price synchronicity. Xu et al. (2024) employ a novel textual analysis and show that greater similarity between a firm’s ESG report and those of its peers in the preceding year reduces the informativeness of firm-specific disclosures, thereby leading to higher stock price synchronicity in the subsequent year among Chinese firms. Li et al. (2023) extend this analysis by revealing non-linear effects. Specifically, ESG ratings reduce synchronicity primarily among high-synchronicity Chinese firms, suggesting diminishing marginal benefits. Ma et al. (2025) analyze Malaysian firms and confirm that higher ESG ratings reduce synchronicity, which is consistent with findings in developed markets.

Although these studies underscore that ESG transparency, whether through ratings, textual uniqueness, or regulatory mandates, generally enhances firm-specific information, thereby reducing synchronicity, the results remain inconclusive. Nuances emerge across markets, ESG dimensions, and data quality. However, the consistency of the findings across emerging (India, China, and Malaysia) and global samples strengthens the use of ESG performances as a tool to improve market efficiency.

Research on the relationship between ESG performance and stock price synchronicity, particularly in Thailand, remains limited and inconclusive. This study is the first to simultaneously investigate the interplay between ESG, the cost of capital, and price synchronicity, aiming to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the market consequences following increased emphasis on ESG practices. By integrating these dimensions, this study offers novel insights into how ESG considerations influence market efficiency and firm-specific information in the Thai equity market.

### 2.3. Control variables
In empirical studies examining the relationship between ESG factors and financial outcomes such as the cost of capital and stock price synchronicity, including control variables in regression models is a standard practice. These variables help to isolate the unique effect of ESG by controlling for other firm-level characteristics and market conditions that may influence the dependent variables (Dhaliwal et al., 2011; El Ghoul et al., 2011; Eliwa et al., 2021; Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022). Failure to control for such factors may result in biased or spurious estimates of the impact of ESG (Kim et al., 2012).

Firm size is among the most common control variables because larger firms typically have greater diversification and easier access to capital markets, often resulting in lower capital costs and influencing stock price behavior (Dhaliwal et al., 2011; Eliwa et al., 2021; Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Ma et al., 2025). Leverage is also frequently included, considering its role in increasing financial risk, which is factored into investor pricing and price synchronicity (Dhaliwal et al., 2011; Eliwa et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2026). Profitability, such as return on assets or equity, serves as an indicator of firm performance that affects investor confidence and the cost of capital (Eliwa et al., 2021; Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Li et al., 2023; Potharla et al., 2024). Growth opportunities, proxied by market-to-book ratios, capture future investment potential and information asymmetry, thus influencing valuation and stock price dynamics (Dhaliwal et al., 2011; Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Ma et al., 2025; Ram, 2023). Firm age is often included as a control variable, measured as the number of years since the company’s incorporation. Generally, older firms have a lower cost of capital (Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Kim et al., 2012; Li et al., 2023; Ram, 2023). Moreover, industry fixed effects or sector dummies are routinely applied to control for sector-specific risks and regulatory environments that influence financial outcomes (Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Yin et al., 2026). Additional firm-level controls examined in recent studies include liquidity (Li et al., 2023), earnings volatility (Ma et al., 2025), and institutional ownership (Ram, 2023), reflecting a broadening recognition of the factors that influence stock price behavior and capital costs.

Therefore, these control variables therefore are essential to ensure that the estimated relationship between ESG and financial outcomes achieves greater validity and is not confounded by alternative explanations (Li et al., 2023; Potharla et al., 2024).

## 3. Data and methodology
### 3.1. Data
This study uses an unbalanced panel dataset covering 2015 to 2024. The sample consists of companies included in the SET100 Index. The selection of SET100 constituents ensures that the analysis focuses on firms with high market capitalization and trading activity that are more likely to disclose ESG information and attract domestic and international investors.

Accordingly, this study employs two specifications: the relationship between ESG performance and all components of the cost of capital, and the relationship between ESG performance and stock price synchronicity. Two types of data frequencies are used in the analysis. Yearly data are employed for ESG scores, control variables, and cost of capital, whereas daily data are utilized to calculate stock price synchronicity. ESG scores are obtained from Bloomberg, which provides standardized and comparable measures of firms’ ESG performance. The SET Index is used as a market benchmark for computing price synchronicity.

The control variables are selected based on their frequent application in previous ESG–financial performance studies and data availability to account for firm-specific characteristics. These include:
*   **Firm Age (AGE):** measured as the number of years since the firm’s listing on the SET.
*   **Trading Volume (VOL):** measured as the average annual trading volume, capturing market liquidity and investor activity.
*   **Standard Deviation (SD):** reflecting the volatility of stock returns over the year.
*   **Market Capitalization (MC):** representing firm size, calculated as the market value of outstanding shares.
*   **Price-to-Book Ratio (PB):** an indicator of valuation and growth potential.

### 3.2. Methodology and specifications
This study employs panel data regression models to examine the relationship between ESG performance and two key financial outcomes: (i) the cost of capital, measured by the cost of debt (COD), cost of equity (COE), and weighted average cost of capital (WACC), and (ii) stock price synchronicity (SYNCH). The sample comprises 96 firms from the SET100 Index, with available ESG scores from Bloomberg for 2015 to 2024. Control variables are included to account for firm-specific characteristics and market conditions. We excluded firm-year observations with missing values in the dependent variables, ESG scores, or key control variables. The weight debt is computed as total interest-bearing debt divided by total capital at fiscal year-end and equity weight is estimated by 1 minus weight debt.

### 3.3. Models examining ESG and the cost of capital
Two models are used to capture the effect of ESG on the cost of capital.

**Model 1** uses the total ESG score as the key independent variable:
$$Cost_{it} = \alpha + \beta ESG_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \mu_i + \epsilon_{it} \tag{1}$$
where $Cost_{it}$ represents the $COD_{it}$; $COE_{it}$; or $WACC_{it}$ for firm $i$ in year $t$
$COD_{it} = ((Short\ term\ debt \times Pre\text{-}tax\ cost\ of\ short\ term\ debt + Long\ term\ debt \times Pre\text{-}tax\ cost\ of\ Long\text{-}term\ debt)/Total\ Debt)) \times (1\text{-}Tax\ Rate^5)$
$COE_{it} = Risk\ free\ rate^6 + (Beta^7 \times Country\ Premium^8)$
$WACC_{it} = (Weight\ Debt \times Cost\ of\ Debt) + (Weight\ Equity \times Cost\ of\ Equity)$
$ESG_{it}$ denotes the total ESG score for firm $i$ in year $t$
$Control_{kit}$ are control variables ($k = 5$) which are AGE, VOL, SD, MC and PB for firm $i$ in year $t$
$\mu_i$ represent firm fixed effects
$\epsilon_{it}$ is the error term.
The coefficient $\beta$ is expected to be negative. Higher ESG performance is generally associated with reduced financing costs and lower required returns from investors, reflecting reduced risk premiums and improved market perception.

**Model 2** replaces the total ESG score with its three components ($X_{it}$), Environmental ($E_{it}$), Social ($S_{it}$), and Governance ($G_{it}$) scores:
$$Cost_{it} = \alpha + \delta X_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \mu_i + \epsilon_{it} \tag{2}$$
This decomposition enables the relative contribution of each ESG pillar to the cost of capital to be examined. The coefficient $\delta$ is also expected to be negative.

### 3.4. Measurement of price synchronicity
Price synchronicity (SYNCH) reflects the extent to which a firm’s stock returns are largely driven by overall market returns, as explained by the capital asset pricing model (CAPM). Following Morck et al. (2000), price synchronicity is calculated from the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) of the market model estimated for each firm-year using daily stock returns:
$$R_{id} = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{md} + \epsilon_{id} \tag{3}$$
where $R_{id}$ is the daily return of firm $i$ on day $d$ and $R_{md}$ is the daily return of the SET Index. To facilitate regression analysis, the $R^2$ values are transformed into price synchronicity as follows:
$$SYNCH_{it} = \ln \left( \frac{R^2_{it}}{1 - R^2_{it}} \right) \tag{4}$$
A value of $SYNCH_{it}$ greater than 0 indicates that firm-specific price movements are largely driven by market-wide information. Conversely, when $SYNCH_{it}$ equals 0, the market model accounts for only 50% of the stock return variation, with other idiosyncratic or firm-specific factors explaining the remaining 50%. A value of $SYNCH_{it}$ less than 0 implies that the market model cannot adequately explain the stock returns, and that other factors, such as firm-specific news, ESG performance, industry effects, or microeconomic conditions, play a more dominant role in influencing price movements.

### 3.5. Models examining ESG and price synchronicity
Two models are used to investigate the relationship between ESG performance and price synchronicity.

**Model 3** employs the total ESG score as the main explanatory variable:
$$SYNCH_{it} = \alpha + \beta ESG_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \mu_i + \epsilon_{it} \tag{5}$$

**Model 4** uses the individual $E_{it}$, $S_{it}$, and $G_{it}$ scores:
$$SYNCH_{it} = \alpha + \delta X_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \mu_i + \epsilon_{it} \tag{6}$$
In Eqs. (5) and (6), the coefficient $\beta$ measures the effect of overall ESG performance on stock price synchronicity, while $\delta$ captures the marginal effects of the environmental, social, and governance pillars. A negative estimate indicates that higher ESG performance, both in aggregate and across individual dimensions, is associated with lower stock price synchronicity, suggesting that stock prices incorporate more firm-specific information than being driven by market-wide factors. This pattern is consistent with improved transparency and the value relevance of ESG disclosures.

### 3.6. Models examining ESG and idiosyncratic volatility of stock prices
This study also examines idiosyncratic volatility which represents the portion of a firm’s stock return variability that cannot be explained by the CAPM. It captures firm-specific risk arising from unique events, such as ESG. Similarly, two models are employed to examine the link between ESG performance and idiosyncratic volatility of stock prices.

**Model 5** employs the total ESG score as the main explanatory variable:
$$Idiosynch_{it} = \alpha + \beta ESG_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \epsilon_{it} \tag{7}$$

**Model 6** uses the individual $E_{it}$, $S_{it}$ and $G_{it}$ scores:
$$Idiosynch_{it} = \alpha + \delta X_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \epsilon_{it} \tag{8}$$
In this context, a higher ESG score signals better information dissemination to the market, enabling prices to reflect more firm-level news and less aggregated market noise. Consequently, a positive coefficient $\delta$ would be consistent with the notion that ESG performance enhances firm-specific information, leading to greater variability in stock returns independent of systematic risk factors.

### 3.7. System generalized method of moments (system GMM) estimation
This study employees the System GMM estimator proposed by Arellano and Bover (1995) and Blundell and Bond (1998) to address the potential endogeneity, dynamic relationships, and unobserved heterogeneity among firms. This method is particularly appropriate for dynamic panel data, where the inclusion of lagged dependent variables and potentially endogenous explanatory variables may render conventional estimators, such as pooled OLS or fixed effects, inconsistent.
$$SYNCH_{it} = \alpha + \lambda SYNCH_{it-1} + \beta ESG_{it} + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \gamma_k Control_{kit} + \mu_i + \epsilon_{it} \tag{9}$$

This framework combines the equations in first differences and levels, using lagged levels of the endogenous variables ($SYNCH_{it-1}$) as instruments. This approach improves estimator efficiency and reduces finite-sample bias, particularly when the dependent variable persists over time. Following the recommendations of Roodman (2009), lag lengths are limited to two to three periods to prevent instrument proliferation. Both one- and two-step estimators are employed, with the latter adjusted using the Windmeijer (2005) finite-sample correction to obtain robust standard errors. Robust standard errors are used throughout to account for heteroscedasticity and cross-sectional dependence. The validity of the internal instruments is assessed using the Hansen/Sargan over-identification test, while Arellano–Bond AR(1) and AR(2) tests are applied to confirm the absence of serial correlation in the differenced residuals.

### 4.3. The relationship between ESG performance and stock price synchronicity

Similarly, the social score flips from positive to negative, suggesting that firms’ social initiatives reduce common price co-movements once within-firm dynamics are isolated. The governance score remains positive and significant, indicating that governance performances may still act as a general market signal rather than contributing to firm-specific differentiation. This contrast highlights how failing to account for firm-level characteristics may mask the information benefits of ESG and that environmental and social initiatives play a key role in reducing synchronicity in the Thai stock market. Regarding the control variables, firm age, trading volume, and return volatility are positive and significant, whereas market capitalization becomes negative and significant, suggesting that larger firms tend to exhibit lower synchronicity.

Consequently, the system GMM estimation not only confirms the direction and significance of the ESG effect observed in the fixed effects model but also strengthens its causal interpretation by addressing simultaneity and omitted variable biases. These findings suggest that firms with sustained improvements in ESG performance genuinely experience greater information efficiency and lower stock price synchronicity. ## 5. Conclusion and future research
This study demonstrates that ESG performance functions as an important market signal in the Thai capital market, influencing the cost of capital and the efficiency of price formation. Drawing on panel data from 2015 to 2024, the results highlight that the financial effects of ESG performance are not uniform across financing channels, but consistently underscore its signaling value, as shown in Table 8.

For debt financing, when firm-specific effects are considered, strong environmental performance is rewarded with lower borrowing costs, suggesting that creditors interpret credible ESG practices, particularly environmental initiatives, as signals of reduced long-term risk and improved sustainability. Conversely, both social and governance scores are positively associated with the cost of debt, suggesting that lenders may perceive extensive social or governance activities as resource-intensive commitments that increase near-term financial risk. These results reveal that environmental performance serves as a risk-reducing signal to debt markets.

For equity financing, the positive association between ESG and equity costs observed in the pooled models disappears when controlling for firm-specific effects. This finding indicates that investors in Thailand do not consistently price ESG practices in equity premiums beyond cross-sectional firm differences. Thus, ESG performance has yet to emerge as a robust return-reducing signal in equity markets, although its informational role may strengthen as reporting standards evolve. Overall, environmental performance lowers the cost of equity, whereas social activities increase it, reinforcing the view that markets distinguish between the value-enhancing and cost-imposing aspects of ESG. Meanwhile, governance remains largely neutral.

Moreover, the findings on stock price synchronicity confirm that ESG performance enhances the incorporation of firm-specific information into prices, particularly through environmental and social transparency. Conversely, the governance pillar remains positively related to synchronicity, implying that governance practices continue to act as general credibility signals rather than mechanisms that foster firm-specific transparency.

Therefore, these results suggest that strong ESG performance acts as a dual market signal that (1) reduces financing frictions in debt markets by signaling lower risk and (2) improves capital market efficiency by embedding more firm-specific information into stock prices. Although its impact on equity financing costs remains less conclusive, the evidence underscores that ESG reporting in emerging economies, such as Thailand, is more than compliance; it is a strategic communication tool. For firms, investing in credible ESG performance can strengthen their relationships with creditors and enhance their informational credibility in the market. For regulators and policymakers, such as the SET, the findings justify continued efforts to elevate ESG reporting standards because stronger performance not only improves corporate accountability but also advances the overall efficiency of financial markets to support sustainable finance.

Future research could extend these findings in several ways. First, cross-country comparative studies in emerging markets could help determine whether the signaling role of ESG performance is context-specific or generalizable across institutional environments. Second, disentangling voluntary versus mandatory performance could clarify whether market participants value discretion in ESG reporting differently than regulatory compliance. Finally, exploring the interaction of ESG performance with broader themes, such as green financing instruments, climate risk exposure, and corporate innovation, could provide a deeper understanding of how ESG communication influences firm performance and market development over time.
