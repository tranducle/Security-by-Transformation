### ABSTRACT

Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors have become increasingly relevant within the financial world. Despite extensive evidence regarding the equity side, limited studies in the literature have investigated the potential effects of ESG performance on the main drivers of debt. This study aims to understand whether there is a positive effect from a cost of debt standpoint for corporations with relatively better ESG performance. Using the ESG score by Refinitiv and a sharp regression discontinuity design, we do not find statistically significant evidence for discrete jumps in correspondence with the ESG score average for the cost of debt of European nonfinancial corporates over the 2018–2020 period. In a sharp regression discontinuity setting, we obtain analogous results by developing a linear probability model.

## 1. Introduction

The search for dependencies between environmental, social, and governance (ESG) criteria and corporate financial performance began in the 1970s, according to Friede, Busch, and Bassen (2015). Since then, over 2000 empirical studies and related reviews have been published on this topic. In their synthesis, the authors report that about 90% of these studies have found a nonnegative relation between ESG criteria and corporate financial performance, thereby confirming the empirical relevance of the ESG investing business case.

However, most studies have focused on the equity side. Specifically, most authors have attempted to identify a relationship between corporate equity performance and ESG strategies and commitments. In this sense, the seminal work of Gompers, Ishii, and Metrick (2003), through the construction of their notable “Governance Index,” was one of the first to find a significant relationship between shareholders’ rights and a series of relevant performance indicators for corporates, such as firm value, profits, and sales growth.

Soon after, Klock, Mansi, and Maxwell (2005) found that the Governance Index could explain a lower cost of debt financing, i.e., a difference of 34 basis points between corporates with strong antitakeover provisions and those with weak antitakeover provisions. As anticipated, subsequent studies mainly focused on the equity side, with only a few considering the relevance of ESG in the cost of debt. Among the few exceptions, a recent publication by Eliwa, Aboud, & Saleh (2021) is notable, wherein the authors managed to distinguish between effective commitments and efforts to construct an image of commitment to ESG strategies.

Thus, in a sharp regression discontinuity setting and using the ESG score by Refinitiv, we attempt to understand whether there is a discrete jump—and if any, to quantify it—in the cost of debt financing for European nonfinancial corporates corresponding with the ESG score average. We consider the data available for the last three years (2018–2020) through Datastream.

The rest of this study is articulated as follows. Section 2 presents a literature review illustrating the most relevant results from the early 2000s, primarily focused on corporate governance and more recent developments in the broader ESG space. Sections 3 and 4 are dedicated to the aims and methods of our work and present our dataset and models. Section 5 presents our empirical results, and Section 6 concludes. In the appendix, alternative models are presented for completeness, to check the robustness of our results, and present details of how the ESG score is computed.

## 2. Literature review

### 2.1. The early 2000s

One of the works that initiated research on corporate governance was by Gompers et al. (2003). The authors concentrate on the level of shareholder rights for large firms only during the 1990s. Even though there is no reference to the cost of debt financing, the construction of the “Governance Index”[^1] should be mentioned for two reasons. First, it has been one of the first attempts to find empirical foundations between ESG thematics and relevant corporate metrics, such as firm value. Second, this index allowed Klock et al. (2005) to find a significant impact of governance for bondholders. Evidently, the latter approach is more market based than the one we will use, but the message is somewhat analogous. The authors found that bond market investors ascribe a positive value to antitakeover governance provisions.

In the same years, Andersona, Mansib, and Reebc (2003) analyzed the relationship linking ownership of the founding family and agency cost of debt. The authors discovered that founding family ownership, common in large and public firms, is associated with a lower cost of debt financing.

The work by Boubakri and Ghouma (2010) is in the same direction. Using a market-based approach, they found a significant effect of governance on bond yield spreads and ratings using a multinational sample of firms. More precisely, they identified a positive effect on bond yield spreads of the voting/cash-flow rights wedge and family control while discovering a negative impact on bond ratings. Interestingly, they found no effect of state control on these variables. Bhojraj and Sengupta (2003) also identified the relevance of governance mechanisms for bondholders, wherein the main idea is that governance can help reduce default risk. More precisely, governance mechanisms act through two different effects: (1) mitigation of agency costs, thereby allowing for closer monitoring of managerial performance and (2) reduction of information asymmetry between firms and lenders.

In a different context, Bradley and Chen (2011) explored another aspect of the relationship between corporate governance and the cost of debt. Using an index constructed as the sum of three indicator variables, they found higher credit ratings and lower yield spreads for corporations that provide limited liability and indemnification for their directors. They concluded that it is not straightforward to understand whether weak governance is uniformly bad for shareholders. Indeed, undisciplined managers engaging in self-serving behavior could pursue risk-reducing activities that are good for bondholders. Thus, the benefit of a reduced cost of debt could be shared among the different shareholders.

Similarly, Cremers, Martijn, and Wei (2007) investigated two new aspects: (1) the impact of shareholder control on credit risk and (2) event risk covenants. For the first one, they found a dependence on takeover vulnerability. They found a positive link between shareholder control and bonds’ yields when the firm is exposed to takeovers and a negative link when it is not. Interestingly, they quantified a maximum of 66 basis points as the difference in bond yields due to differences in takeover vulnerability. As for the event risk covenants, they found a divergence between shareholder governance and bondholder interests when there are no bond covenants.

Within the same context, Wang and Zhang (2009) further analyzed the link between institutional equity investors and the cost of debt capital. From a different perspective, they proposed this linkage not as a corporate governance effect but as an information asymmetry issue. Distinguishing among different groups of institutional investors, they found empirical support for the idea that institutional equity ownership reduces credit spreads through their influence on information asymmetry and not only through a corporate governance effect. From an ownership perspective, but in post-default restructuring scenarios, Aslan and Kumar (2012) used an international sample of syndicated loans, together with dynamic ownership structure data, to empirically analyze the endogenous determination of corporate control concentration and the cost of debt. A theoretical foundation for these effects is also presented in their work.

### 2.2. Recent ESG developments

As far as the more recent literature is concerned, a more comprehensive approach to ESG criteria and strategy has been developed. Among the recent studies that consider the potential effect of corporate social responsibility (CSR) on credit ratings, we highlight Attig et al. (2013), who managed to find empirical evidence for relatively higher ratings for firms with good social performance, controlling for different firm characteristics and potential endogeneity between CSR and ratings. In similar research, but with a more market-based perspective, we find Stellner, Klein, and Zwergel (2015). The interesting aspect of this study is the investigation of the role of a corporate’s country ESG performance and its effects as a moderating factor, if any. Using credit ratings and zero-volatility spreads (i.e., z-spreads) as proxies for credit risk, the authors found significant evidence supporting the idea that a country’s ESG performance affects the link between CSR and credit risk, despite finding only weak evidence for this relationship. They quantify in 9.64 basis points the reduction in corporate z-spreads if a firm’s CSP (i.e. Corporate Social Performance) is comparable to its country’s ESG performance.

Hoepner et al. (2016) analyzed the cost of bank loans of borrowers from 28 different countries and the most relevant industries to confirm the importance of a corporate’s country sustainability dimension. Not only did they find a statistically significant and negative relationship between corporates’ cost of debt and the corresponding country’s sustainability score, but they also found no evidence for a firm-level sustainability influence. These results contradict a previous study by Oikonomou, Brooks, and Pavelin (2014). Through a longitudinal dataset, they found that lower corporate yield spreads are actually associated with good CSP. Furthermore, they found supporting evidence for an impact on credit ratings as well as the probability of a corporate issue being considered a speculative asset.

Similarly, La Rosa et al. (2018) found strong support for companies to improve their corporate social performance.[^2] Through an accounting and a market-based approach, they used a sample of listed European nonfinancial corporates for the period of 2005–2012 to illustrate the negative link between interest rates and CSP. A parallel, positive relationship between credit ratings and CSP was also found. For a similar analysis, but in the Japanese context and with a more quantitative approach, see Fabozzi, Wah Ng, and Tunaru (2021). In this study, a new proxy for CSR by Sustainalytics was used with quantile regression.

Besides the general focus on corporate CSP, there is a growing interest in the literature on the potential impacts of environmental commitments and strategies. We use the ESG score by Refinitiv as a proxy for corporate ESG performance. As already mentioned, a comprehensive review up to 2015 can be found in Friede et al. (2015). From a lending perspective, Kleimeier and Viehs (2018) found a significant, negative relationship between carbon emissions’ voluntary disclosure and loan spreads for publicly listed firms. In addition, thanks to CDP’s database, they found that these effects are due to environmental risk, not investor preferences.

Lastly, a new branch of research has started focusing on the new, so-called “use-of-proceeds” (UoP) instruments that are becoming increasingly common in the industry and among practitioners. The most used type of bond is the green bond, i.e., a bond whose proceeds are specifically used for projects that have a positive impact on the environment and which are appreciated by an increasing number of investors.[^3] The number of investors with ESG mandates is constantly increasing in the fixed income market as well.[^4]

When talking about UoP bonds, besides considering the potential differences in terms of pricing with respect to the conventional instruments, a reference should also be made to the risk of greenwashing, i.e., the combination of poor environmental performance combined with its positive communication, as defined in Delmas and Burbano (2011), related to corporate bonds in our specific case. This phenomenon may be due to the financial convenience of green bonds (and other UoP instruments), as shown by Gianfrate and Peri (2019) in a study involving 121 European green bonds and using a propensity score matching approach.

By contrast, Baker et al. (2018) found that green municipal bonds are issued at a premium, whereas Wulandari et al. (2018) found that liquidity risk has become negligible for green bonds despite having been a relevant variable for their yield spreads. Lastly, Zerbib (2017) found a negative premium for green bond yields, using a matching method and focusing on 135 investment grade green issuances worldwide. Hence, whether UoP and, specifically, green bonds are issued at a premium compared with their conventional counterparts is yet to be understood by academic researchers and practitioners.

## 3. Aims

The idea behind our empirical investigation is that thanks to better ESG strategy, commitments, and performance, European nonfinancial corporates may be rewarded by institutional investors, lenders, and any other type of shareholder with a potentially lower cost of debt financing. The latter may be due to several reasons, including a possible reduction in environmental or reputational risks.

To our knowledge, the main novelty of our work is due to the use of the sharp regression discontinuity design applied to the relationship between ESG performance and the corporates’ cost of debt. Among the few examples of applying this technique to the corporate world, Bronzini and Iachini (2014) analyzed an R&D subsidy program in several corporates in Northern Italy. Otherwise, there are several examples of sharp regression discontinuity approaches implemented in the context of economics and social sciences. In this regard, we highlight an example from Duchini (2017), whose focus is on Italian undergraduate students and wherein the author tries to understand the effect of college remedial education. In Lalive (2008), the time spent on job search is analyzed with regard to extensions in the duration of employment benefits using an Austrian sample.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1. The dataset

This section provides a more detailed presentation of the proxies we selected for this study.

For ESG performance, we use the ESG score by Refinitiv available through Datastream. We focus solely on European nonfinancial corporates for the last three years of data available, using only the firms for which corresponding accounting data are also available. Hence, our observation period corresponds to the years 2018–2020. For a more detailed description of how the score is constructed, see the Appendix C and Refinitiv (2021). One of the most relevant aspects of this score for our study is its percentile rank scoring methodology. This allows us to implement a sharp regression discontinuity approach to understand the relative, i.e., compared with a corporate’s peers, any positive effect for a corporate to be above the average in terms of ESG performance. This will be our independent (also called “forcing” or “running” in the sharp regression discontinuity (RD) setup) variable.

For the cost of debt, we use an accounting proxy given by the ratio of interest expense on debt to total debt of a corporation; both measures are available through Datastream. In this regard, we follow the same approach as that of Izzo and Magnanelli (2012); they used interest expense on debt divided by total debt as a proxy for the cost of debt. We constructed this variable to increase the sample size available. While a built-in cost of debt is available through Datastream, only a few true corporate values are actually available, thereby drastically reducing the potential sample size.

We also considered a series of control variables to understand whether they could improve the explanatory power of our model. However, they did not prove to be more effective than the ESG score alone in explaining potential differences (and discrete jumps) in the cost of debt. Therefore, we chose to exclude them from our model of reference and included the alternative model and relative results in Appendix A for completeness. These controls were always taken from Datastream and included several measures of corporate profitability and size. These are:

*   net sales or revenues;
*   total assets;
*   total debt as a % of common equity; and
*   EBIT & depreciation.

Table 1 presents the summary statistics of our dataset.

Average ESG score is around 58, even though we were expecting a score of 50, considering the methodology used to compute the score in the first place. This slight difference must be accounted for because we use the ESG score average as our selected cutoff in the RD setting. The difference between the actual and expected score may occur because we are considering three years of observations from 2018 to 2020 (the last three years of data available) and because we built our dataset based on availability from Datastream.

### 4.2. Main model

As previously mentioned, we set up a sharp RD model with a cutoff corresponding to the average of our running variable, i.e., the ESG score. More precisely, our model is given by:

$$cost\_of\_debt_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG\_score\_d_i + \beta_2 ESG\_score\_d_i sust_i + \beta_{SRD} sust_i + u_i \tag{1}$$

for $i$ with $|ESG\_score\_d_i| < b$, where $ESG\_score\_d$ is our running variable demeaned by its average,[^5] $sust_i = 1(ESG\_score\_d_i > 0)$[^6] is the treatment variable with a cutoff corresponding to the ESG score mean,[^7] $b$ is the width of our window/bandwidth, and $u_i$ is the error term. $\beta_{SRD}$ is our coefficient of interest, where SRD stands for sharp RD.

We do not implement a standard sharp RD model. Rather, through different values for bandwidth $b$, we aim to obtain results similar to a nonparametric RD approach using local linear regressions. The idea consists of running linear regressions on both sides of the cutoff—the mean ESG score in our case—using only the observations within our selected window. Compared with a standard sharp RD setting, we let the data show whether there is discontinuity corresponding to the cutoff while checking the robustness of our results through various values for bandwidth $b$. See Cattaneo, Idrobo, and Titiunik (2019) for a more detailed introduction to RD models.

For simplicity, we do not consider local polynomial regressions in our model, even though they could constitute a valuable extension to our approach. In this regard, it is worth mentioning Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014) for an interesting proposition of new confidence interval estimators in a polynomial sharp RD setting. Furthermore, bandwidth values could be selected using a data-driven algorithm as shown, for example, by Imbens and Kalyanaraman (2012).

For what concerns our model of reference, we decided to include four bandwidth values that start from 5 and increase by 5 every time we move to a wider window. We arrive at the last model, which covers a range of 40 centered on our selected cutoff. We opted for this model and not for one with a narrower window size in the interest of data availability, even though we report the results for narrower window sizes in the appendix as reference and for completeness. With the necessary adaptation required, our approach is similar to what D. S. Lee (2001) did in his work as a first analysis. Then, he used a parametric approach to estimate the function leading up to and beyond the threshold to retain the highest level of flexibility. The choice to use different bandwidth values is an intermediate solution between a naive approach that would split the sample into two subgroups and a parametric approach that would let the information in the data decide the functional form of the regression function.

### 4.3. Linear probability model

We also present a linear probability model in the sharp RD setting, similar to D. S. Lee (2001). More precisely, we build a dummy variable that takes the value of 1 for companies with a cost of debt above the average (ca. 2.2% in our case). Apart from the dependent variable, the model setup is the same as shown above and below. Note that we call the dependent, dummy variable *high*,[^8] i.e., $high_i = 1(cost\_of\_debt_i > 2.18\%)$.

$$high_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG\_score\_d_i + \beta_2 ESG\_score\_d_i sust_i + \beta_{SRD} sust_i + u_i \tag{2}$$

for $i$ with $|ESG\_score\_d_i| < b$.[^9] We also implement this model to understand whether above-average ESG performance affects the probability of being above the average in terms of the cost of debt financing. A linear probability model has several limitations from an econometric and a conceptual standpoint, and a logit or probit model could be used instead.[^10] For simplicity, we only implement this type of model and leave to future research a potential improvement through other types of more sophisticated models.

## 5. Results and discussion

### 5.1. Graphical analysis

When using sharp RD models, it is always advisable to first run a graphical analysis of the data to understand whether there might be an actual discontinuity. Note that in our analysis, we decided to drop five outliers from our sample (with a cost of debt above 7%) because these values are extreme compared with the rest of the sample, especially considering the average and standard deviation of our dependent variable. Here we present two types of scatterplots:

*   a simple one, where we plot our running variable (i.e., *ESG_score*) vs. the cost of debt (Fig. 1);
*   a possibly more readable scatterplot, where we put data in 100 bins, plotting bin averages[^11] with 100 equal-sized bins (Fig. 2).

At this point, considering possible discontinuity corresponding to the *ESG_score* average (i.e., circa 58), Fig. 3 zooms in on bandwidth $b = 20$ to better visualize our analytical objective.[^12] As shown in Fig. 3, there might be a discontinuity in terms of the potential effect of the *ESG_score* on the corporation cost of debt.

Although simple, this first type of graphical check proves useful in many applications of the sharp RD model. Indeed, if there is no graphical evidence for potential discontinuity, it is difficult to envision that any other model, even if more sophisticated, could find any statistically significant discontinuity. Moreover, this preliminary check might be important to understand whether there are potential jumps away from the considered cutoff—in our case, the *ESG_score* mean—which does not seem to be the case.

### 5.2. Specification checks

We first refer to the histogram of our running variable, *ESG_score*, to check its relative smoothness. Indeed, if discontinuity corresponds to the selected cutoff, it may indicate that the forcing variable has been manipulated. See the example of students’ test scores in S. Lee and Lemieux (2010) for a detailed explanation of the phenomenon. In our case, a few more observations correspond to our selected cutoff. However, we should remember that the *ESG_score* variable is constructed by Refinitiv through a percentile rank scoring methodology using industry and country benchmarks. Hence, when we observe the average of our running variable, if not too extreme, the RD design should remain valid, noting that we are considering a three-year period from 2018 to 2020 (Fig. 4).

Then, we specify that the variation in the treatment of our model setup being above the average in terms of *ESG_score*, can be considered randomized as if it were from a randomized experiment. It is true that corporations have some influence over their *ESG_score*, setting their ESG strategy and objectives. However, the exact final score is determined by a third-party entity, which, in principle, should be *super partes*. In other words, even though some firms will have a higher probability of being around the considered cutoff, i.e., the *ESG_score* average, every corporation can be assumed to have approximately the same probability of being either above or below the selected threshold; this is an idea represented in our case by the dummy variable *sust*.

To conclude, we note from Fig. 4 that when considering the highest bandwidth value ($b = 20$), we are covering a large portion of our sample; in our dataset, not many observations fall outside this interval. This is probably why the actual average of our running variable is not exactly 50 as we would theoretically expect.

### 5.3. Regression results

As mentioned, as a valid alternative to purely data-driven methods, it is possible to report the results for different values of bandwidth $b$ to show the sensitivity of the estimates. Even when using local linear regression techniques, to a certain extent, we exploit the same idea of nonparametric RD approaches. Our estimates are presented in a unique table, where we show the sharp RD results for $b = 5$, $b = 10$, $b = 15$, and $b = 20$. Note that for each regression, we used a robust variance estimator.[^13] In Table 2, we are interested in the coefficient of the variable *sust*,[^14] which corresponds to the average treatment effect (ATE) for a corporate to be above average in terms of ESG performance. More precisely, it indicates the expected variation in terms of cost of debt (measured in percentage) for a European corporation with an above-average ESG score.

Even though we expect ATE to be negative and statistically significant for all windows, we only find positive estimates, except in the narrowest case (Table 2). Moreover, we do not find any statistically significant estimate. As we will see in greater detail in the next section, the results obtained do not empirically confirm that being above average in terms of ESG strategy, commitments, and actual performance—as measured by the ESG score from Refinitiv—has discrete positive effects in terms of cost of financing as measured by the corporate cost of debt, at least for European nonfinancial corporations.

Moreover, observing the estimates for the coefficient of the demeaned ESG score variable, we obtained estimates with positive signs, except in the narrowest window case. The estimates for this coefficient represent the marginal effect of increases in ESG score, ceteris paribus, on the cost of debt for companies with a below-average ESG score. Hence, even though not statistically significant, our estimates point to the idea—in three out of four cases—that a marginal positive effect for improved ESG performance is not found for corporates with below-average ESG scores as far as the cost of debt financing is concerned.

Instead, If we look at the group of companies with above-average ESG scores, we see that the estimate of the ESG score’s marginal effect on the cost of debt changes sign in every considered case apart from the bandwidth value $b = 10$.

Momentarily leaving considerations of statistical significance aside, it is noteworthy to consider that in three out of four cases, the estimate of the slope (the marginal effect of ESG performance on the cost of debt) changes sign when going from below-average to above-average companies in terms of ESG score.

### 5.4. Linear probability model

We now present the results for the linear probability model discussed earlier. As we note in Table 3, we obtained three positive estimates for the sharp RD coefficient in the wider windows and a negative estimate for the narrowest one. This means that, on average, being above the average in terms of ESG score does not reduce the probability of being above the average in terms of cost of debt financing. We did not find empirical evidence for a discontinuity in correspondence of the ESG score average. However, even if not statistically significant, three of our estimates indicate that being above the average in terms of ESG performance may lead to a higher probability of being above the average from a cost of debt perspective. Notably, the estimate from one of the narrowest windows (i.e., for $b = 5$) is negative, even if close to zero, thereby pointing to potential further investigation by future research. Nevertheless, we should keep in mind in this section all the limitations from which the linear probability model suffers, for example, we could obtain predicted values outside the interval [0; 1]. Hence, we use these results as a benchmark reference for our main model’s results.

In contrast with the results we will present when using leverage as the dependent variable, we found that moving from our untreated group to the treated one, i.e., going from below- to above-average corporates in terms of ESG score, the marginal effect on the probability of being above average in terms of cost of debt due to variations in ESG performance changes signs. More precisely, we found that for below-average companies, even if not statistically significant, increases in ESG performance led to decreases in the probability of being above average for the cost of debt (in three out of four cases). Then, when considering above-average companies, we obtain a change in sign sensitivity in three out of four cases, thereby leading to mixed evidence of estimated marginal effects that are positive for the two narrower windows and negative for the two wider cases. Overall, even with a lack of statistical significance, with this simple linear probability model, we again find a change in marginal effects moving from our untreated to treated group, as found in our model of reference. These results are even more important because we consider this linear probability model a benchmark for our model of reference.

### 5.5. Discussion

First, our results for the two wider windows align with the research that identifies an overall positive effect in terms of cost of financing for corporates that have a relatively better ESG performance, concerning above-average companies. Our findings align with those of Polbennikov et al. (2016), even though they adopt a more market-based approach and study the potential effect of ESG on corporate bonds in the secondary market.

Even though our results are not statistically significant, the signs of the estimates of the running variable coefficient for two out of four cases (i.e., the widest window cases, $b = 15$ and $b = 20$) for above-average ESG companies indicate that better ESG performance might have a positive effect in terms of the cost of debt for European nonfinancial corporates. The other two cases indicate the opposite relationship. Hence, these results are only partially in line with the research from the early 2000s, which focused more on corporate governance. Among all the results previously mentioned, we are particularly speaking about Klock et al. (2005). The mixed evidence in terms of the estimated signs we found may be due to the low number of observations for the narrowest windows: $N = 119$ and $N = 212$, respectively. Consequently, our results are only partially aligned with the most recent ESG development in the literature, which finds a negative link between ESG–CSR performance and the cost of debt financing, either from a capital markets or lending perspective. For example, we refer to Attig et al. (2013) and Kleimeier and Viehs (2018).

For companies with a relatively worse ESG performance (precisely those below the average), the sign of the estimates for the running variable are all positive (even if close to zero), except the narrowest window, i.e., for $b = 5$. Hence, there seems to be a different marginal effect for the two subgroups we identified in our sample: for companies performing relatively worse than average in ESG terms, no positive effect ensues in terms of the cost of debt financing when their ESG performance improves. A positive effect seems more evident, at least for the estimated signs (two out of four), for the subgroup represented by companies with relatively better ESG performance. As already mentioned, these results should not be interpreted causally due to the lack of statistical significance. However, their relevance may be found with deeper investigation by potential future research, not only for where the ATE and potential discrete jumps in the cost of debt are concerned but also for this potential difference in terms of marginal effects. The ultimate aim of our study is to support nonfinancial corporates in their decision-making process aimed at optimizing their financial management, which, in this specific setup, translates into the search for the most appropriate way to account for ESG performance effects on the cost of debt financing. In other words, we attempted to further investigate the relationship between the ESG space and the cost of debt, whose relevance is constantly growing, especially in light of the increasing popularity of ESG instruments in the debt capital markets.

For the statistical technique, it is not only the sharp RD design that allows us to check for possible discontinuities in correspondence with the selected cutoffs but also the type of model to be used in this setting. We recall that other approaches, such as fixed effects and instrumental variables, may lead to misleading inferences under these circumstances, as shown in D. S. Lee (2001). See D. S. Lee (2008) for a more formal analysis of the conditions needed to implement the RD design.

### 5.6. The effect on leverage

As a further robustness check, we report the results of our sharp RD model when using leverage[^15] as the dependent variable. We report this robustness check to confirm the potential causal chain we have identified. Through our model, we are trying to understand whether nonfinancial corporates’ lenders (i.e., mainly institutional investors and banks) allow a relatively lower cost of debt financing to firms with above-average ESG performance because of a series of factors, including potentially lower environmental risk. However, we do not use this model as one of our references because the level of leverage may be more under corporate control, thereby ending up being a more endogenous variable than the cost of debt and invalidating the sharp RD assumptions.

Hence, if this is the case, we should find approximately the same results those for cost of debt. For leverage, we report the results we showed for the cost of debt.[^16]

The estimates for our coefficient of interest, i.e., the coefficient of *sust*, are almost always negative (apart from the case in which $b = 5$) and not statistically significant (Table 4). Hence, the result is consistent with most of the estimated values for the ATE in our model of reference, the latter being positive for three out of four.

On the contrary, we do not find the same differences for estimated signs when moving from the below-average to above-average subgroups of our sample. In this case, there is mixed evidence in terms of the estimated sign for the coefficients of our running variable for companies with relatively worse ESG performance, with only the one from the widest window being statistically significant at a 5% level. Our reference model, meanwhile, had three positive and one negative estimate for the narrowest case, with none of them being statistically significant. Additionally, we do not find a change in the sign for the sensitivity of the dependent variable to changes in our running variable from our untreated to our treated group. In this case, the change in sign is only for the narrowest case.

Even if we cannot read these results causally because of a potential endogeneity problem, it is reassuring for our model that we obtained results and estimates consistent with the previously reported ones regarding our coefficient of interest. Moreover, we did not find the same pattern of behavior for the sensitivity coefficient that as for our model of reference when going from the untreated to the treated group, thereby leaving to future research the possibility for further investigation of this potential difference in behavior.

## 6. Conclusions

### 6.1. Main results

In our analysis, we did not find a statistically significant ATE for corporates presenting above-average ESG scores (i.e., above ca. 58). This means that we did not find robust, statistically significant, and positive effects in terms of cost of debt for corporates with an above-average ESG score. The relevance of our results should be considered from an academic and a strategic perspective as far as European nonfinancial corporations are concerned. However, as previously mentioned, the signs of the estimates for the two wider windows of the ESG score variable’s coefficient for our treated group (i.e., companies with above-average ESG performance) highlight a relatively positive effect in terms of the cost of debt for corporates that improve their ESG performance. Moreover, the estimates of the coefficient for the interaction between the *sust* and *ESG_score_d* variables indicate a potential difference in the marginal effect of ESG performance improvement for the two subgroups of our sample that are above and below the average companies in terms of the ESG score.

Overall, as discussed in the preceding section, our results are only partially consistent with both (1) the stream of research from the early 2000s focused on corporate governance and (2) more recent ESG developments, which find a negative link between ESG–CSR performance and the cost of debt.

### 6.2. Limitations

Our empirical analysis is based on two proxies other than the different control variables we considered (see Appendix A for the presentation of this alternative model). Hence, a potential limitation of our analysis is the approximation given by these proxies. The main strength of the Refinitiv ESG score remains the percentile ranking scoring methodology through which it is constructed, thereby allowing for relative comparisons among corporates. However, the risk of greenwashing should always be kept in mind when considering ESG performances and their evaluation. Overall, it may be useful to account for this risk comprehensively. In this regard, it may be insightful to consider the analysis by Yu, Luu, and Chenc (2020), who find that firm-level governance factors and cross-listing could effectively deter greenwashing.

Furthermore, we focused our analysis only on the ESG score by Refinitiv, whereas different providers of ESG performance metrics may be considered to test the robustness of our results. This aspect may be especially relevant because different ESG score providers may not share the same idea about a firm’s ESG performance (e.g., Dorfleitner, Halbritter, & Nguyen, 2015). Moreover, it may be useful to consider the type of incentives for firms that request different types of ESG ratings. For a general example of this idea, even though related to credit ratings, see Mahlmann (2009).

For our proxy of the cost of debt, we used an accounting proxy with all its limitations. Dhaliwal et al. (2008) found a direct link between nonaudit fees and the cost of debt for corporates in the investment grade space, using a sample of 560 new debt issues, thereby highlighting the potential fragility of our proxy. Park and Wu (2009) found significant effects of financial restatements on the debt market, focusing on the secondary loan market. In addition, we did not consider any measure to account for the quality of accounting reporting by the firms in our sample and the related potential impact of accounting quality on the cost of debt construction. For example, see Bharath, Sunder, and Sunder (2008) for a comprehensive study on the significance of accounting quality for a borrower. Hence, to further test the robustness of our model, the accuracy of the accounting proxy used for the cost of debt could be evaluated and improved.

We used Datastream as a data provider, building the proxy for the cost of debt similar to Izzo and Magnanelli (2012). Alternatively, we could have considered the ratio of interest expense to an average measure of outstanding debt. See Eliwa, Aboud, & Saleh (2021), Gray, Koh, and Tong (2009), and Francis et al. (2005) for some examples of the application of this alternative approach.

In addition, future research may further expand on the sharp RD technique we used. First, polynomial techniques may be employed instead of local linear regressions. For an interesting method to select polynomial orders in an RD design, see Pei et al. (2021). Gelman and Imbens (2019) recommend not using polynomials with too-high orders when using the RD design. Moreover, an expansion of our model may account for more than one cutoff, for example, corresponding to the first and third quartiles of the ESG score distribution. For more details on the potential implementation of consistent and asymptotically normal estimators in this setup, see, for example, Bertanha (2020). Our setup may be further expanded by implementing a fuzzy RD design if we prefer to assume that there is no clear, sharp cutoff in the cost of debt.

Lastly, as already discussed, future research may further investigate the potential difference in marginal effects of an improvement in ESG performance on the cost of debt between the two subgroups we considered, i.e., above and below the average companies from an ESG score point of view.

To conclude, the robustness of our results may be further tested by expanding our dataset from a geographical and temporal perspective.

*   leverage measured as total debt as a percentage of common equity[^18] to control for the current level of company indebtedness;

## Appendix C. The ESG score

We report some additional details about how the ESG score by Refinitiv (i.e., our running variable) is constructed. Besides giving additional color on how the score is computed, it is important to consider it as a check regarding the validity of the assumption on the continuity of our dependent variable. For an RD setup, we need $E[Y_i(1)|X_i = x]$ and $E[Y_i(0)|X_i = x]$ to be continuous in $x$—where $X$ is our running variable.

As mentioned, the percentile rank scoring methodology is a feature of ESG score computation. This is only one of many key features of the ESG score computations. Below, we report the calculation principles that can be found in Refinitiv (2021), which relate only to ESG scores.[^19]

1.  using ESG materiality weightings on a scale of 1 to 10 to differentiate the importance of ESG factors considered across industries;
2.  a weighting system that puts disclosure at the center—not reporting highly material data points negatively affects a company’s score;
3.  using industry and country benchmarks at the data point levels;
4.  using a scoring methodology based on percentile ranking.

For completeness, we synthetically report the description associated with each quartile.

*   **First quartile:** poor relative ESG performance and an insufficient degree of transparency;
*   **Second quartile:** satisfactory relative ESG performance and a moderate degree of transparency;
*   **Third quartile:** good relative ESG performance and an above-average degree of transparency;
*   **Fourth quartile:** excellent relative ESG performance and a high degree of transparency.

Hence, given the weighting methodology used by Refinitiv and the percentile ranking technique, we can consider the cost of company debt as continuous for companies with above-average and below-average ESG scores. In particular, it may be useful to remember how we built our dependent variable as the ratio of total interest expense to total debt of a company, which also allows us to consider the continuity assumption verified and, hence, apply our RD approach.
