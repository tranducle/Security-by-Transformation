### ABSTRACT
Institutional factors play a critical role in shaping the relationship between companies’ environmental, social, and governance (ESG) practices and their financial performance. This paper investigates how institutional factors influence the relationship between ESG practices and the cost of debt for non-financial firms globally, focusing on seven dimensions of governance quality and environmental resilience. Findings show that companies with superior ESG ratings operating in countries with high institutional quality benefit from a reduction in their cost of debt. Conversely, firms in countries with lower institutional quality do not experience the same advantage, underscoring the importance of effective governance and environmental resilience in fostering sustainable business practices. Results are robust to tests addressing endogeneity concerns and possible confounding factors, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings provide insights for practitioners, financial institutions, and policymakers, highlighting the need to consider the institutional context when assessing the impact of ESG factors on financial outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

The interaction between businesses and stakeholders is mediated by institutional factors, the regulatory, behavioral, and cultural rules that provide stability to the social lives of individuals and organizations within a specific context (Lee 2011; Scott 2008; Yu and Choi 2016). Institutional factors such as environmental protection laws, legal systems, and anti-corruption measures are relevant for the financing of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) practices, as they determine the extent to which companies can engage in and commit to sustainability standards.

At the same time, the transition towards more responsible socio-economic models requires an increasing contribution from companies in terms of sustainable investments, a significant portion of which should be supported by the financial system. Nowadays, financial institutions are asked by different stakeholders to incorporate sustainability indicators into their business evaluation models, recognizing lower risk associated with companies that demonstrate stronger ESG performance. Therefore, the link between companies’ financial performance and sustainability practices needs to be contextualized within the appropriate institutional framework in which they operate.

The relationship between ESG practices and financial performance has been studied from various perspectives, including stock performance, the cost of equity, and the role of leverage (see Atz et al. 2023; Coelho, Jayantilal, and Ferreira 2023, for recent surveys). When it comes to firm financing, many studies have found that companies more focused on sustainability on average obtain a lower Cost of Debt (CoD) because financial institutions recognize their lower riskiness in environmental, social, and governance aspects (e.g. see Apergis, Poufinas, and Antonopoulos 2022; Cheng, Ioannou, and Serafeim 2014; Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Erragragui 2018; Gangi et al. 2021; Gracia and Siregar 2021). However, this conclusion is not unanimous and is open to – even contradictory – shifts, particularly depending on the countries under analysis (Gonçalves, Dias, and Barros 2022). This national differentiation suggests that there may be some institutional factors at the country level that might influence the relationship between sustainability and financial costs.

We consider institutional factors in the analysis of the interactions between the cost of debt and the level of sustainability in the ESG perspective since institutions can directly influence both of these dimensions (Pinheiro et al. 2023). The academic literature on the influence of institutional factors on the relationship between ESG performances and the CoD is still scarce. In the European context, countries’ stakeholder orientation increments the effect of ESG performance on the CoD (Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021). For ASEAN countries, where regulation on sustainability issues is still under development or ineffective, firms’ sustainability practices act as substitutes for country sustainability when considering their effect on the firms’ cost of debt (Gracia and Siregar 2021). Also, the type of financial system influences the relation, since in market-oriented financial systems the benefits of ESG performance on the CoD is lower than in bank-oriented systems (Alves and Meneses 2024). As Shin et al. (2023) highlight, integrating micro – (firm-)level factors with macro-level ones is crucial when studying the effect of sustainable practices on business performance.

Our work aims to fill this gap by empirically exploring the link between ESG performance and the CoD in different institutional settings for non-financial firms worldwide. Our central research question is: *What is the role of institutions in mediating the influence of ESG factors on the perceived riskiness of companies and, therefore, on their CoD?* This question is of the utmost importance as it helps us to understand how financial institutions price information on ESG practices based on the quality of the institutions that form the context in which all stakeholders live and interact, and how policymakers could support credit conditions associated with the most sustainable companies.

To answer this question, we examine the institutional factors that account for the quality of governance at the country level. Specifically, we evaluate the six dimensions provided by the World Bank: rule of law, control of corruption, government effectiveness, political stability, regulatory quality, and voice and accountability. We complement these dimensions with an additional country-level institutional factor that measures the ability of countries to respond and adapt to environmental risks. Specifically, we use an indicator of (resilience to) environmental vulnerability produced by the IMF, based on a methodology developed by the Joint Research Centre of the European Commission (Marin-Ferrer, Vernaccini, and Poljansek 2017). Then, we distinguish countries based on their level of institutional quality (high vs low). This differentiation allows us to examine whether the connection between ESG scores and CoD is influenced by differences across institutional contexts. We find that companies with superior ESG ratings operating in countries with higher institutional quality experience a significant reduction in their CoD, while this effect fades in contexts with lower institutional quality.

This work contributes to the academic debate on the ESG-CoD relationship in different ways. First, we address different dimensions of institutional quality, to consider how they could shape the relationship between ESG practices and the CoD. Previous literature focused on aggregate dimensions, such as countries’ stakeholder orientation (Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021) or financial systems’ market orientation (Alves and Meneses 2024). Second, our approach builds on previous research by incorporating a focus on multiple aspects of countries’ government institutional quality and their resilience to environmental risks – factors that are not widely covered in existing literature while being closely tied to the ESG pillars. Third, we take a global approach, and do not consider only single countries such as the US (Erragragui 2018), India (Malik and Kashiramka 2024), and Spain (Reverte 2012), or groups of countries, such as the EU (Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Gerwanski 2020), and BRICS (Fandella, Sergi, and Sironi 2023). Taking a global approach helps identify which institutional factors influence the effectiveness of better ESG practices on financial performance, considering the significant heterogeneity in institutional quality across the world, as opposed to focusing only on relatively homogeneous groups of countries, such as European or developing nations. Finally, given the different responses to the COVID-19 outbreak across countries and institutional contexts, we examine whether the role of institutional frameworks in the ESG-CoD relationship is influenced by exceptional high-risk circumstances such as the pandemic.

Our contribution to the empirical literature emphasizes that better ESG performance is a sufficient condition for achieving a lower CoD only in contexts with good institutional quality. This highlights the importance of good governance quality and the ability to be resilient to environmental risks as enabling factors for sustainability practices adopted by companies. We highlight that firms operating in better institutional settings enjoy lower costs of debt when they have better ESG performance. This could be explained on the one hand by the fact that firms in a high-institutional quality context can signal themselves as less risky through better ESG performance, whereas firms in a low-institutional quality context cannot. High institutional quality affects the reduction of information asymmetries between companies and their stakeholder, which could be the main channel that is highlighted by our results. On the other hand, it could be that in a low-quality institutional context, investments in ESG practices are (i) not correctly evaluated or directly connected to a reduction of corporate risk or an increase in the firm value by investors or (ii) are not accurately incentivized by the institutional context.

For these reasons, our findings are valuable for practitioners and financial institutions, offering insights to guide the assessment of the impact of ESG factors on a company’s CoD across varying institutional frameworks. Additionally, our research underscores the need for better integration of ESG practices in the assessment of the creditworthiness of companies in low institutional quality countries.

This is particularly relevant in the context of the global push for sustainability following the 2015 Paris Agreement, as many countries have started to implement actions and strategies to promote sustainable businesses and their financial conditions, with the dual aim of supporting corporate operations and achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). In this context, regulation and institutional factors play a key role. For instance, the development of the green bond market varies significantly across countries, with macroeconomic and institutional factors driving their issuance volume (Tolliver, Keeley, and Managi 2020). Furthermore, evidence suggests that green bonds are issued with lower spreads than conventional ones (Teti et al. 2022; Caramichael and Rapp 2024). These findings highlight how institutional quality can influence the financial outcomes of sustainability initiatives. Therefore, this paper could serve as a guide for policymakers to foster sustainable development by improving the quality of institutions within their countries.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the main related empirical literature and introduces the research hypotheses. Section 3 describes the data and the empirical framework. Section 4 discusses the main findings, while Section 5 presents a battery of robustness tests. Section 6 concludes the paper.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

In this section, we review the relevant academic literature in three stages. First, we provide an overview of studies examining the effect of ESG practices and performances on firms’ financial outcomes, with a particular focus on the cost of debt. Second, we analyze the literature on the influence of institutional factors on market conditions, firms’ financing conditions, and ESG practices. Finally, we identify the research gap concerning the role of institutional factors in the linkage between ESG factors and the CoD, framing the research hypotheses that will be tested in the following sections. Table 1 provides a summary of the key literature streams and highlights the research gap addressed in this paper.

### 2.1. ESG practices and the cost of debt

Over the past two decades, numerous empirical studies have explored the link between Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) / ESG practices and firms’ investment decisions and performance. While some investigations focused on the relationship between CSR/ESG and stock market performance (Capelli, Ielasi, and Russo 2021; Feng et al. 2022; Hawn, Chatterji, and Mitchell 2018; Serafeim and Yoon 2022), our paper aligns with literature examining the impact of ESG practices on corporate finance, specifically by exploring how changes in the CoD are driven by factors that may affect a company’s risk profile and how investors perceive its probability of default. This body of literature often draws on stakeholder theory, information asymmetry theory, and institutional theory to explain the relationship between ESG performance and the CoD. From these perspectives, firms engage in sustainable practices to operate legitimately within society, reduce sustainability-related risks, and meet the expectations of various stakeholders. Moreover, as sustainability issues have material financial implications, investments in ESG practices can also help firms lower their financial risk. By adopting sustainable practices, companies signal lower risk to investors, thereby differentiating themselves from those unable to adopt such practices.

The empirical literature on this topic has produced mixed results. One stream of research has specifically investigated the relationship between ESG practices and CoD, with most studies finding a negative relationship (Cheng, Ioannou, and Serafeim 2014; Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Raimo et al. 2021). This is often attributed to the ability of companies investing in ESG initiatives to mitigate sustainability-related risks, thereby enhancing their financial stability. As an example, from an environmental perspective, studies have highlighted that air pollution and carbon emissions increase the CoD (Bauer and Hann 2010; Jung, Herbohn, and Clarkson 2018; Tan, Chan, and Chen 2022), as higher emissions and pollution are associated with greater risks of litigation, regulatory non-compliance, and negative social perceptions from the communities (Fauser and Utz 2021). In the social dimension, firms with higher levels of social capital or responsible human capital management have been found to enjoy lower bank loan spreads and CoD (Hasan et al. 2017; Gangi et al. 2021). Similarly, from the governance pillar, strong corporate governance practices can lower the CoD by reducing agency cost, improving monitoring, and decreasing information asymmetry (Lorca, Sánchez-Ballesta, and García-Meca 2011; Ghouma, Ben-Nasr, and Yan 2018). Moreover, greater compensation for outside directors through stock and options has been shown to reduce the agency costs of debt (Ertugrul and Hegde 2008).

Other studies have identified either a positive link between ESG factors and CoD (e.g. Fandella, Sergi, and Sironi 2023), or mixed outcomes (Dunne and McBrayer 2019; Gigante and Manglaviti 2022; Gonçalves, Dias, and Barros 2022; Sharfman and Fernando 2008), also depending on the specific countries considered in the analysis. For instance, Sharfman and Fernando (2008) find a positive effect of environmental risk management on the CoD, offering several explanations: inefficiency stemming from compliance with environmental risk management frameworks, the need for higher debt levels to fund environmental investments, and the potential short-term riskiness of such investments. In addition, Heinkel, Kraus, and Zechner (2001) show that heavily polluting firms face a higher cost of capital, which may incentivize them to adopt more socially responsible practices, depending on the share of ‘green’ investors in the market.

The inconsistency in these findings appears to be linked to an incomplete identification of the relationship, potentially due to the omission of key national-level factors. This highlights a research gap, as most studies do not account for the institutional context or the influence of institutional factors. This paper aims to address this gap by exploring the intersection between ESG-CoD dynamics and the role of institutional factors.

### 2.2. The role of institutional factors

The quality of institutions shapes the national framework in which companies operate, significantly influencing their interaction with various stakeholders (e.g. Lee 2011; Yu and Choi 2016). A high-quality institutional environment helps reduce the risk perceived by stakeholders, particularly financial institutions, which in turn lowers the CoD (Álvarez-Botas and González-Méndez 2019). By establishing a framework of rules governing interactions among different agents, institutions provide stability to both individuals and corporate behavior (Scott 2008). From a business perspective, this stability is crucial, as it enhances key corporate traits such as transparency in corporate governance (Doidge, Karolyi, and Stulz 2007), the efficiency of accounting standards (La Porta et al. 1998), the cost of equity (Hail and Leuz 2006), as well as the CoD (Tee and Teoh 2024).

The quality of governance in a country significantly influences firms’ financial conditions through various mechanisms. Strong democratic institutions, characterized by regular elections, freedom of speech, and press freedom, enhance transparency and accountability, fostering public trust and effective governance (Rose-Ackerman and Palifka 2016). This leads to lower borrowing costs, as investors perceive reduced risks in stable and democratic environments (Rock 2009). Efficient government bureaucracy also reduces corporate risks, lowering the cost of borrowing (La Porta et al. 1998). Similarly, the rule of law, enforced by independent judiciaries, protects property rights and ensures fair dispute resolution, contributing to a lower CoD (La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, and Shleifer 2006; Van Rijckeghem and Weder 2001). Companies operating in countries with a stronger quality of governance – where workers’ rights are upheld and commercial contracts are easily enforceable – are generally perceived as lower-risk entities, resulting in reduced debt costs (Tee and Teoh 2024). For instance, robust labor unions can dissuade management from participating in risky endeavors (Chen, Kacperczyk, and Ortiz-Molina 2012), while favorable employee relations bolster a firm’s creditworthiness and reduce debt expenditures (Bauer, Derwall, and Hann 2009). In countries with stronger labor rights, however, weaker contract enforceability and limited legal shareholder protection can lead to higher debt levels, particularly for low-growth firms (Gul 2001; Liang and Li 2022).

Conversely, weaker political institutions are often associated with higher levels of corruption (Lederman, Loayza, and Soares 2005; Rajan and Zingales 2003) which can negatively impact firms. High corruption levels increase the risk of asset diversion from debtholders (Johnson et al. 2000), while companies operating in countries with stronger control of corruption tend to experience lower CoD (Çam and Özer 2022; Qi, Roth, and Wald 2010), partly by reducing risks associated to information asymmetry (Bermpei, Kalyvas, and Leonida 2021). Corruption also undermines the enforcement of contractual rights, raising default risk and debt costs (Mauro, Medas, and Fournier 2019).

Last, the urgency of the climate crisis and the importance of transitioning to more sustainable development models have led to countries with resilient institutional frameworks being considered safer and less risky, better equipped to handle climate change and environmental risks. Empirical studies exploring the relationship between environmental and financial risks suggest that environmental penalties can increase debt costs (Ding, Appolloni, and Shahzad 2022; Bauer and Hann 2010; Chava 2014; Graham, Maher, and Northcut 2001; Schneider 2011). However, strong institutional factors, such as robust infrastructures supporting social and physical investments, can mitigate climate-related debt costs, stabilizing the CoD in more vulnerable countries (Kling et al. 2018).

While high-quality institutional frameworks can reduce business risks, growing evidence indicates they can also promote the spread of better ESG performance and practices. One common explanation is that companies operating in regions with weaker institutions have to demonstrate better ESG disclosure and practices to be recognized as sustainable by their stakeholders (Duque-Grisales and Aguilera-Caracuel 2021; Rathert 2016; Singhania and Saini 2023). In such contexts, private companies can serve as a relevant mechanism for driving ESG improvements, even in contexts with low-quality institutions (Kühn, Stiglbauer, and Fifka 2018; Pinheiro et al. 2023). For instance, studies have found a negative relationship between ESG factors and corruption levels (Yu and Van Luu 2021; Khalid et al. 2022) with this effect observable even at sub-national levels (Ucar and Staer 2020) and stronger than other societal norms like social capital or religiosity (Hossain and Kryzanowski 2021). Moreover, anti-corruption policies have been shown to help enhance ESG performance (Kong, Shu, and Wang 2021).

Another reason is that companies operating in stronger institutional contexts are subject to greater scrutiny from investors, which drives them to further increase their efforts to improve their ESG disclosure (Huang et al. 2022). For instance, Cahan et al. (2016) find that firms operating in countries with stronger governance frameworks tend to exhibit higher levels of CSR disclosure quality, suggesting a positive link between national governance and ESG practices. Similarly, de Villiers and Marques (2016) show that in countries where investors can more effectively voice their concerns and regulations are more stringent and better enforced by the government, CSR disclosures tend to be more informative. Furthermore, companies with higher ESG scores are expected to be better equipped to respond to shocks related to natural or environmental crises (Huang and Lin 2022), and managers of companies more exposed to environmental risks tend to increase transparency in providing information for sustainability ratings (Huang et al. 2022). Overall, these studies support the notion that robust country governance frameworks incentivize firms to adopt more sustainable business practices.

### 2.3. ESG practices, institutional factors, and the cost of debt

As discussed in the previous paragraph, both ESG practices and financial risks are directly influenced by institutional factors. Therefore, it is crucial to consider these factors when analyzing the interactions between ESG ratings and the CoD. Given the lack of consensus regarding the relationship between ESG practices and the CoD, an in-depth analysis on how, and which, institutional factors amplify or moderate this relation is necessary to shed light on the mechanisms through which sustainability-linked risks translate into financial risks.

To the best of our knowledge, few studies have explored the institutional context as a primary determinant of the relationship between ESG practices and financial performance, particularly concerning the CoD. Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh (2021), in their study of the relationship between ESG performance and the CoD in the EU, incorporate countries’ stakeholder orientation as an aggregate indicator that encompasses various factors, including legal environment, environmental laws, and labor protection. They find that better ESG practices reduce firms’ CoD, particularly in countries with a strong stakeholder orientation. In other words, firms are incentivized to invest in ESG practices when stakeholder groups exert pressure on them. In contrast, Gracia and Siregar (2021) report limited findings for ASEAN countries, indicating that the interaction between a country’s sustainability performance and firms’ ESG practices does not significantly affect corporate CoD. Alves and Meneses (2024) show that the relationship between ESG performance and the CoD is stronger in countries with bank-based financial systems, in line with the stakeholder theory, compared to market-based financial systems, where interactions between lender and borrower tend to be more transactional. Our paper takes a different approach by examining institutional quality rather than country characteristics such as financial systems orientation. Last, while Shin et al. (2023) examines the impact of the interaction between institutional factors and ESG performance on firms’ profitability, our focus is on how this interaction influences the CoD, which is directly linked to financial risk.

The heterogeneity in the results in the literature may stem from the varying institutional factors considered, which can shape the relationship between ESG practices and the CoD in distinct ways and with differing magnitudes. A country’s resilience to environmental vulnerability plays a key role in mitigating the physical environmental risk that firms face. Therefore, this resilience could be more relevant in explaining changes in the ESG-CoD relationship than other general institutional variables, such as the Rule of Law or Control of Corruption, which address different types of risks.

The inconsistency in the direction of the relationship between ESG factors and the CoD observed in the literature may be attributed to the neglect of institutional factors as a relevant channel influencing this relationship, particularly through the lens of the Agency Theory and the Stakeholder Theory. We propose that stronger ESG measures reflect a) firms’ effort to reduce information asymmetry and agency costs, and b) their consideration of all relevant stakeholders, resulting in improved financing conditions. Therefore, we postulate the first research hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 1:** Firms with higher ESG ratings experience a lower cost of debt.

Based on the heterogeneous results in the extant literature regarding the relevance of institutional factors on the ESG-CoD relationship, and drawing from legitimacy and stakeholder theories, we propose two alternative research hypotheses.

First, we anticipate that companies operating in countries with high institutional quality – measured by factors such as greater resilience to environmental vulnerability, higher rule of law score, better control of corruption, higher government effectiveness, higher political stability, better regulatory quality, and better stakeholders’ voice and accountability score – may encounter a lower cost of debt. With this regard, we expect that better institutional quality enhances the effectiveness of sustainable practices in reducing the CoD. In this view, institutional quality is an enabler of the relationship.

**Hypothesis 2:** Ceteris paribus, high institutional quality positively influences the relationship between ESG practices and the Cost of Debt. Henceforth, in countries with higher institutional quality, superior corporate ESG performance is associated with a lower cost of debt.

However, according to signaling theory, we also suggest the alternative hypothesis that in environments with low institutional quality, firms may employ strong ESG practices to signal their status as low-risk borrowers, thus benefiting from a reduced CoD.

**Hypothesis 3:** Ceteris paribus, low institutional quality positively influences the relationship between ESG practices and the Cost of Debt. Henceforth, in countries with low institutional quality, superior corporate ESG performance is associated with a lower cost of debt.

## 3. Data and methodology

### 3.1. Data

We use a unique dataset covering 4,691 companies, at the worldwide level observed from 2007 to 2023.$^1$ The dataset is the result of a matching between different databases covering: (i) ESG ratings, from Morgan Stanley Capital Indices (MSCI) ESG, which includes firm-level information based on annual sustainability reports, corporate governance statements, specialized (government and NGO) datasets, and (global and local) media sources; (ii) financial statements from Orbis – Bureau van Dijk commercial database; (iii) institutional factors, that is, Resilience to Vulnerability (IMF INFORM), Rule of Law (World Bank), Control of Corruption (World Bank), Government Effectiveness (World Bank), Political Stability (World Bank), Regulatory Quality (World Bank), and Voice and Accountability (World Bank); (iv) country-level indicators to control for economic and population growth (World Bank), financial development (IMF), and gender gap (World Economic Forum). The final dataset is structured as an unbalanced panel for a total of more than 30,000 firm-year observations.$^2$ For each company, our matched dataset includes information on our main variables of interest, namely the ESG ratings and the CoD, as well as relevant company-level and country-level indicators.$^3$

### 3.2. Methodology

To investigate the relationship between ESG performances and the cost of debt, the following panel data fixed effect model specification describes our baseline estimation:

$$CoD_{it} = \alpha + \beta ESG_{it-1} + \gamma X_{it-1} + \delta Z_{it-1} + \phi_i + \phi_t + \phi_c + \phi_s + \phi_c \times trend + \phi_s \times trend + \epsilon_{it} \quad (1)$$

where $i$ indicates companies, $t$ refers to the years of the sample, $s$ and $c$ the sector and country of companies, respectively. $CoD$, the dependent variable is the (natural logarithm of the) ratio between financial expenditures and the 1-year lagged total debt. The higher its value, the higher the cost with respect to the level of debt. $ESG$ is the main regressor in our model accounting for the ESG performance of sample companies. $ESG$ is a continuous variable ranging from 0 (worst-in-class) and 10 (best-in-class). $X$ is a vector of time-varying control variables at the company level. In particular, consistently with previous literature, we complement the model by including Total Assets, Interest Coverage Ratio, Leverage Ratio, Current Ratio, and Return on Assets to account for heterogeneous characteristics of sample companies as their size, their ability to repay their debts, their level of indebtedness, their ability to pay short-term obligations, and their profitability, respectively. $Z$ is a vector of time-varying control variables at the country level, accounting for economic growth (GDP growth), financial development (IFM Financial Development Index), demographic changes (Population growth), and gender disparities (World Economic Forum Gender Gap Index). The main regressor and the whole vector of control variables are one-year lagged to cope with the possible endogeneity of the relationship due to reverse causality.$^4$ Moreover, we include a set of company, sector, time, and country fixed effects, as well as country-trend and sector-trend fixed effects ($\phi$). In alternative specifications of the model, we substitute country-trend and sector-trend fixed effects with country-year and sector-year fixed effects. In this latter case, we exclude from the estimation country-level control variables which would be collinear with these fixed effects. Lastly, $\epsilon_{it}$, is the error term, clustered at the company level.

To account for institutional channels and their possible effect on the relation between ESG practices and the firm’s cost of debt ($\beta$), we estimate model (1) across various sub-samples, creating a differentiation between companies operating within high-institutional quality contexts and those within low institutional quality contexts. Specifically, we consider the median of the country distribution for each institutional factor as the threshold to identify companies operating in countries with high (low) quality institutions. This approach allows us to identify potential variations in the impact of ESG practices on the CoD based on the quality of the institutional framework within which companies operate.

## 4. Findings and discussion

We begin by presenting findings on the relationship between ESG performance and the CoD in our sample. This helps to frame our baseline analysis of the role of institutional factors within this relationship. The results are summarized in Table 2.

In the first specification (column 1), which includes only company and year fixed effects, we find that a higher ESG score is negatively associated with the CoD, with the estimate being significant at the 10% level. In column 2, we add country and sector fixed effects to the specification. The estimated effect is – 0.017, statistically significant at the 10% level. The next two specifications further extend the model. Column 3 includes interaction terms for country-year and sector-year fixed effects, while column 4 includes country-trend and sector-trend fixed effects. In both cases, the estimated coefficient for ESG is – 0.022 and statistically significant. The final two specifications (columns 5 and 6) represent the full models. We now present the findings of our baseline analysis conducted on the sub-samples of companies operating in countries with higher (lower) levels of institutional quality attributes. In particular, Table 3 documents the results of the estimation of Equation (1) conducted on companies operating in countries with different levels of resilience to environmental vulnerability (Columns 1 and 2), rule of law (Columns 3 and 4), control of corruption (Columns 5 and 6), government effectiveness (Columns 7 and 8), political stability (Columns 9 and 10), regulatory quality (Columns 11 and 12), and voice and accountability (Columns 13 and 14). This allows us to test whether and how institutional factors influence the relationship between ESG and CoD. Relatedly, this can provide insights into the macro context in which ESG practices are expected to lead to higher potential reductions in CoD, with implications for corporate investment and financing strategy.

We find that higher ESG scores are significantly associated with a reduction in the CoD for firms in countries with greater resilience to environmental vulnerability, stronger rule of law, better control of corruption, higher government effectiveness, better regulatory quality, and stronger voice and accountability, with estimated negative coefficients ranging from – 0.023 to – 0.032, all significant at the 5% level. In contrast, no significant effects are observed for lower levels of these institutional factors. An exception is political stability, where the coefficients are slightly insignificant for both sub-samples (Columns 9 and 10), indicating no notable differences. In this respect, our research hypothesis 2 is accepted for all the institutional factors considered, except for political stability, while hypothesis 3 cannot be accepted for any of the institutional variables analyzed.

These findings support the hypothesis that companies benefit from reduced CoD associated with improved ESG performance in markets where enabling institutional factors are present, both in terms of resilience to environmental risks and quality of governance. From the perspective of capital providers, investors, and financial institutions recognize the risk-reducing value of ESG practices in the assessment of companies’ creditworthiness when institutional quality is high.

With this regard, our results are in line with institutional theory and stakeholder theory. As institutional factor determines how corporates interact with society, better institutional quality is associated with greater firms’ transparency towards relevant stakeholders, including financial intermediaries and investors. Given that institutional and governance quality significantly reduce firms’ risk (see, e.g. La Porta et al., 1998; La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, and Shleifer 2006; Van Rijckeghem and Weder 2001; Bermpei, Kalyvas, and Leonida 2021; Tee and Teoh 2024), they also influence corporates’ ESG practices, leading, for example, to higher disclosure quality requirements for firms (Cahan et al. 2016; de Villiers and Marques 2016). The combination of these two aspects leads to our empirical findings, which are in line with Shin, Moon, and Kang (2023), who show that cultural aspects of countries are relevant in explaining the relationship between corporate profitability and ESG performance. Conversely, higher political stability does not seem to be an enabler factor of the relationship between ESG practices and the CoD, although political stability is conducive to financial development (Chletsos and Sintos 2024). In countries where political stability is high, the relationship between ESG performance and the CoD may be weak because the risk reduction associated with higher ESG practices may not be relevant relative to the lower risk that exists in this context. In other words, the effect of ESG on reducing the CoD may be less pronounced when political stability risks are already low.

Conversely, financial institutions do not seem to recognize a differential CoD based on the ESG performance of companies in countries with low levels of institutional quality. As a matter of fact, our results can be explained by two reasons. First, in a low-quality institutional context, investments in ESG practices may not be properly valued by investors and may not be directly linked to a reduction in corporate risk or an increase in firm value. Investors may prioritize other variables not directly linked to ESG practices when assessing the creditworthiness of borrowers. On the other hand, investment in ESG practices may not be effectively incentivized by the institutional environment, which may incentivize other objectives, and hence corporates’ investment in ESG practices may be seen as sub-optimal. Although firms operating in countries with weaker institutions should engage in better ESG disclosure and practices in order to be recognized as sustainable by their stakeholders (Duque-Grisales and Aguilera-Caracuel 2021; Rathert 2016; Singhania and Saini 2023), this engagement could be very costly, which could lead to different outcomes in terms of the level of corporate risk.

Our results have different implications for policymakers, investors, and firms. First and foremost, since institutional factors are drivers of the relationship between ESG practices and the CoD, our results highlight that policymakers should prioritize the improvement of the institutional context, in which firms operate to help companies improve their ESG performance. A robust institutional environment provides the foundation for firms to invest in ESG practices, but also makes companies accountable for their lack of sustainability practices, specifically when companies’ behavior is harmful to society. When companies operate in a well-regulated environment, they are required to adhere to standards that promote environmental sustainability, social responsibility, and ethical governance. This reduces the likelihood of harmful corporate behavior, such as pollution, exploitation, or corruption. Hence, in the context of low institutional quality, policies to improve the institutional framework will also help firms that invest in ESG practices and achieve better sustainable performance to be recognized for their effort with a reduction of their borrowing costs.

Our findings also call for improved actions by investors and capital providers. While low institutional quality may not properly incentivize companies’ ESG practices and also makes it difficult to assess companies’ ESG performance, investors and financial institutions still play a key role in allocating capital to more sustainable companies. In particular, investors could fill the gap left by the low quality of institutions by adopting high ESG standards and criteria when assessing companies’ creditworthiness – and thus in their own financing decisions – and providing incentives for companies to invest in these standards. As ESG practices are recognized as a risk mitigation tool, investors and financial institutions can not only channel their capital to companies with strong ESG profiles but also incentivize those with low ESG performance to improve their sustainability levels through appropriate financial support.

Finally, our results have important managerial implications. First, our results call for effective investments in ESG practices, which not only improve the sustainability of firms and contribute to the achievement of the SDGs but also help firms in high institutional quality contexts to raise debt capital at lower cost. In this context, financial institutions can correctly assess ESG practices as crucial risk-mitigating activities and therefore include ESG assessment in the creditworthiness assessment of borrowers. Second, managers of companies operating in countries with low institutional quality should be aware that investing in ESG practices does not directly and immediately translate into a reduction in their borrowing costs. Managers should therefore take a long-term perspective and understand that the value of ESG investments lies not only in short-term cost reductions but also in the mitigation of future risks. As stakeholders in this context may not have the expertise to evaluate companies’ ESG activities, managers must proactively and transparently report on ESG practices through high-quality disclosures or certifications to demonstrate the long-term financial value of their sustainability initiatives.

## 5. Robustness tests

### 5.1. Geography and sectors

To test the robustness of our baseline findings to country-specific behaviors, we replicate the estimation of Equation (1) by excluding one country at a time.$^6$ The estimated coefficients and their 90% confidence interval, presented in Figure 1, document full alignment with the baseline findings, that is a negative and significant relationship between the ESG score and the CoD for countries showing higher levels of institutional quality. Therefore, we can conclude that our main results are not geographically driven.

Following the same approach, we estimate again Equation (1) by excluding one sector at a time to test for potential outlying sector-specific behaviors. Figure 2 plots the estimated coefficients and their 90% confidence interval, and documents that our baseline findings are robust to the exclusion of specific sectors.

### 5.2. Interaction terms

For the sake of robustness, rather than estimating Equation (1) on two sub-samples based on the institutional quality of the country, we replicate our analysis by augmenting Equation (1) with an interaction term between our main regressor, ESG, and a binary indicator for each of the seven adopted proxies for institutional factors, INST. These indicators are equal to 1 for countries showing lower levels of institutional quality and 0 otherwise. To confirm our baseline findings, we expect (i) the estimated coefficient for ESG to be negative and statistically significant, and (ii) the linear combination of ESG and the interaction terms of ESG with each institutional factor binary indicator to be not statistically significant. Table 4 shows the results of the seven estimations, which depict a negative relationship between superior ESG scores and the CoD in the baseline case (i.e. higher levels of institutional quality), except for political stability as per the baseline estimations. The coefficients of the interaction terms, ESG × INST, show opposite signs to those of ESG, and the overall effect for the case of companies based in low-quality institutional countries captured by the linear combination is not significant, confirming our previous results.

### 5.4. Endogeneity issues

We now examine two potential sources of endogeneity in our model: reverse causality and omitted variables. Our baseline model addresses possible endogeneity from reverse causality by incorporating lagged variables. To further test for reverse causality, we estimate Equation (1), where we swap the dependent variable, CoD, with the main regressor, ESG, while keeping the structure of the control variables and fixed effects unchanged. As before, all independent variables, including CoD, are lagged by one year. If a statistically significant coefficient were to be found for CoD, it would raise concerns about the validity of our baseline estimate, indicating potential endogeneity due to reverse causality. However, the results of this estimation, reported in Column 1 of Table 6, show a non-statistically significant relationship, confirming that the direction of the relationship between CoD and ESG is consistent with the baseline model: a higher ESG rating is associated with a lower future cost of debt, not vice versa.

Regarding the second point, although our model incorporates several variables at both the company and country levels, we aim to further address the risk of endogeneity by employing a two-stage least squares (2SLS) approach using instrumental variables. Specifically, we select two instrumental variables represented by binary indicators: SDG and CO2. The SDG variable is set to 1 for firms located in countries with higher levels of achievement in the Sustainable Development Goals and 0 otherwise (source: United Nations), while the CO2 variable is set to 1 for firms with lower CO2 emissions and 0 otherwise (source: World Bank). We expect that these country-level indicators should not have a direct impact on the cost of debt but could be positively correlated with a firm’s ESG performance.

The results of this estimation are presented in Table 6, column 2 and 3. The first-stage results, reported in column 2, show that companies operating in countries more aligned with the SDGs or with higher emissions levels significantly influence a company’s ESG practices, confirming the validity of these instruments. Additionally, the first-stage F-test has a p-value of 0.000. The second stage estimation highlights that the positive relationship identified in the baseline analysis remains significant when using the 2SLS approach to address potential endogeneity concerns. The coefficient for the ESG variable in the second stage is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level, aligning with the baseline results. Furthermore, the Sargan test for over-identifying restrictions yields a p-value greater than 0.10, supporting the joint validity of the instruments. These robustness tests further strengthen the reliability of our main findings.

### 5.5. Alternative definitions of cost of debt and ESG

Our baseline analyses were based on a definition of the dependent variable, the cost of debt, constructed as the (natural logarithm of the) ratio between financial expenditures and the lagged value of the total debt, on the grounds that today’s interest expenses burdens depend substantially on past debt decisions. To test the robustness of our results to the fact that they do not depend on a specified definition of the cost of debt, in a new set of estimates we adopt another alternative constructed as the (natural logarithm of the) ratio between current financial expenditures and the average of current and past (1-year lagged) debt. This alternative definition allows total debt to be smoothed over two years, thereby reducing the possible impact of large debt peaks due to significant debt issuances which have a relatively small impact on the interest expense for the year. We replicate the baseline analysis reported in Table 3, but using this alternative definition, AltCoD, as the dependent variable. The results, reported in Table 7, show full consistency with the baseline results, suggesting that they do not depend on the specific operationalization of the definition of the cost of debt.

We adopt a similar testing approach but instead focus on the main explanatory variable of interest, i.e. ESG. In the baseline analysis, ESG is the rating attributed by MSCI to each company in a specific year. We test the robustness of these results by adopting an alternative approach based on the classes of ESG ratings, ranging from CCC (very low corporate capacity to cope with ESG risks) to AAA (very high corporate capacity to cope with ESG risks). To perform the conversion from number scores to ESG classes, we follow the approach of Billio et al. (2021), which is based on the following conversion system: CCC-1, B-2, BB-3, BBB-4, A-5, AA-6, AAA-7. We then use the new variable, ESG Class, as an alternative main regressor to ESG of the baseline estimates. The results, reported in Table 8, confirm in terms of sign and statistical significance what was obtained in the baseline analysis, with the sole exception of a negative and significant coefficient for companies operating in countries with lower political stability, further confirming that our results are not determined by the measurement strategy of the ESG indicator.

## 6. Concluding remarks

The intersection of corporate practices with environmental and social responsibility has given rise to a transition in financial decision-making. Research in sustainable finance has been investigating if and to what extent Environmental, Social, and Governance practices influence financial outcomes. Our study adds to this research stream by reinforcing the connection between superior ESG performance and a tangible reduction in the Cost of Debt, examining the role of institutional factors, which are relevant to the understanding of corporate financing practices. Specifically, the examination of resilience to vulnerability, rule of law, control of corruption, governance effectiveness, political stability, regulatory quality, and voice and accounting at the country level introduces granularity, demonstrating that the impact of ESG scores on the CoD is contingent upon the broader institutional context.

These results underline the importance of incorporating macro-level factors when studying the financial implications of sustainable practices. Our findings are robust to a battery of robustness tests that indicate that the observed relationships are not influenced by specific regions or sectors, by exceptional circumstances like the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, nor by the choice of specific definitions of cost of debt and ESG.

Our results highlight the importance of incentivizing and promoting ESG integration into corporate strategies, recognizing the positive feedback loop between sustainable practices and financial well-being. Our findings argue for a combination of general and specific approaches: general in the sense that the analysis of the relationship between ESG practices and the CoD should always be framed in the institutional context; specific because rules that work in one framework may not work in other realities. With sustainable finance constantly evolving, financial institutions and policymakers should keep reviewing and refining their analytical tools to better manage and consider the connections between ESG, financial performance, and institutional factors.
