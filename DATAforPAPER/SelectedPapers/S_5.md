# The moderating role of audit quality in the relationship between ESG practices and the cost of capital: Evidence from the United Kingdom

Saddam A. Hazaea $^a$, Chun Cai $^a$, Saleh F.A. Khatib $^{b,*}$, Mohammed Hael $^c$

### ABSTRACT

This study investigates the impact of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance on the cost of capital, using a sample of 406 non-financial companies in the United Kingdom from 2014 to 2023. We examined how ESG performance and audit quality influence the cost of equity (CoE), cost of debt (CoD), and weighted average cost of capital (WACC). The findings reveal a significant negative relationship between ESG performance and all three measures, indicating that strong ESG practices can reduce financing costs. Additionally, higher audit fees are positively associated with WACC and CoE, with a particularly pronounced effect on CoD. Engagement with Big 4 auditors moderates these relationships. Overall, the results demonstrate that ESG performance not only enhances corporate reputation and sustainability but also delivers financial advantages by lowering the cost of capital. These insights have significant implications for corporate financing decisions and provide guidance for managers, investors, policymakers, regulators, and government entities.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, corporate responsibility has undergone substantial transformation (Ashurov et al., 2024). It has shifted from a sole focus on meeting investor goals to balancing the interests of the stakeholders and community. This development and transformation are consistent with the stakeholder theory approach. The theory emphasizes the importance of companies meeting the expectations of all stakeholders, such as employees, regulators, government agencies, investors, and society, to ensure long-term sustainability (Alessa et al., 2024; Awa et al., 2024; Freeman et al., 2020). In recent times, environmental, social, and governance (ESG) related investing has emerged globally, representing “sustainable investment” and “ethical investment” (Nazir et al., 2022). Investors seeking to enhance corporate governance adopt the ESG concept associated with ethical investment (Derwall, 2007). Thus, stakeholders encourage companies to operate within ESG-related standards to promote a greener environment and a better society (Zhang et al., 2022a). This aligns with the viewpoint of voluntary disclosure theory, which posits that companies extensively report non-financial information in order to reduce information asymmetry and ensure the credibility of their activities with all stakeholders (Mohanty et al., 2024; Rossignoli et al., 2022).

ESG practices play a crucial role in enhancing companies’ environmental and social responsibility. However, challenges, such as environmental degradation and social inequality, continue to persist (Deegan, 2017). Although, driven by market and societal pressures, some legislation compels companies to fulfill their environmental and social obligations, most governments still regard this as voluntary (Deegan & Shelly, 2014; Zahid, Saleem, & Maqsood, 2023). The voluntary nature of the adoption of ESG standards by many companies and regulatory bodies highlights the perspective of signaling theory. This theory posits that companies disclose their ESG activities to enhance their reputation by demonstrating their commitment to sustainability, reducing investor uncertainty, and improving perceptions and confidence (Banerjee & Pradhan, 2022; Moussa & Elmarzouky, 2024; Paridhi et al., 2024). On the other hand, various environmental and social practices have faced criticism in some literature for failing to achieve sustainable development (Eliwa et al., 2021; Husillos et al., 2011). This indicates that sustainability practices among firms remain limited. Therefore, companies need to demonstrate enhanced environmental and social performance to assure all stakeholders of their commitment to societal expectations.

The efforts of countries, including the United Kingdom, to direct their attention to achieving sustainable growth or green finance have led to the development of ESG and sustainability reports. According to signaling theory, these reports are regarded as vital tools for companies to demonstrate their commitment to achieving sustainability and to attract investors who trust sustainable investing (Friske et al., 2023). The United Kingdom has established goals aimed at aligning environmental and economic performance in order to contribute to achieving carbon neutrality. Consequently, the government may implement regulations that mandate companies to adhere to ESG performance requirements and their disclosure (Treasury & DMO, 2021; Zhang et al., 2022b).

On the other hand, considering the United Nations’ interest in policy research, all markets are striving to meet the requirements to attract international investors, as well-crafted financial reports are among the most important factors for attracting investors (Zhang et al., 2022a). Companies often follow two strategies to communicate social and environmental concerns. The first is disclosing the actual actions, known as “objective tactic” strategies, that the company has taken regarding its environmental and social activities. The second, called “symbolic tactics”, involves demonstrating that the activities undertaken by companies align with societal expectations even though their policies do not reflect that (Ashforth & Gibbs, 1990). Therefore, some study results indicate that the ESG performance literature is related to symbolic tactics and strategy (Cho & Patten, 2007), while other studies, such as Khan et al. (2021), confirm that it is grounded in an objective tactical strategy. In this context, Kim et al. (2021) demonstrate that ESG performance has an important and consequential effect on capital financing decisions. However, the results of some studies indicate a need for further research to clarify the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital (Devalle & Fiandrino, 2017; Eliwa et al., 2021).

Research results show that effective practices and the proper implementation of ESG improve performance (Alareeni & Hamdan, 2020; Fu & Li, 2023; Sandberg et al., 2023). These practices also enhance the relationship with all key stakeholders in various businesses (Parmar et al., 2010), sustain and develop competitive advantages (Katsamakas & Sanchez-Cartas, 2023; Russo & Perrini, 2010), reduce information asymmetry (Bilyay-Erdogan, 2022) and risk (Broadstock et al., 2021), and have capital-raising benefits (Wong et al., 2024). These findings align with the voluntary disclosure theory, which posits that increasing the transparency of ESG reporting can lower capital costs and mitigate perceived risks, thus ensuring enhanced investor confidence (Nishitani et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital is still debated. While some studies, such as Chen et al. (2023), argue that ESG practices can contribute to reducing the cost of capital and improving value. Raimo et al. (2021) and Eliwa et al. (2021) confirm a lack of literature addressing the effect of ESG on the cost of capital. Previous studies focused on the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital by investigating a single variable, such as the cost of debt (CoD) (Raimo et al., 2021; Eliwa et al., 2021; Apergis et al., 2022), the cost of equity (CoE) (Chen et al., 2023; La Rosa & Bernini, 2022; Tang, 2022), and the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) (Atan et al., 2018). Whereas this study presents an investigation that includes all measures of the cost of capital, which further enhances knowledge, and in an environment different from the environment in which previous studies relied.

Due to the inconsistent results presented by some studies, it is important to conduct further studies to better understand the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital. These studies should also examine moderating and mediating variables in different regulatory environments (Ramirez et al., 2022; Zahid, Taran, et al., 2023). In this study, we utilized audit quality as a moderating variable, one of the most important factors closely related to ESG investing (Zahid et al., 2022). Given that investing in ESG adds value to the company by enhancing its social reputation, there are risks and negative behaviors that executives may exhibit (Lemma et al., 2022; Sassen et al., 2016). Auditors are considered to be one of the most vital sources for achieving impartiality in corporate management.

Auditing is regarded as an essential source that provides ESG assurance and contributes to achieving ESG-related risk management (Asante-appiah & Lambert, 2022; Borghesi et al., 2014). This mitigates agency problems and enhances confidence among all stakeholders, including investors and lenders (Ammar et al., 2023). Therefore, we employ a proxy variable and use data from the United Kingdom, which boasts a robust regulatory framework for corporate governance and financial reporting. This stability could promote more consistent disclosures regarding ESG practices, which may affect the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital. In addition, the data used covers the period following the financial crisis of 2008, which is a period of recovery and tightening of regulations, systems, and standards, thereby reinforcing the connection between ESG and the cost of capital. Finally, the United Kingdom is among the countries that have promoted ESG practices, especially with the growing interest from investors for sustainable investments. This heightened awareness suggests that firms with higher ESG performance are rewarded with lower capital costs, as investors and lenders perceive them as less risky.

The significant contribution made by this study is that it provides deep insights into the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital, which has been inconsistent in previous literature due to limited research. This study broadens the field of inquiry by using the CoD and WACC to measure the cost of capital, which has previously been addressed in a limited manner and in specific environmental areas. This expansion can assist companies in making better long-term investment decisions. Additionally, this study aims to provide clear scientific insights into the effect of ESG on financing costs, positing that firms with high ESG standards have a lower cost of capital. Furthermore, this research includes the moderating variable role of audit quality, assessed using two indicators (Big 4 and audit fees), to highlight the importance of the audit quality process in supervising and improving ESG performance. Auditing plays a critical role in governance, as it serves as an essential tool for problem-solving and risk management. Moreover, it provides valuable insights to all stakeholders regarding the company’s future prospects.

Concentrating on United Kingdom companies as one of the most important countries, along with European countries that pay great attention to corporate social responsibility issues, this research improves our comprehension of the role of ESG in promoting investment activities. It should be noted that findings from previous research conducted in other markets, such as United States (e.g., Ng & Rezaee, 2015; Wong et al., 2024), China (e.g. Ruan & Liu, 2021), Latin America (e.g., Ramirez et al., 2022), and Japan (e.g. Suto & Takehara, 2017), cannot be generalized to the United Kingdom market due to differences in the regulations and variations in the level of litigation risks between countries. Finally, this study also carries political implications, including elucidating the potential for transitioning to and achieving a net-zero economy through the green revolution that the United Kingdom is working on (Uk, 2020), which further contributes to fulfilling the objectives of COP26 and the Paris Agreement.

In light of the findings, there is a strong implication that enhancing ESG performance among companies could contribute to the broader objectives of global initiatives, such as COP26 and the Paris Agreement. Specifically, these agreements aim to mitigate climate change and promote sustainable development, which aligns with the study’s observation that superior ESG performance correlates with a lower cost of capital. This indicates that companies with strong ESG practices are better positioned to attract investment at reduced costs, which may incentivize further ESG improvements. In addition to obtaining information that contributes to achieving the best results related to the practice of ESG, this study complies with the guidelines and regulations issued by the Financial Conduct Authority of the United Kingdom in November 2021, which emphasized the need to develop a change strategy prioritizing the ESG practices’ enhancement.

The remainder of the research consists of six sections. Part 2 presents the “Theoretical Underpinning”. Part 3 covers the “Literature review and Hypotheses Development”. Part 4 covers the “Methodology and Data”. Part 5 covers the “Results” and Section 6 concludes with the “Conclusion”.

## 2. Theoretical underpinning

The cost of capital is one of the most important tools through which different financing strategies can be determined and decisions implemented that are compatible with sustainability issues (Dhaliwal et al., 2014; Ramirez et al., 2022). Conversely, companies that ignore the implementation of ESG activities face lower returns, diminished value, and potential failure. Therefore, capital owners believe that companies engaging in ESG activities are less exposed to risks (Johnson, 2020a, 2020b). The cost of capital is defined as the rational rate of return that investors receive based on the risks of the investment (Wang & Chen, 2023). In other words, the cost of capital is viewed as a risk-sharing mechanism, as ESG is perceived as the product that is purchased by investors in exchange for obtaining the return represented by the cost of capital, which is viewed as a result of ESG performance (Mackey et al., 2007; Nazir et al., 2022). In this context, investors play a pivotal role in positively or negatively influencing practices related to ESG investments and improving performance (Nazir et al., 2022). Achieving sustainability means fulfilling the demands of today’s community while ensuring that future generations can also meet their own needs (Johnson, 2020a, 2020b). This emphasizes that sustainability prioritizes the diverse interests of both current and future stakeholders.

Based on stakeholder theory firms should conduct their activities responsibly, so companies that focus on achieving the interests of stakeholders should consider various ESG concerns that may negatively affect their performance in the long term (Bavoso, 2012). Conversely, legitimacy theory posits that informing stakeholders about various ESG practices is tantamount to justifying and informing society about the company’s activities (Patten & Crampton, n.d.). Thus, legitimacy theory assumes that ESG performance is associated with societal pressure, especially in light of practices that contradict the company’s values and society’s expectations (Nazir et al., 2022). In this context, legitimacy theory posits that ESG performance is an essential mechanism for maintaining the company’s legitimacy, which contributes to increasing profits in the long term and works to mitigate the risks (Khanchel et al., 2023). The signaling theory suggests that companies seek to enhance their reputation by revealing their involvement in sustainability initiatives (Banerjee & Pradhan, 2022; Moussa & Elmarzouky, 2024).

Agency theory focuses on creating compatibility and reducing information asymmetry between agents, such as company managers and shareholders (Wang & Jin, 2023). Voluntary disclosure theory also asserts that companies seek, through the disclosure of non-financial information, to reduce information asymmetry and enhance trust with stakeholders (Mohanty et al., 2024; Rossignoli et al., 2022). This is regarded as one of the most significant factors that contribute to the effective evaluation of ESG practices. This alignment can lead to the achievement of sustainability and financial goals in the short-term, which often has an effect on the cost of capital. The audit function is a crucial governance tool that plays a key role in ensuring the quality and effectiveness of a company’s operations. It not only provides satisfaction and reassurance to shareholders but also instills confidence in the company’s managers’ ability to invest in ESG activities that align with the company’s goals and interests. By doing so, it helps to minimize agency costs, and, ultimately, leads to a reduction in the overall cost of capital (Cheung & Lai, 2023; Jiraporn & Gleason, 2007).

Based on the above, it is evident that a solid investment in ESG would mitigate risks and increase the company’s profits, thereby increasing its value. Finally, Barnea and Rubin (2010) demonstrate that shareholders or owners of companies are the primary groups influencing investment in ESG by monitoring companies’ management. Therefore, assuming that superior ESG performance will boost the company’s profits and, in turn, enhance its societal value, it is essential to understand the relationships among ESG performance, audit quality, and the cost of capital. However, our understanding of how excellence in this relationship is achieved remains limited.

## 3. Literature review and hypotheses development

### 3.1. ESG performance and cost of capital

ESG results play a crucial role in helping companies prepare a strategic design. Ramirez et al. (2022), Ding and Lee (2024), and Maji and Tiwari (2025) demonstrated that sustainability issues, including ESG, are vital. Although previous literature emphasized the role of ESG indicators in corporate financial performance, there is still a lack of understanding of the extent and nature of its impact on other financial indicators across diverse regulatory and cultural environments. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by investigating one of the most important factors in determining corporate financial strategy – the cost of capital. To enhance company performance, it is necessary to understand the nature of the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital, which contributes to determining appropriate financial strategies and making rational decisions.

In recent years, ESG performance and the cost of capital have emerged as critical topics in corporate finance research (Nazir et al., 2022). Dhaliwal et al. (2011) explored the advantages of non-financial ESG performance. The results showed that implementing non-financial disclosures leads to organizations and companies obtaining the benefits from a reduction in the cost of capital. Despite these findings, previous literature has not sufficiently examined how the quality and scope of ESG practices affect the cost of capital across different sized companies and sectors. While companies that initiate non-financial disclosure are generally more inclined to raise capital compared to companies that have not disclosed the performance of ESG information, Bilyay-Erdogan (2022) noted that disclosing ESG performance offers numerous benefits, including reducing information asymmetry. Minimizing information asymmetry is a crucial element of corporate governance strategies, as it ensures risk mitigation and enhances transparency and investor confidence (Cormier et al., 2010). However, prior research lacks comprehensive insights into how governance quality influences this relationship. Therefore, consistency of information would help build significant and high confidence among investors toward corporate performance related to ESG (Nazir et al., 2022). In this regard, investors will reward companies that demand a lower return on their debt (Li et al., 2017). Likewise, Chen et al. (2023) demonstrated that ESG performance significantly lowers the cost of capital, especially with increasing corporate stock diversification, and reduces corporate market risks. Similarly, the findings of Wong et al. (2024) indicated that strong and significant ESG performance aids in reducing the cost of capital represented by the CoD and the CoE. These studies enhance understanding by incorporating governance mechanisms into the ESG-cost of capital analysis. It is important to note that ESG performance is closely related to assessing the efficiency of corporate investment, a phenomenon that evolves continuously over the life of the company (Ahmed et al., 2020; Nazir et al., 2022). In this regard, companies face limited financial resources allocated to various investment activities (Akbar et al., 2022). However, prior research has not thoroughly explored how firms prioritize ESG investments under financial constraints. This paper seeks to address this gap by analyzing the impact of ESG on capital allocation decisions.

On the other hand, the results of some literature suggested that there is no positive relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital. Ng and Rezaee (2015) demonstrated that the ESG sustainability components are negatively related to the cost of capital when assessed through the lens of corporate growth and research efforts, and only positively related when measured in a strong, efficient economy. Similarly, Hampl and Vagnerová Linnertová (2024) found that ESG performance is negatively related to the CoD. The results of Koutoupis et al. (2025) revealed a negative impact on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital measured by the CoD and the CoE. The contradiction in the literature highlights the need for further investigation in various economic and regulatory environments, which will help all stakeholders in making decisions. In light of these contradictions Orij (2010) and Buallay (2019) showed that the costs of obtaining capital can be reduced through the quantity and quality of non-financial performance as stakeholder confidence in the use of invested funds diminishes. Crifo et al. (2017) confirmed the necessity of understanding the different classifications, whether financial or non-financial, of borrowing decisions to verify the relationship between ESG and the cost of financing.

In light of the growing interest of all parties in issues related to the environment in recent times (Nazir et al., 2022). The results of several studies, including those by Khan (2022); Liu et al. (2021b), and Wang et al. (2022), showed significant effects stemming from a commitment to environmental responsibilities, such as improving financial performance. However, few studies evaluated the moderating effect of regulatory frameworks on this relationship. This study aims to address that gap by considering various governance factors, particularly audit quality. It seeks to increase confidence among stakeholders, secure their ongoing support, and enhance investment efficiency and corporate development through the acquisition of strategic resources. Therefore, incorporating governance and regulatory perspectives, this study aspires to contribute to the understanding of how ESG-driven costs of capital operate in developed countries. Nations should strive to enact legislation and establish long-term mechanisms. This may encourage various companies to fulfill their environmental responsibilities and strengthen the evaluation system related to ESG issues (Liu et al., 2021a). Previous studies have highlighted the importance of investing in ESG at the onset of industrialization. These studies demonstrated that there is a negative effect on the financial performance of the company, as ESG activities are a cost and companies bear the burden (Chen et al., 2023). This study expands the literature by investigating whether these early-stage costs convert into long-term financial gains. Conversely, there is still a challenge in developing countries, where Duque-Grisales and Aguilera-Caracuel (2021) indicated that ESG is negatively related to financial performance. Chen et al. (2023) concluded that ESG performance significantly reduces the cost of capital. In this regard, Priem and Gabellone (2024) reported that environmental and social factors negatively affect the cost of capital in countries with weak regulatory and legal frameworks. Conversely, strong governance positively influences the cost of capital by encouraging companies to increase their borrowing. It can be argued that obtaining a high cost of capital from creditors and investors results in higher interest rates and returns. In contrast, the results of Atan et al. (2018) showed that ESG has a high impact on the increase in the cost of capital as a result of the high-interest rate.

In addition to using CoD and CoE to measure the cost of capital, this study uses the WACC measurement. Although a few studies have used this variable, the findings are inconsistent. Atan et al. (2018) used a sample from the Malaysian market to investigate the relationship between ESG performance and WACC. The results showed a positive effect. Meanwhile, the results of Johnson (2020a, 2020b) revealed a non-monotonic relationship between ESG and WACC using the South African market. Johnson (2020a, 2020b) and Fandella et al. (2023) showed a contradiction based on the type of sector used, indicating a negative relationship in the services sector, which aligns with stakeholder theory, but a positive relationship when using the industrial sector. Finally, the results of Ernst and Woithe (2024) indicated that there is no linear relationship between the ESG score and WACC.

It should be noted that the current literature has focused largely on the Chinese and American markets, while investigation in other markets is limited (Chen et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2022; Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Ruan & Liu, 2021). From the discussion above, the results of the studies are conflicting in determining the nature of the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital. The results depend on the type of regulatory environment, the work context, the type of risks, and stakeholder awareness. The United Kingdom stands out among other countries for providing transparency and strong protection for investors (La Rosa & Bernini, 2022). In addition, it was one of the first countries to issue recommendations and regulations governing the disclosure of climate and carbon information, as well as to continuously enhance corporate governance (Liao et al., 2015; Simic et al., 2024). Furthermore, implementing public awareness programs that contribute to the development of investment in ESG and attract new investors positively contributes to reducing the cost of capital and adding value. Therefore, our study proposes the following hypothesis.

**H1.** There is a positive relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital.

### 3.2. The moderating role of audit quality

The company’s ability to access equity funds smoothly may lead to overinvestment in ESG activities. Simultaneously, managers and executives may work excessively to invest in various ESG activities to enhance their personal benefits rather than maximize shareholder value (Borghesi et al., 2014). This raises concerns about unplanned ESG investments benefiting management at the expense of shareholders’ and capital owners. Therefore, an effective control mechanism is necessary to balance stakeholders’ interests and mitigate these risks. Hazaea et al. (2022), and Zahid, Saleem, and Maqsood (2023) reported that auditing is one of the most important techniques used in monitoring various activities, including administrative activities to restrict the behaviors of opportunistic managers and providing rational financial reports. Audit quality is determined by the processes carried out by highly qualified and experienced auditors. These processes aim to work on improving and enhancing the resource allocation mechanism. They also aim to increase the efficiency of debt contracting, reduce the financial frictions associated with moral risks, and minimize the unfavorable selection processes made by management or stakeholders (Cai et al., 2024; Causholli & Knechel, 2012; Rijal et al., 2023; Zahid, Saleem, et al., 2023). DeFond and Zhang (2014) concluded that high audit quality directly contributes to adding value to participants by ensuring that the company’s financial data accurately reflects its fundamental activities and operations.

Additionally, the emergence of agency costs related to the environment often arises from inconsistencies in information between the company’s management and stakeholders (Zahid et al., 2022). Therefore, it is crucial to implement quality audits based on the presence of highly qualified and independent auditors, which helps prevent any opportunistic behavior by any party (Buchanan et al., 2021). Likewise, Watkins et al. (2004) affirmed that the auditing quality is one of the key governance methods for addressing agency problems. Auditing theory suggests that the efficiency of the auditor reflects the effectiveness of external auditing, the scale of the audit, and the auditor’s brand identity, which are factors that enhance the quality of effective auditing (DeFond & Zhang, 2014; Knechel et al., 2013). Some studies, such as those by Karjalainen (2011), Zahid, Saleem, and Maqsood (2023), and Cai et al. (2024), used the Big 4 as a measure of the effectiveness and quality of auditing. They posited that the auditors from these firms are more efficient and independent, and that because of their strong reputation, the Big 4 strive to uphold without making any errors. Similarly, Nagy et al. (2023) suggested that the Big 4 deliver high-quality assurance and auditing because of several factors, including the use of the latest technology to enhance quality control, significant investment in human capital, independence in decision-making, and efforts to manage risks through comprehensive, unbiased evaluations. Xiao et al. (2020) confirmed that the Big 4 provide substantial support for corporate social responsibility reports, which strengthens their responsibilities in ensuring accurate information. Kausar et al. (2016) showed that the Big 4 firms are associated with a high and significant audit value in the eyes of stakeholders. Conversely, DeFond and Zhang (2014) indicated that audit fees are one of the most critical measures of audit quality. Higher fees imposed by audit firms signal the availability of high-quality auditing because of the presence of increased audit hours, thorough auditing processes, and the presence of highly qualified auditors (Montenegro & Brás, 2018). In contrast, the results of Rajgopal et al. (2021) reported that although increased audit fees are a signal of high-quality auditing, they may indicate a negative relationship with audit quality, especially in cases of litigation, a lack of independence, or risky clients. Finally, it should be noted that some literature determined a positive impact of audit quality on the cost of capital represented by the CoD and the CoE (Choi & Lee, 2014; Fernando et al., 2010). Although prior literature confirmed the strong link between audit quality and governance, there is a lack of investigation into how audit quality influences the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital. Using the Big 4 and audit fees as the primary indicators for measuring audit quality, this study aims to address this gap to ascertain the extent to which audit quality enhances ESG performance, and, consequently, reduces the cost of capital.

The second hypothesis is.

**H2.** Audit quality positively moderates the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital.

## 4. Methodology and data

### 4.1. Sample and data

Our study employs panel data analysis to investigate the effect of ESG performance on the cost of capital. Panel data provides a multi-dimensional dataset, incorporating variations in a cross-sectional unit (in this case, firms) over time. This approach enables a more refined understanding of the dynamics at play compared to a cross-sectional design, which does not account for changes over time (Bravo & Reguera-Alvarado, 2019). In compiling our dataset, we initially excluded all financial firms because of their unique accounting principles and regulatory frameworks, which influence the preparation and presentation of their annual reports. Additionally, any firm that lacked data on the dependent variable (ESG performance) was removed from the sample. Our primary data source was the ASSET4 database accessed via DataStream, complemented by additional information from Bloomberg. The ASSET4 database is a reputable repository that provides objective and systematic data on ESG performance using over 280 key performance indicators and 750 individual data points (Eliwa et al., 2021; Fatemi et al., 2018).

The sample under investigation consists of 406 non-financial United Kingdom companies, tracked over a decade from 2014 to 2023. The selection criteria aimed to capture the typical corporate landscape within the United Kingdom. We excluded companies with incomplete data records to maintain consistency and reliability. In preparing our dataset for analysis, we applied “winsorization” to the variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles. This method is used to reduce the influence of extreme values or outliers that could skew the results and potentially misrepresent the underlying trends. To address potential survivor bias, our study included firms with at least four consecutive years of data on any variable. This criterion ensures that the firms in our sample have a sustained operational history, which is vital for analyzing trends over time.

### 4.2. Measurement of variables

We utilized the ESG score as an independent variable since it is a multi-dimensional indicator derived from various financial and non-financial metrics. The Refinitiv Eikon database calculates the ESG score on a scale from 0 to 100. A score of 0 represents the minimum, while a score of 100 represents the maximum. The ESG score increases as more information is uncovered by the company. Conversely, the Refinitiv Eikon database publishes a range of corporate social responsibility reports for all investors. It publishes an individual score for each indicator from the E, S, and G systems or publishes the index score comprehensively. Thus, all investors can access any company’s ESG score along with the option to obtain score report data and scoring methodology. We followed Nirino et al. (2021) and Fandella et al. (2023) by employing the comprehensive and combined score for the three ESG components, which delivers a thorough and well-rounded evaluation of the firm. This assessment is based on the information in the ESG pillars. Following Fandella et al. (2023), the cost of capital is assessed as a dependent variable using three alternative model specifications measured at time t: (i) CoD, (ii) CoE, and (iii) WACC.

The CoD is the effective rate that companies must pay on all bonds, loans, and other interest-bearing debt capital. CoD can be defined operationally as the return on the company’s debts and loans until the maturity date, which will be repaid to all investors through debt instruments. The CoD value is derived using Bloomberg’s database. This value can be calculated using the following equation:

$$CoD = (r_f + \text{credit risk rate})(1-t) \tag{1}$$

Where:
* $r_f$: Expresses the amount of the country’s risk-free rate.
* Credit risk rate: Refers to the average CoD financing companies share with other external investors.
* $t$: A parameter indicating the marginal corporate tax rate. It is important to note that recent studies have explored the relationship between ESG and CoD (Eliwa et al., 2021; Fandella et al., 2023; Raimo et al., 2021).

The CoE signifies the return expected by shareholders as compensation for the risks associated with investing in company stocks. According to Sharpe (1964), the CoE value can be calculated using the Capital Asset Pricing Model, as illustrated in the following equation:

$$CoE = r_f + \beta (r_m - r_f) \tag{2}$$

Where:
* $r_f$: Refers to the risk-free rate of return of an investment.
* $r_m$: Measures the return of the market portfolio.
* $r_m - r_f$: Refers to the market risk premium.
* $\beta$: Beta measures the sensitivity of assets to the returns generated by the market portfolio.
Recent studies have explored the relationship between ESG and CoE (Azmi et al., 2021; Fandella et al., 2023).

The WACC is the total cost for a company to acquire equity capital and debt to fund its operations. It is regarded as a weighted average of the two previous measures (CoD and CoE), with the weights reflecting the proportion of each distinct unit of capital relative to the total capital obtained. The WACC value can be calculated using the following equation:

$$WACC = CoE \frac{E}{D + E} + CoD \frac{D}{D + E} \tag{3}$$

Where:
* $E/(D + E)$: Is the proportion of equity in the total raised capital.
* $D/(D + E)$: Refers to the percentage or amount of the external financing source.
It is important to note that recent studies have explored the relationship between ESG and WACC (Brooks & Oikonomou, 2018; Eliwa et al., 2021; Wong et al., 2021).

### 4.3. Empirical model

According to the theories formulated and the above discussion to describe the independent and dependent variables, this study model can be defined as follows:

$$Cost\ of\ capital = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Lag(Cost\ of\ Capital)_{it} + \beta_2 ESG_{it} + \beta_3 Size_{it} + \beta_4 BOARDS_{it} + \beta_5 BOARDG_{it} + \beta_6 ROA_{it} + \beta_7 LEV_{it} + \beta_8 CASH_{it} + \beta_8 LOSS_{it} + \beta_8 DIV_{it} + \beta_9 COVID_{it} + YEAR\_dummies_{it} + INDUSTRY\_dummies_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{4}$$

We utilized continuous and time-varying indicators to capture the degree of ESG variation, ensuring that the error term “$\varepsilon$” is identified and distinguished from the main variable presented at the company level. In addition, the results are estimated using various effects – fixed or random – and pooled ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions are applied to compare them using the F test (fixed vs. OLS). On the other hand, we conducted an examination of the variance inflation factor (VIF) linear relationship, as illustrated in Table 3. The $i$ in equation (1) denotes the firm, while the time in years is expressed by $t$. Moreover, $\varepsilon$ denotes the error term. Finally, to clarify the moderating role of audit quality, measured using AUDFEE and Big 4. We provide $ESG * AUDFEE$ and $ESG * BIG$ to determine the effect of the interaction of AUDFEE and Big 4 on the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital.

$$Cost\ of\ capital = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Lag(Cost\ of\ Capital)_{it} + \beta_2 ESG_{it} + \beta_3 AUDFEE_{it} + \beta_4 BIG4_{it} + \beta_5 ESG_{it} * AUDFEE_{it} + \beta_5 ESG_{it} * BIG_{it} + \beta_6 Size_{it} + \beta_7 BOARDS_{it} + \beta_8 BOARDG_{it} + \beta_9 AGE_{it} + \beta_{10} ROA_{it} + \beta_{11} LEV_{it} + \beta_{12} CASH_{it} + \beta_{13} LOSS_{it} + \beta_{14} DIV_{it} + \beta_{15} COVID_{it} + YEAR\_dummies_{it} + INDUSTRY\_dummies_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{5}$$

This equation aligns with what was mentioned in equation (4), with the addition of audit quality measures $ESG * AUDFEE$ and $ESG * BIG4$ to account for the influence of audit quality on the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital.

### 5.4. ESG performance, audit quality, and cost of capital

Companies seek essential factors that will enable them to obtain highly reliable data. Therefore, companies often work to enhance audit quality by involving one of the Big 4 firms (Zahid, Saleem, & Maqsood, 2023). On the other hand, the evaluation of companies’ commitment to preparing and organizing reports that enjoy transparency, accuracy, and a high degree of reporting is typically assessed through the precision and quality of audit operations (Kausar et al., 2016). The results of Chow et al. (2018), Zahid et al. (2022), and Zahid, Saleem, et al. (2023) suggest that the disclosure of non-financial reports related to ESG is improved by high-quality financial statements. In addition, companies’ dedication to implementing a high-quality audit bolsters the credibility of reports, including ESG reports. This contributes to enhancing the integrity and confidence of investors and lenders, thus facilitating their access to financing and attracting capital (Trpeska et al., 2017; Zahid, Saleem, & Maqsood, 2023).

Conversely, the results of DeFond and Zhang (2014) indicated that audit fees are one of the most important measures of audit quality. Higher fees imposed by audit firms are an indicator of the availability of high-quality auditing as a result of increased audit hours, the presence of in-depth auditing, and highly qualified auditors (Montenegro & Brás, 2018). We utilized the Big 4 and audit fees to examine the role of audit quality as a moderator in the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital. Table 5 displays the results of applying regression models consisting of 6 panels, including control variables for company characteristics. Similar to the results of Table 4 above (before introducing the moderating variable represented by audit quality), the findings reveal that the ESG score coefficients appearing in all columns have a significant negative relationship with WACC, CoD, and CoE. The results show that audit fees have a highly positive and significant impact on three measures of capital costs, with a particularly positive increase in the effect on CoD. Higher audit fees suggest that the company’s operations are more extensive and complex, and therefore, subject to greater scrutiny due to increased operational risk. Under these circumstances, investors and lenders may demand higher returns due to their perceived risk. Consequently, audit fees are associated with the increase in capital components, reflecting the perception of heightened risks and the need for comprehensive audit work by larger and more operational companies. $ESG*AUDFEE$ also demonstrates a positive correlation, indicating that as audit fees rise, the benefits of engaging in ESG activities diminish. In addition, the results show that the Big 4 have a moderate statistical impact on three measures of capital costs. This is because the Big 4 have a strong reputation for transparency and credibility in their auditing processes. These may help mitigate perceived risks and subsequently lower the cost of capital. Therefore, using Big 4 services may significantly contribute to reducing the negative impact on the cost of capital, thereby enhancing the advantages of practicing ESG activities through the use of Big 4 services. Thus, we conclude that audit fees have significant importance in terms of the cost of capital in United Kingdom companies, albeit this significance diminishes when assessed using the Big 4. The reason may be that there are a large number of companies that use firms other than the Big 4 to carry out audits.

### 5.6. Additional analysis

In this section, we conduct further analysis to assess the robustness of our initial findings. The dataset has been categorized into two segments based on company size. The first segment comprises large companies with total assets exceeding the industry median. The second segment includes companies with total assets falling below the industry median. The results presented in Tables 7 and 8 confirm the validity of the assessment in Table 5. The results indicate differences in the degree of influence of audit quality on the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital. The results show that the influence of audit fees and Big 4 is more pronounced in small companies than in large companies, especially in WACC and CoE. On the other hand, we observe statistically significant differences regarding the ESG value, with an increase in its negative correlation in large companies, suggesting that these firms possess more social, environmental, and institutional classifications. These results may also stem from the fact that large companies typically have a clear vision and apply international standards, while also engaging stakeholders in executing their ESG-related initiatives. Consequently, these firms gain more from ESG performance in terms of reducing capital costs. In addition, the larger the company, the greater its capacity to control potential risks in market disturbances and secure lower risks with increased external financing (Erragragui, 2018; Hasan et al., 2017). Finally, these findings affirm that small companies can lower their cost of capital by considering ESG factors and integrating them into their operations (Jang et al., 2020).

## 6. Conclusion and implications

Given the lack of empirical evidence regarding the effect of ESG on the cost of capital and the role of audit quality as a moderating variable, we have focused on conducting a comprehensive analysis by breaking down the cost of capital into three fundamental components (WACC, CoD and CoE) to provide more realistic and detailed results.

In this study, we examine the relationship among three key variables: ESG performance, the cost of capital, and audit quality. The results reveal a significant negative relationship between ESG performance and the three cost of capital metrics. These findings suggest that robust and effective ESG practices by companies contribute to lower financing costs by reducing perceived risk and enhancing investor confidence. Therefore, sustainability practices play a crucial role in shaping the cost of capital and investor behavior.

The results indicate that audit fees increase capital costs with an increased focus on CoD, and also somewhat mitigate the ESG relationship. This implies that higher audit fees signify greater audit effort and perceived risk. In addition, the results reveal that Big 4 audit quality has a moderate statistical effect on WACC, CoD, and CoE. Audits by the Big 4 firms are typically linked to slightly increased capital costs. This may suggest that larger companies, which are more likely to be audited by the Big 4, are often more complex due to expanded operations. This increased complexity can result in higher equity and debt costs due to perceived risks and increased regulatory burden. We performed additional tests based on the classification of companies into large and small with the aim of reinforcing the results and identifying the factors that influence the relationship. Although some prior studies confirm the positive relationship between ESG and the cost of capital, our findings indicate a negative relationship, especially for large companies. This discrepancy opens avenues for future research to explore this by conducting studies in different settings. Based on the study’s findings, it seems that the level of ESG implementation among companies in the United Kingdom remains confined to a limited number of firms, which may hinder their ability to distinguish between enhancing ESG and lowering the cost of capital. Therefore, these results indicate a pressing need for further advancements in ESG practices among United Kingdom companies and increased collaboration with the Big 4 firms. Implementing these changes can boost ESG performance, thereby enhancing the reputation of companies and facilitating access to more affordable capital. It is important to note that smaller companies can greatly benefit from their ESG initiatives in reducing financing costs. This also applies to larger companies, which typically have well-defined strategies for ESG activities due to their market influence, the diversification of risks across various areas, and their capacity to meet stakeholder expectations.

These findings have significant theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, this study contributes to the growing literature on the cost of capital and sustainability, particularly by examining the moderating role of audit quality. The results indicate that ESG performance not only enhances the reputation of the companies and their sustainability credentials but also generates a financial benefit by lowering the cost of capital. These findings are therefore of great importance in the context of the increasing interest from investors and lenders in ESG-compliant investments, as they highlight how companies can leverage their ESG practices to improve their financial performance by reducing financing costs. Practically, the results confirm that companies seeking to reduce their cost of capital should focus significantly on enhancing their ESG performance. Companies that pay higher audit fees or are audited by the Big 4 are better positioned to lower their cost of capital. This is not only a result of enhanced financial reporting standards but also due to the greater trust and transparency associated with these auditors.

Regulators and policymakers should also consider the significance of audit quality in the ESG field. By encouraging companies to adopt higher audit standards, regulators can further reduce the cost of capital and enhance the transparency of corporate practices, which may encourage increased investment in sustainability activities.

Finally, there is a strong implication that enhancing ESG performance among companies could contribute to the broader objectives of global initiatives, such as COP26 and the Paris Agreement. This study also suggests that audit quality, particularly higher audit fees and the involvement of Big 4 firms, plays a crucial role in this dynamic. Therefore, by ensuring more rigorous verification of ESG-related disclosures, audit quality could enhance investor confidence, thereby further lowering the cost of capital. While this study identifies a connection between ESG performance and reduced capital costs, additional research could examine how specific ESG initiatives (e.g., carbon reduction targets or social equity programs) directly contribute to achieving the goals of these international agreements. Furthermore, understanding how policy and regulatory frameworks can facilitate or reinforce this relationship could provide more actionable policy implications. In addition, this study clarifies the potential for transitioning to and achieving a net zero economy through the green revolution underway in the United Kingdom. Investors continuously seek to acquire stocks that continuously improve the access of investee companies to low-cost financing. Generally, strong investment in ESG helps lower financing costs by increasing equity financing, thereby achieving significant returns through all stocks, irrespective of a high or low ESG rating.

This study has some limitations, while fixed effects control for time-invariant and group-specific unobserved heterogeneity, they do not address endogeneity caused by time-varying factors or omitted variables that correlate with the regressors. Thus, additional methods (e.g., instrumental variables or lagged variables) are needed to address other sources of endogeneity.
