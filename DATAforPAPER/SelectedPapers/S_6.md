# The impact of sustainability reporting on the cost of capital: evidence from Vietnam’s listed companies

**Huu Cuong Nguyen and Hien Khanh Duong**
*Faculty of Accounting, University of Economics – The University of Da Nang, Da Nang, Vietnam*

**Abstract**

**Purpose** – This study aims to investigate the relationship between sustainability reporting and the cost of capital among Vietnamese firms using the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) standards.

**Design/methodology/approach** – Using a sample of the 100 largest firms by market capitalisation listed on the Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh stock exchanges as of 31 December 2023, this study applies regression models to examine how sustainability disclosure influences the cost of debt (COD), cost of equity (COE) and the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) over the period from 2021 to 2023.

**Findings** – The findings indicate a significant negative relationship between sustainability disclosure and the COD, COE and WACC, with environmental-related sustainability development goals (SDGs) disclosures having the most substantial impact. These results highlight the critical role of transparency in reducing information asymmetry and agency costs, ultimately lowering the cost of capital.

**Research limitations/implications** – This study extends stakeholder and signalling theories by demonstrating how sustainability disclosure affects both shareholders and creditors in a developing economy.

**Practical implications** – This study provides actionable insights for corporate managers and financial institutions on how sustainable development practices can enhance access to capital at more favourable rates. Policymakers and banks are encouraged to implement green finance initiatives to promote sustainability further.

**Social implications** – As Vietnam strives to combat climate change, this research underscores the importance of sustainable practices in building trust with investors and lenders.

**Originality/value** – To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study offers one of the first comprehensive examinations of the link between sustainability reporting and capital costs in Vietnam, offering important empirical evidence for academics and practitioners.

**Keywords:** Cost of capital, Cost of debt, Cost of equity, Sustainability disclosure, WACC

## 1. Introduction

In the context of increasing concern about climate change and environmental degradation, sustainable development has become a critical focus for businesses worldwide. To achieve sustainability development goals (SDGs), companies are incorporating environmental and social considerations into their operations and stakeholder relationships (Van Marrewijk, 2003). Sustainable development initiatives not only enhance a company’s public image but also highlight its commitment to social responsibility (Du et al., 2010). Such initiatives can attract customers, boost sales and elevate a firm’s market value (Luo and Bhattacharya, 2006).

While previous research has extensively explored business ethics and corporate social responsibility (CSR), fewer studies have delved into the broader and more complex concept of sustainable development, which only began to gain significant attention in the 1980s. Discussions often centre around corporate responsibility to the environment, employees and local communities, with varying perspectives on their implementation and impact (Van Marrewijk, 2003).

Despite increasing global environmental concerns, Carnegie et al. (2024) highlight that accounting professionals frequently give limited attention to SDGs, suggesting a gap between sustainability imperatives and professional practice. In contrast, recent research by Samarawickrama et al. (2024) underscores that enhancing the quality of CSR committees can significantly improve firms’ adherence to mandatory CSR spending, pointing to the critical role of internal governance mechanisms.

Additionally, Martínez-Ferrero and García-Sánchez (2017) find that companies in countries with higher levels of legal and cultural development, particularly those in industries with a strong focus on sustainability, are more likely to issue assurance information. This finding highlights the influence of external institutional factors and industry characteristics on corporate sustainability practices.

The cost of capital is a key financial concept for corporate financial managers, influencing decisions about raising funds through debt or equity and determining the associated risks. Numerous studies have examined factors affecting the cost of capital, including corporate financial challenges (Stulz, 1999), information asymmetry (Duarte et al., 2008), economic policy uncertainty (Obenpong Kwabi et al., 2022) and accounts payable quality (Le et al., 2021).

While numerous studies have examined the impact of corporate development and CSR activities on financial performance (Han et al., 2016; Laskar, 2018), firm value (Tarek, 2019) and consumer behaviour (Mercadé-Melé et al., 2018), the relationship between sustainable development and the cost of capital remains underexplored. Most research on this topic have conducted in developed economies (Ali et al., 2017), with limited focus on developing countries such as Vietnam. This geographic imbalance leaves a significant gap in understanding how sustainability practices influence capital costs in emerging markets.

The existing literature on this topic presents mixed findings. Some studies suggest that sustainability efforts lower the cost of capital, while others report no significant relationship. Moreover, few studies have examined the simultaneous impact of CSR on both the cost of debt (COD) and the cost of equity (COE; Harjoto, 2017; Cui et al., 2018; Sheikh, 2019). For instance, Shad et al. (2020) found that overall sustainability reporting, as well as economic sustainability reporting, reduced both the COD and COE. However, environmental sustainability reporting was shown to reduce only the COD, with no significant effect on the COE, while social sustainability reporting demonstrated no impact on either measure.

In Vietnam, sustainability has been a growing concern since the country committed to the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (United Nations, 2015). Vietnam’s sustainable development ranking has risen from 88th in 2016 to 54th in 2023 (Sustainable Development Report, 2024). This commitment is reflected in policy measures such as Decision No. 622/QD-TTg (Prime Minister, 2017), which outlined a national action plan for sustainable development, and Circular No. 96/2020/TT-BTC (Ministry of Finance, 2020), which introduced regulations for listed companies on sustainability reporting. Despite these advances, Vietnam remains one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change, with rapidly rising greenhouse gas emissions and a significant contribution to oceanic plastic waste (USAID, 2022).

In Vietnam, the legal framework to promote green finance development began to take shape with the approval of the *National Strategy on Green Growth for the 2011–2020 period and Vision to 2050*. This strategy outlines three critical tasks:
(1) reducing greenhouse gas emissions;
(2) greening production; and
(3) fostering a sustainable lifestyle and consumption.

Decision No. 1393/QD-TTg, dated 25 September 2012, formally identified green growth as a development approach aligned with the nation’s goals of renewing the growth model and restructuring the economy (Prime Minister, 2012).

Despite these efforts, Vietnam has yet to establish a dedicated budget line for climate change responses, with such funding currently integrated into the broader environmental budget. Moreover, the implementation of green credit faces considerable challenges, including significant capital requirements, extended investment horizons, low financial returns and the inherent risks associated with green investment projects. These obstacles create difficulties in selecting, appraising, evaluating and monitoring green credit activities (Nhan Dan, 2024).

Given the growing emphasis on sustainable development in Vietnam, it is crucial to investigate whether businesses view sustainability as advantageous for both their operations and societal impact. This is particularly relevant in light of the limited research exploring the relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital. This study addresses this gap by examining how sustainability reporting affects the COD, COE and WACC in Vietnamese firms. Existing research in Vietnam has primarily focused on social responsibility reporting and its relationship with the cost of capital. However, this study introduces a novel perspective by measuring the impact of sustainable development information. Our data set is unique in tracking 77 expenditures on the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) (2016) standards within the Vietnamese context, which vigorously promotes the application of GRI standards and underscores the green economy’s role in the 2021–2030 period and the vision for 2050.

It contributes to the literature by providing empirical evidence from an emerging market, which is crucial for broadening the understanding of the financial implications of sustainability reporting. Specifically, the research addresses the following key questions:

*   **RQ1.** Does disclosing sustainable development information affect the cost of debt?
*   **RQ2.** Does disclosing sustainable development information affect the cost of equity?
*   **RQ3.** Does disclosing sustainable development information affect the weighted average cost of capital?

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature and theoretical framework; Section 3 outlines the research methodology; Section 4 presents the results; and Section 5 concludes the study.

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis development

### 2.1 Theoretical background

#### 2.1.1 Theoretical basis of cost of capital.
Firms raise capital through a mix of debt and equity to fund their investments and operations. The cost of capital represents the return required by investors, both debt holders and equity holders, for providing this capital. For equity investors, the COE reflects the return they expect, adjusted for the risk associated with holding the firm’s shares. Modigliani and Miller (1958) laid the foundation for understanding this, demonstrating that in perfect markets, the COE increases with financial leverage.

The COD, on the other hand, represents the interest rate a firm pays on its borrowings, influenced by factors such as the firm’s credit risk and prevailing market conditions (Brealey et al., 2017). Firms with lower credit risk can typically borrow at lower rates, thereby reducing their COD.

The WACC combines both COE and COD, weighted by their proportion in the firm’s capital structure. WACC reflects the firm’s overall cost of capital and is a critical benchmark for evaluating investment decisions, as a lower WACC reduces the firm’s hurdle rate for capital projects (Damodaran, 2012).

#### 2.1.2 Theoretical basis for the impact of sustainable development on the cost of capital.
The relationship between sustainable development and the cost of capital has been extensively explored through various theoretical lenses. Although there is no universally accepted framework, this relationship can be analysed from both economic and socio-political perspectives (Gray et al., 1995; Deegan, 2002). These perspectives offer valuable insights into the mechanisms through which sustainability disclosure influences financial outcomes.

From an **economic perspective**, theories such as agency theory, signalling theory and resource dependence theory focus on the market consequences of sustainability activities, often prioritising financial stakeholders over others. Agency theory (Jensen and Meckling, 1976) highlights the potential for managerial opportunism in the presence of information asymmetry, which can misalign the interests of managers and stakeholders. Sustainability disclosure addresses this issue by reducing information asymmetry, thereby aligning managerial actions with stakeholders interests and lowering the perceived risk to creditors and equity holders (Cerbioni and Parbonetti, 2007; Jang and Ardichvili, 2020). Empirical evidence supports this argument, demonstrating that firms engaging in voluntary social and environmental disclosures exhibit more stakeholder-oriented behaviour, ultimately leading to a reduced cost of capital (Bénabou and Tirole, 2010; Eccles et al., 2012; Raimo et al., 2020).

Signalling theory (Spence, 1973) provides another lens for understanding the impact of sustainability disclosure. This theory posits that firms use sustainability reports as a mechanism to signal long-term stability and responsible corporate behaviour to the market. These signals enhance transparency and reduce adverse selection, resulting in lower COE and COD (Cheng et al., 2017; Dhaliwal et al., 2012). Firms with comprehensive sustainability disclosures are often perceived as less risky by financial markets, benefiting from reduced capital costs (Cormier and Magnan, 2014). Similarly, resource dependence theory (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978) suggests that firms require external resources for growth, and sustainability disclosure facilitates access to such resources. Studies show that firms with higher levels of sustainability disclosure secure more favourable financing terms from lenders (Dhaliwal et al., 2012; Michaels and Grüning, 2017; Yang et al., 2018).

In addition to these economic perspectives, **socio-political theories** such as legitimacy theory, stakeholder theory and institutional theory further explain the relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital. Legitimacy theory (Lindblom, 1994; Suchman, 1995) argues that firms engage in sustainability reporting to align with societal expectations and enhance their legitimacy. By improving a firm’s reputation and social standing, sustainability reporting reduces financial risk and the cost of capital (Cho and Patten, 2007; Chauvey et al., 2015). Stakeholder theory (Clarkson, 1995; Freeman, 2010) extends this understanding by emphasising the importance of managing the expectations of diverse stakeholder groups. Firms that disclose sustainability information address the needs of both financial and non-financial stakeholders, such as creditors, employees and communities, thereby reducing their overall risk profile and lowering capital costs (Chen and Roberts, 2010).

Institutional theory (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Meyer and Rowan, 1977) provides a complementary perspective by suggesting that firms comply with social and environmental norms to ensure organisational stability. Sustainability reporting reflects this compliance, enhancing the firm’s survival prospects and improving access to resources, including more favourable financing options (Chen and Roberts, 2010; Eliwa et al., 2021). Collectively, these socio-political theories demonstrate how sustainability disclosure aligns corporate behaviour with broader societal expectations, thereby influencing the firm’s cost of capital.

While each of these perspectives offers valuable insights, signalling theory is the most foundational and critical framework for understanding the relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital. By reducing information asymmetry and signalling long-term stability and responsible behaviour, sustainability disclosure aligns the interests of stakeholders and enhances market confidence, leading to lower capital costs.

### 2.2 The impact of corporate sustainable development on the cost of equity

Recent studies have increasingly focused on how financial markets perceive corporate sustainability, particularly in relation to risk. Evidence suggests a negative relationship between CSR performance and risk (Boutin-Dufresne and Savaria, 2004; Girerd-Potin et al., 2014; Bouslah et al., 2018). Information on sustainability can influence shareholders, thereby affecting the COE, which represents the rate of return required by shareholders.

Li and Liu (2018) show that high-quality CSR disclosure negatively impacts the COE for listed firms, especially those in environmentally sensitive industries. Their findings suggest that state-owned enterprises tend to benefit more from CSR disclosure in terms of reduced COE. Similarly, Chava (2014) reports that firms with environmental concerns face higher interest rates on loans and fewer participating banks, further supporting the idea that poor sustainability practices lead to higher COE and COD.

Ellili (2020) demonstrates that ESG disclosure significantly reduces both COE and COD for companies listed on the UAE financial market. These findings are consistent with earlier research by El Ghoul et al. (2011), Dhaliwal et al. (2012), Suto and Takehara (2017), Cui et al. (2018), Wang et al. (2021) and Abu Afifa et al. (2023), who highlight the financial benefits of sustainability reporting in reducing capital costs.

However, some studies, such as Dahiya and Singh (2021), argue that in certain markets (e.g. India), CSR performance may not reduce COE, as the costs associated with sustainable practices can outweigh the perceived benefits. Effective ESG disclosure and corporate reputation are crucial factors in determining whether sustainability reporting leads to lower equity capital costs (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Houqe et al., 2024).

Building on the theoretical foundations and existing empirical evidence supporting a negative relationship between CSR and the COE, the following hypothesis is proposed to examine the impact of sustainability information disclosure on the COE capital:

*   **H1.** There is a negative relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of equity.

### 2.3 The impact of corporate sustainable development on the cost of debt

Theoretically, firms that integrate non-financial aspects, such as environmental and social considerations, into their operations can reduce business risks and information asymmetry, which in turn lowers their COD (Lopatta et al., 2016). Ye and Zhang (2011) found a U-shaped relationship between social performance and COD in Chinese firms, with extremely high or low social responsibility leading to higher debt costs.

Numerous studies have reported a negative relationship between sustainability disclosure and COD, including Ellili (2020), Xu et al. (2021), Alves and Meneses (2024) and Zheng and Shen (2024). Banks and lenders view CSR practices as signals of reduced corporate risk, leading to more favourable loan terms (Dhaliwal et al., 2012). However, some research, such as Goss and Roberts (2011), suggests that sustainability activities may be viewed as costly investments, leading to higher debt costs in certain cases.

Building on the theoretical foundations and existing empirical evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed to examine the impact of sustainability information disclosure on the COE capital:

*   **H2.** There is a negative relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of debt.

### 2.4 The impact of corporate sustainable development on the weighted average cost of capital

Building on the findings related to COE and COD, this study hypothesises that higher levels of sustainability disclosure will lead to a lower WACC. Prior research provides robust support for this expectation. For instance, Michaels and Grüning (2017) and Ellili (2020) found that increased sustainability disclosure is associated with reduced WACC. Similarly, Kothari et al. (2008) suggest that disclosing investor-relevant information can lower a firm’s WACC by mitigating information asymmetry and improving transparency.

Moreover, Buallay (2019) demonstrates that ESG factors have a dual impact: positively influencing market performance, which supports value creation theory, while negatively affecting operational and financial performance, aligning with the theory of reducing the cost of capital. These findings provide a solid theoretical foundation for linking sustainability disclosure to a firm’s capital costs. These theoretical and empirical foundations provide a robust basis for proposing the following third hypothesis:

*   **H3.** There is a negative relationship between sustainability disclosure and the weighted average cost of capital.

## 3. Research methodology

### 3.1 Sample and data description

This study focuses on the 100 listed companies with the largest market capitalisation as of 31 December 2023, accounting for nearly 90% of the total market capitalisation at the end of 2023. As such, the sample is highly representative of the overall market. The framework for collecting sustainability data is based on the GRI standards (Global Reporting Initiative, 2016).

The study uses data from various corporate reports, including annual reports, financial statements, ESG reports and sustainability reports, covering the period from 2021 to 2023. This timeframe marks the initial phase of implementing Decision No. 1658/QD-TTg, dated 1 October 2021, whereby the Prime Minister approved the *National Strategy on Green Growth for the 2021–2030 period, with a Vision to 2050*. This strategy aims to reduce greenhouse gas emissions intensity per unit of GDP, promote green economic sectors and adopt circular economic models (Prime Minister, 2021). All data sources are publicly available on company websites, ensuring transparency and accessibility. Additionally, data on beta coefficients and market risk premiums were obtained from Vietstock (www.vietstock.vn), while government bond interest rates were sourced from the Ministry of Finance (www.mof.gov.vn).

### 3.2 Research model and variable measurement

#### 3.2.1 Research model:

$COE_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 SDG_{it} + \beta_2 FSIZE_{it} + \beta_3 LEV_{it} + \beta_4 TobinQ_{it} + \beta_5 CASH_{it} + \beta_6 ROA_{it} + \beta_7 AGE_{it} + \beta_8 GOV_{it} + \beta_9 IND_{it} + \beta_{10} COVID_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}$ (1)

$WACC_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 SDG_{it} + \beta_2 FSIZE_{it} + \beta_3 LEV_{it} + \beta_4 TobinQ_{it} + \beta_5 CASH_{it} + \beta_6 ROA_{it} + \beta_7 AGE_{it} + \beta_8 GOV_{it} + \beta_9 IND_{it} + \beta_{10} COVID_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}$ (3)

where $t: \in [2021; 2023]; i \in [1; 100]$

An F-test was conducted to select between the ordinary least squares and fixed-effects model (FEM), while the Hausman test was used to determine whether the FEM or random effects model (REM) was more appropriate. The authors also tested for serial autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity, addressing any issues by applying the generalised least squares (GLS) model to correct for these potential problems.

#### 3.2.2 Dependent variable
**Cost of equity (COE):** COE represents the return that a company must provide to its shareholders. It is calculated using the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), which incorporates the market risk premium and the beta coefficient (Sharpe, 1964; Lintner, 1965). The formula for COE is as follows:

$COE = R_f + \beta \times (R_M - R_f)$

where $R_f$ is the risk-free rate of return, typically represented by the yield on 10-year government bonds; $R_M$ is the return of the market portfolio; $R_M - R_f$ is the market risk premium; and $\beta$ represents the systematic risk of a security. This approach has been widely used in studies examining sustainability disclosure, such as Chen et al. (2009), Ellili (2020) and Bhatia and Kaur (2024).

**COD:** COD refers to the effective cost of interest that a company pays on its borrowings. Interest data is sourced from the income statement, while liabilities data is obtained from the company’s balance sheet. COD is calculated using the following formula:

$COD = \frac{\text{Interest Expense}}{\text{Liabilities}}$

This method has been applied in studies on sustainability disclosure, including Jabbouri et al. (2019), Saha and Bose (2021), Ha and Huynh (2022), Xu et al. (2021), Houqe et al. (2024), Alves and Meneses (2024) and Zheng and Shen (2024).

**Weighted average cost of capital (WACC):** WACC represents the average cost of both equity and debt, weighted by the firm’s capital structure (Saha and Bose, 2021; AlKhouri and Suwaidan, 2023). The formula for WACC is as follows:

$WACC = COE \times \frac{Eq}{Eq + De} + COD \times (1 - T) \times \frac{De}{Eq + De}$

where $Eq$ is total equity; $De$ is total liabilities (debt); and $T$ is the actual corporate tax rate, calculated as the ratio of actual income tax to pre-tax income. If the company reports zero pre-tax income or a loss, $T$ is set to zero.

#### 3.2.3 Independent variable
The level of SDG-related disclosure is measured using a dichotomous approach. Each indicator is assigned a value of “1” if disclosed by the company and “0” if not disclosed. The maximum number of SDG items a firm can disclose is 77, in alignment with the GRI (2016) guidelines (Appendix). To ensure the reliability and validity of the SDG-related checklist and scores, several measures are implemented following the approach of Nguyen (2023).

The SDG score is calculated as the average of the disclosed indicators. This method has been widely adopted in previous studies, including Papaspyropoulos et al. (2010), Ellili (2020), Usman (2020), Kumar et al. (2021), Nuskiya et al. (2021), Abu Afifa et al. (2023), AlKhouri and Suwaidan (2023), Erin and Olojede (2024), Alshahrani et al. (2024), Bhatia and Kaur (2024) and Zheng and Shen (2024).

#### 3.2.4 Control variables
This study incorporates several control variables, drawn from previous research, to account for factors that may influence the relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital. These control variables are detailed in Table 1.

The GOV variable is included because of the unique characteristics of Vietnamese enterprises, where a significant proportion of listed companies are state-owned. State ownership provides advantages such as easier access to loans and the ability to secure funding without collateral. As a result, the state ownership ratio can significantly influence a firm’s capacity to mobilise capital and its overall cost of capital (Boubakri and Ghouma, 2010). Similarly, the IND variable is incorporated to account for industry-specific characteristics. Variations in business operations and associated risks across different industries result in disparities in the cost of capital, particularly for firms operating in environmentally sensitive sectors.

### 4.2 The relation between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital

#### 4.2.1 The relation between sustainability disclosure and the cost of equity.
Table 3 presents the estimation results of equation (1), where the GLS method is used to address the limitations of the FEM model. The results indicate that sustainability disclosure has a significant negative impact on the COE ($\beta = -0.07, p < 0.01$), which is consistent with the underlying theory that sustainability disclosure reduces information asymmetry and agency costs. This finding aligns with previous studies, such as those by Ng and Rezaee (2015), El Ghoul et al. (2011), Dhaliwal et al. (2012), Chava (2014), Suto and Takehara (2017), Cui et al. (2018), Li and Liu (2018), Ellili (2020), Shad et al. (2020), Wang et al. (2021) and Abu Afifa et al. (2023).

Several variables in the model, including FSIZE, LEV, ROA and IND, do not exhibit statistical significance in relation to COE. However, the firm value, as measured by Tobin’s Q, has a significant negative impact on COE ($\beta = -0.019, p < 0.001$), indicating that higher enterprise value corresponds to a lower COE.

The results also reveal a negative relationship between the cash ratio (CASH) and COE ($\beta = -0.164, p < 0.001$), suggesting that holding excess cash may signal efficiency in the company’s operations, leading to a decrease in the COE. Furthermore, the AGE variable has a significant negative impact on COE ($\beta = -4 \times 10^{-4}, p < 0.01$), implying that more established companies tend to have better reputations, which aids in lowering their cost of capital.

Interestingly, similar to the findings of Li and Liu (2018), the results show that state-owned enterprises have a lower COE compared to private enterprises ($\beta = -0.014, p < 0.05$). ## 5. Conclusion and implications

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the impact of sustainability disclosure on the cost of capital for Vietnamese listed enterprises. Drawing on multiple theoretical frameworks, including stakeholder theory, resource dependence theory and signalling theory, the research highlights how the degree of sustainable development information disclosure affects key stakeholders, including creditors and shareholders, thereby influencing the COE, COD and the WACC.

The results confirm that greater transparency in sustainability reporting reduces information asymmetry and agency costs, contributing to a lower cost of capital. These findings align with the existing literature on sustainability disclosure and capital costs while also offering valuable insights into the unique context of a developing economy such as Vietnam. Specifically, the study reveals that the disclosure of sustainable development information, alongside broader efforts to implement sustainable development initiatives, can significantly reduce the cost of capital for Vietnamese businesses. This reduction not only benefits enterprises but also serves as a foundation for the Vietnamese economy to advance towards sustainable development. These efforts are aligned with Vietnam’s commitments to the United Nations and its overarching sustainability goals, providing a critical pathway for achieving long-term economic and environmental objectives.

The study has significant implications for both corporate managers and financial institutions. For corporate managers, the findings underscore the importance of adopting and disclosing sustainable development practices. Companies that align themselves with sustainability trends not only minimise operational risks but also enhance their ability to raise capital at a lower cost. This reduction in capital costs is critical for businesses seeking to improve their financial performance and compete in the capital markets. In particular, environmental information plays a pivotal role, as investors and lenders place significant emphasis on a firm’s environmental performance when determining the COE and COD. This highlights the growing importance of sustainability in financial decision-making, especially in environmentally sensitive industries.

For financial institutions, especially banks, the study emphasises the need to implement policies that encourage sustainable development. The introduction of green credit packages could be a vital mechanism for fostering environmentally responsible business practices. However, in the process of developing green finance, the issue of “greenwashing” has emerged as a potential risk. Greenwashing describes organisations that publish misleading or false information to appear environmentally responsible, with the intent of attracting customers and securing green finance sources (Tateishi, 2018). Figure 3 illustrates Vietnam’s green financial development platform, which the country aims to expand in the coming years. While this platform provides a strong foundation for Vietnam to combat climate change, as committed in the 2015 United Nations agenda (United Nations, 2015), efforts must also be made to prevent greenwashing to ensure genuine progress towards sustainable development.

Despite its contributions, this research is not without limitations. The use of a scoring system based on 77 sustainability criteria presents challenges in consistency, particularly because of variations in how companies report their sustainable development activities. These differences in reporting standards may have introduced some degree of bias in the assessment of sustainability disclosure. Additionally, the measurement of the COD using interest expenses and total debt, rather than actual loan interest rates, may not fully capture the true COD for these firms. Moreover, the study did not account for lagged effects, which could influence the relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital over time. These limitations suggest that further research is necessary to refine the measurement of both sustainability disclosure and capital costs.

Future research could build on this study by incorporating a longer time frame to examine how the relationship between sustainability disclosure and the cost of capital evolves over time. Expanding the research sample size to include a broader range of firms and industries would enable a more comprehensive understanding of the market dynamics. Addressing endogeneity issues within the model and introducing lagged variables would provide deeper insights into the long-term effects of sustainability practices on capital costs.

Additionally, exploring alternative methods for measuring the COE and incorporating more detailed data on actual loan interest rates would enable more robust comparisons across different financial contexts. These refinements would not only validate and strengthen the findings of this study but also provide precise insights for both academics and practitioners.

Future research may also investigate novel areas, such as the role of religion in influencing sustainability disclosure and its impact on the cost of capital, similar to the findings of Shehadeh et al. (2024b). Another promising direction involves examining the impact of gender diversity on the quality of sustainable development information, as highlighted in Shehadeh et al. (2024c). These extensions could offer fresh perspectives on the complex interplay between organisational characteristics and sustainability practices, further enriching this field of study.
