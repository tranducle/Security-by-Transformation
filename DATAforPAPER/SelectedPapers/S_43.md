# ESG performance and the cost of debt: exploring the role of internal audit

Sibei Yan<sup>a</sup>, Seung Uk Choi<sup>b</sup>, Iny Hwang<sup>c</sup> and Hyung-Rok Jung<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Business School, Chengdu University, Chengdu, China; <sup>b</sup>Department of Accounting and Taxation, School of Management, Kyung Hee University, Seoul, Republic of Korea; <sup>c</sup>College of Business Administration, Seoul National University, Seoul, Republic of Korea

### ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effect of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) performance on capital-raising costs in the debt market. The study also examines the role of internal audit organizations in this relationship. The results of the current study show that firms with high ESG performance have lower cost of debt. More importantly, when ESG is classified into sub-factors (E, S, and G), all three are negatively associated with the cost of debt. Next, the current study documents that the presence of an audit committee, the increase in the number of internal audit members, and the higher independence of the internal audit organization incrementally weaken the negative relationship between ESG and the cost of debt. The results remain robust across various additional tests and when endogeneity concerns are mitigated. Overall, these findings suggest the possibility that, as a primary risk assessment function, an independent and large internal audit organization exposes and highlights ESG risks to external credit rating agencies. The findings of this study offer valuable practical insights into how internal audit organizations can effectively assess and contribute to the evaluation of ESG performance.

## I. Introduction

Global environmental challenges, along with the rapidly evolving policies that respond to them, are driving companies to prioritize sustainability more than ever. In particular, Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) factors, as advocated by prominent public organizations like the United Nations, have emerged as critical survival strategies for businesses across all sectors.

With the increasing importance of ESG and its direct link to a company’s survival, credit rating agencies are compelled to incorporate ESG factors into their evaluations. This trend is driven by the rapid growth of ESG-related assets, necessitating their consideration in credit ratings. For instance, ESG funds reached a record high of $2.97 trillion in 2021 (Morningstar 2022). Furthermore, a survey from global asset managers indicates an anticipated increase in ESG-related assets from $18.4 trillion in 2021 to $33.9 trillion by 2026 (PwC 2022).

In academia, the significance of non-financial performance has been extensively examined in prior literature. While numerous studies have explored the effects of general social contribution activities, relatively few have investigated the impact on capital costs specifically from the perspective of ESG. Moreover, the existing research on ESG has predominantly concentrated on the equity market (Gigante and Manglaviti 2022). The investigation into the relationship between ESG and the cost of debt (COD) is a more recent development.

For instance, a few recently published studies have examined the relationship between ESG performance from the perspective of the debt market (Apergis, Poufinas, and Antonopoulos 2022; Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Gao, He, and Li 2022; Gehricke, Ruan, and Zhang 2024; Gigante and Manglaviti 2022; Lavin and Montecinos-Pearce 2022; Raimo et al. 2021). However, the empirical relationship remains unclear, with existing studies yielding mixed and inconsistent results. Therefore, this study aims to build upon these prior investigations by exploring whether ESG performance is factored into credit evaluations and influences the cost of debt. More importantly, we seek to illuminate the role of internal audit (IA) organizations, such as the audit committee, in evaluating ESG performance.

Capital market participants are keen to verify whether firms engage in ESG activities and how these activities might enhance firm value. This growing interest may lead investors to seek an internal audit function that evaluates their firms’ ESG risks at an early stage. Nevertheless, empirical research on whether IA organizations conduct ESG assessments is insufficient.<sup>1</sup> We present the following three scenarios outlining the potential influence of the internal audit organization on the relationship between ESG and COD.

First, an IA may reinforce the correlation between ESG and COD. As the internal monitoring mechanism reduces agency costs and information asymmetry (Anderson, Mansi, and Reeb 2004; Lorca, Sánchez-Ballesta, and García-Meca 2011), internal audit functions, such as the audit committee, are likely to incrementally reduce COD by enabling an optimal level of ESG investment through risk assessment. Second, since not all ESG investments are reliable and conducive to corporate value (e.g. greenwashing), an independent and professional IA organization can potentially unveil the associated ESG risks to creditors (Bradley and Chen 2015). This exposure might entail costs for creditors and concurrently establish a mechanism to attenuate the negative correlation between ESG and COD. Third, it is also possible that IA bodies may not have any incremental role. Given that ESG disclosures and associated activities remain non-mandatory, especially within developing nations like Korea, the potential exists for internal audit organizations to exhibit varying levels of diligence in auditing these aspects. Therefore, an empirical investigation is required to ascertain how an IA effectively evaluates and reports the risks entailed in conducting ESG activities.

Our study yields the following outcomes using data from Korean firms listed on the stock market between 2018 and 2021. First, our findings underscore that improved ESG performance exerts a tangible influence in reducing COD. Furthermore, upon dissecting the ESG performance into its sub-factors – Environmental, Social, and Governance – we discern robust and statistically significant inverse relations between these factors and COD. Second, the results of investigating the incremental effects of internal audit organizations reveal a gradual attenuation of the negative relationship between ESG and COD. In particular, the presence of an audit committee, the increase in the size of the internal audit function, and the high level of independence of the internal audit organization incrementally reduce the negative relationship between ESG and COD. In addition, the role of IA is more pronounced in low resources subsamples, proxied by operating cash flows and cash holdings. We posit that this outcome can be attributed to the exposure of ESG risks to external credit rating agencies facilitated by the IA’s primary risk assessment for ESG investments.

Our results remain consistent across various additional tests, affirming their robustness and credibility. We also confirm that our results remain unaffected by endogeneity controls, including Heckman two-stage regression and instrumental variable regression tests.

Our study makes contributions to both academic literature and practical understanding in the following ways. First, it underscores the increasing significance of ESG factors in response to recent environmental challenges. By extending previous research, this study delves into the interplay between ESG performance and the cost of debt. While previous studies have predominantly explored capital costs within the context of the stock market, recent investigations focusing on the debt market have broadened scholarly perspectives (Bhuiyan and Nguyen 2020; Gigante and Manglaviti 2022; Kordsachia 2021). In line with this trend, our study contributes to the literature by highlighting the positive impact of ESG performance on capital raising from the debt market. Second, a pivotal outcome of our study is its exploration of the roles played by IA. Our findings indicate that the effect of ESG on reducing the cost of debt diminishes when IA organizations are functioning effectively. This suggests that the conflicting results regarding the impact of ESG on the cost of debt reported in previous studies (Erragragui 2018; Gigante and Manglaviti 2022; Kordsachia 2021; Utz 2017) may be at least partially influenced by variations in internal audit practices. Essentially, the presence of an audit committee or a substantial number of independent internal audit members seems to bring forth the exposure of ESG investment risks to credit institutions. This insight holds noteworthy implications for practical ESG investment strategies. Third, most prior research on this topic has focused on developed countries (Lavin and Montecinos-Pearce 2022; Raimo et al. 2021). Given the limited scope and varied findings of existing research, we shift our focus to Korean-listed firms. This shift provides an opportunity for a deeper understanding of ESG efficacy in a context where capital markets are less developed and stakeholder-centred corporate relationships are still emerging. Our examination offers a comprehensive assessment of ESG within this unique setting.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we outline the relevant literature and form our hypotheses. In Section 3, we discuss the methodology and sample. In Section 4, we present the main empirical findings. In Section 5, we offer our conclusions.

## II. Literature review and hypotheses development

### Prior research on ESG performance and cost of debt

The growing interest in social and environmental matters has triggered the rapid growth of ESG activities. Nonetheless, the precise influence of ESG performance on COD remains elusive. Certain studies indicate that enhanced ESG performance has the potential to mitigate COD by diminishing information asymmetry and enhancing earnings quality (Apergis, Poufinas, and Antonopoulos 2022; Bacha, Ajina, and Ben Saad 2021; Bhuiyan and Nguyen 2020; Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Kordsachia 2021; Raimo et al. 2021). Specifically, companies exhibiting strong ESG performance tend to draw higher levels of interest from institutional investors (Petersen and Vredenburg 2009) coupled with elevated analyst coverage (Bhuiyan and Nguyen 2020; Jo and Harjoto 2014; Naqvi et al. 2021). This enhanced focus translates to increased accessibility to information about these companies, mitigating information asymmetry and lowering COD. Xu, Xu, and Yu (2021) show that a higher ESG performance level can give creditors a clearer insight into a company’s financial standing. Furthermore, enhanced ESG performance correlates with heightened earnings quality (Bozzolan et al. 2015; Kim, Park, and Wier 2012) and better firm performance (Alareeni and Hamdan 2020; Rehman, Khan, and Rahman 2020; Zhu, Sun, and Leung 2014), consequently diminishing default risk. For example, using Australian data, Bhuiyan and Nguyen (2020) document a negative relationship between ESG performance and COD, emphasizing ESG’s potential to mitigate business risks. Kordsachia (2021) shows that firms with better ESG performance experience reduced COD, particularly during financial distress.

Other studies have yielded no significant correlation or even a positive relationship between ESG performance and COD (Erragragui 2018; Gigante and Manglaviti 2022; Goss and Roberts 2011; Magnanelli and Izzo 2017). Based on the agency theory, an elevated level of ESG performance could signify managerial overinvestment aimed at burnishing their reputation (Bhuiyan and Nguyen 2020). Furthermore, this view is supported by Utz’s (2017) findings, which highlights instances of ESG overinvestment among firms in the Asia Pacific region.

Unlike other stakeholder groups, creditors or credit rating agencies are more concerned about whether ESG can improve a firm’s repayment capacity and solvency. Thus, the impact of excessive ESG activities by a firm might not always be viewed positively in creditors’ credit rating evaluations.

### Prior research on the relationship between internal audit function, cost of debt, and ESG

Allegrini and Greco (2013) point out that internal audit reduces information asymmetry problems and opportunistic actions of management, thus improving the disclosure quality (Krishnan, Wen, and Zhao 2011; Kusnadi et al. 2016; Pozzoli, Pagani, and Paolone 2022; Rainsbury, Bradbury, and Cahan 2009). If agency problems and information asymmetries are adequately mitigated by internal audits, additional control mechanisms may not be required (Cerbioni and Parbonetti 2007) and firms may experience a reduction in COD (Ashbaugh-Skaife, Collins, and LaFond 2006).

Empirical research studied the relationship between IA and ESG performance, with findings yielding mixed results. Using data from 59 Gulf Cooperation Council-listed banks, Buallay and Al-Ajmi (2020) show that audit committee members’ financial expertise is negatively related to ESG performance, while audit committee independence and meeting frequency positively affect ESG performance. Based on the Australian energy sector firms, Arif et al. (2021) find that audit committee activism and independence increase firms’ compliance with GRI guidelines in ESG disclosure. Similarly, Pozzoli, Pagani, and Paolone (2022) find that audit committee independence and expertise positively influence ESG performance, whereas audit committee tenure is negatively associated with ESG performance. In summary, the IA’s impact on ESG performance hinges on its specific attributes.

### Hypotheses development

While research analysing the impact of ESG performance using stock market reactions or the costs of equity capital has accumulated empirical results, studies focused on the debt market remain relatively scarce (Gigante and Manglaviti 2022). However, credit rating agencies have been increasingly emphasizing ESG due to the rapid growth of ESG-related assets and the tightening of related regulations. Reflecting these heightened interests, a handful of recent studies have investigated the influence of ESG on the debt market. However, the reported results are varied and contingent on the countries and research designs they employed.

On the one hand, certain studies suggest a negative association between ESG performance and COD (Apergis, Poufinas, and Antonopoulos 2022; Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Raimo et al. 2021). In examining EU countries, Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh (2021) highlight how ESG disclosure and performance can reduce COD. Apergis, Poufinas, and Antonopoulos (2022), analysing S&P 500 firms, demonstrate that entities with lower ESG scores exhibit higher bond spreads and lower bond ratings. Similarly, with data from S&P 1200 observations, Raimo et al. (2021) show a negative correlation between ESG disclosure and COD.

On the other hand, employing a sharp regression discontinuity design within the context of EU countries, Gigante and Manglaviti (2022) do not find significant evidence that firms with higher ESG scores than the average experience a decrease in the cost of debt. In the case of US firms, Erragragui (2018) reports that most social performance activities are not factored into creditors’ pricing decisions.

In summary, the debate in this area remains inconclusive. In this study, we anticipate that an improved ESG performance will reduce capital-raising costs within the debt market. We offer following reasons.

First, this expectation is grounded in contemporary realities, where creditors, including banks, must evaluate the ESG risks associated with their clients when making lending decisions. For instance, a global consortium of 400 major banks collaborates with the United Nations Environment Programme Finance Initiative (UNEP FI) to address existing ESG challenges. This trend is mirrored in South Korea, where the 11 largest banks are engaged with UNEP FI.<sup>2</sup> These institutions have adopted the Principles for Responsible Banking and the Net Zero Banking Alliance in alignment with UNEP FI’s guidelines. These frameworks empower financial establishments to institute ESG principles, fostering a sustainable financial landscape. Given this evolving landscape, it is logical to anticipate that creditors will incorporate assessments of debtors’ ESG performance into their considerations, thereby influencing capital costs accordingly.

Second, ESG is pivotal in mitigating information asymmetry between creditors and debtors. From the creditor’s perspective, apprehensions regarding the long-term viability of debtors’ operations are factored into capital costs. Debtors frequently strive to lower their capital-raising expenses by presenting their operational performance in an overly favourable light. However, earlier research contends that robust ESG performance can serve as a countermeasure against opportunistic behaviour. For instance, past studies have demonstrated that firms exhibiting elevated ESG performance tend to disclose more persistent earnings (Ma and Yoo 2022). Furthermore, an increase in ESG performance is positively correlated with high-quality earnings (Rezaee and Tuo 2019). Drawing from these research findings, it is reasonable to anticipate that companies exhibiting strong ESG performance are less likely to engage in opportunistic behaviours, such as manipulating earnings to deceive creditors. If this is the case, firms with higher ESG performance would likely experience lower COD than those with lower ESG performance.

Third, one of the principal determinants influencing the COD is the debtor’s default risk. Creditors are chiefly concerned with the debtor’s ability to meet principal and interest payments. Previous research indicates that ESG factors can potentially mitigate this risk (Buallay 2019; Li, Zhang, and Zhao 2022). For instance, Buallay (2019) shows a positive correlation between ESG disclosure and profitability indicators like Return on Assets (ROA) or Tobin’s Q. Li, Zhang, and Zhao (2022) further substantiate this by directly demonstrating that higher ESG ratings correlate with reduced default risks for Chinese firms. This cumulative evidence points to ESG performance lowering a firm’s likelihood of default, thus leading to an anticipated decrease in COD.

In summary, we expect a negative relationship between ESG and COD. In addition, it is important to note that outcomes from the individual components of ESG might not exhibit uniformity (Buallay 2019; Erragragui 2018). Specifically, the Environmental, Social, and Governance factors could profoundly impact COD. As a result, we examine each factor (E, S, and G) in addition to the comprehensive ESG performance. Building upon the preceding discussion, we propose the following as our first hypothesis.

**Hypothesis 1:** Companies with high ESG performance tend to experience a reduction in the cost of debt.

Next, we examine the effectiveness of the IA organization. We outline three potential scenarios regarding the impact of IA on the connection between ESG and COD.

First, if an increase in ESG performance leads to a decrease in COD, it is plausible that the IA would bolster this correlation. Prior research contends that internal monitoring mechanisms curtail agency costs by communicating a firm’s risk profile to creditors (Anderson, Mansi, and Reeb 2004; Lorca, Sánchez-Ballesta, and García-Meca 2011). Notably, within the various corporate governance mechanisms, an internal audit function, such as the audit committee, evaluates risks associated with diverse corporate activities, thereby safeguarding financial performance. This role of internal audit organizations will likely contribute to a decline in COD by facilitating an optimal level of ESG investment through internal risk assessment.

Second, an internal audit function could mitigate the impact of ESG on reducing COD. Based on the agency theory, creditors seek to reinforce their governance structure to oversee managerial actions, with the audit committee as a key component. An augmented IA capacity that uncovers risks tied to a firm’s ESG investments can empower creditors to factor these considerations into their credit assessments. It is important to recognize that not all ESG strategies offer the same reliability and value enhancement. Concerns such as greenwashing underscore the significance of questioning the effectiveness and trustworthiness of various ESG initiatives. An independent and proficient IA organization can assist external stakeholders by spotlighting the risks associated with ESG investments. For instance, Bradley and Chen (2015) demonstrate that an independent board increases COD by endorsing risk-taking actions that might appear costly to creditors. This mechanism could potentially dampen the negative association between ESG and COD.

Third, internal audit bodies may not have any incremental role in the relationship between ESG and COD. The audit committee’s core responsibility is scrutinizing disclosed data like financial statements. However, it is important to note that ESG-related disclosure is not yet mandatory in Korea.<sup>3</sup> If this is the case, it is conceivable that the IA organization may not undertake evaluative functions concerning ESG investments.

Collectively, it remains a subject for empirical investigation whether the IA function effectively monitors ESG investment. Drawing on insights from prior research (Arif et al. 2021; Buallay and Al-Ajmi 2020), we categorize the traits of IA organizations into the followings: the presence of an audit committee, the number of total IA members, the number of independent IA members, and the inclusion of accounting and financial experts within IA organization. We formulate the following as our second hypothesis.

**Hypothesis 2:** The attributes of an internal audit organization affect the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt.

## III. Research design

### Measuring the variables

#### Cost of debt
Using credit ratings as a measure of the cost of debt has received substantial support in previous literature (Jiang 2008; Qi, Roth, and Wald 2010). Additionally, prior studies have documented a systematic relationship between corporate governance mechanisms and credit ratings (Ashbaugh-Skaife, Collins, and LaFond 2006). In alignment with these earlier investigations, we adopt credit ratings as a proxy for the cost of debt. Specifically, we use the credit ratings published by the National Information and Credit Evaluation (NICE).<sup>4</sup> The variable *COD*, which categorizes firms into 10 distinct credit ratings, ranges from highest (grade 1) to lowest (grade 10). Consequently, a lower *COD* value corresponds to a higher credit rating. In addition, we use the interest rate as an additional proxy for the COD. Specifically, *COD_INTEREST* is defined as the interest expenses divided by the average interest-bearing debt.<sup>5</sup>

#### ESG performance
We measure ESG performance using the widely utilized ESG score from the Korean Corporate Governance Service (KCGS) ESG database, which was applied in previous research on Korean firms (e.g. Hwang, Kim, and Jung 2021). KCGS has evaluated corporate governance since 2003 and provided ESG scores since 2011, focusing on listed Korean companies. KCGS revised its evaluation categories for the ESG-score in 2013 and subsequently updated the categories for the G-score in 2018. The assessment aligns with OECD governance principles and ISA 26,000 standards. KCGS’s ESG scores include separate assessments for Environmental (E-score), Social (S-score), and Governance (G-score) factors. These factors encompass various sustainability and corporate responsibility indicators, such as environmental management, stakeholder engagement, and governance practices.

The evaluation procedure is as follows.<sup>6</sup> First, data that companies have disclosed, such as annual reports, sustainability reports, etc., as well as information from news media and pertinent institutions, is gathered from over 1,000 sample companies. Subsequently, leveraging a set of 300 evaluation criteria, fundamental and comprehensive evaluations are undertaken to compute the scores. This process culminates by incorporating feedback from the sample companies involved.<sup>7</sup>

In our study, the ESG (E, S, and G) score is calculated by adding the scores of the secondary indicators. The variable *LN_ESG* (E, S, and G) is the natural logarithm of the ESG (E, S, and G) score, with a higher value indicating better performance.<sup>8</sup>

#### Internal audit attributes
In the context of Korean regulations, the Commerce Act (Article 542) mandates that listed firms with total assets exceeding 2 trillion Korean Won (approximately $1.5 billion) establish an audit committee comprising at least three directors, with two-thirds being independent directors and one director possessing accounting and financial expertise. Firms can also voluntarily establish an audit committee. Companies without an audit committee must appoint a director responsible for internal audits.

We hand-collect internal audit data from annual reports. Our study focuses on four internal audit characteristics extensively examined in previous research (e.g. Arif et al. 2021; Buallay and Al-Ajmi 2020). The four proxy variables are measured as follows: *IA_EST* is a binary variable that equals one if a firm has an audit committee, and zero otherwise. *IA_SIZE* represents the size of the IA organization, quantified by the number of internal audit members. *IA_OUT* indicates the independence of the IA organization, calculated based on the number of independent internal audit members. *IA_EXP* is a binary variable that denotes the expertise of the IA organization and is equal to one if a firm employs at least one accounting and financial expert and zero otherwise.

### Empirical models

To test the hypothesis that ESG performance is related to the COD, we regress the cost of debt on the ESG (E/S/G) performance. To mitigate the endogeneity concern in this relationship, we use the one-year ahead COD (*COD<sub>t+1</sub>*). Specifically, the following Equation (1) depicts lead-lag regression to be estimated for testing Hypothesis 1:

$$COD_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 LN\_ESG(or LN\_E, LN\_S, LN\_G)_t + \beta_2 SIZE_t + \beta_3 LEV_t + \beta_4 GRW_t + \beta_5 ROA_t + \beta_6 FA_t + \beta_7 CASH_t + \beta_8 CFO_t + \beta_9 DV_t + \beta_{10} FOR_t + \beta_{11} OWN_t + \beta_{12} BIG4_t + \beta_{13} BETA_t + Year Fixed Effects + Industry Fixed Effects \tag{1}$$

The dependent variable, *COD*, signifies the cost of debt, denoting credit ratings. *LN_ESG* (E/S/G) measures a firm’s ESG performance. We expect a negative and significant coefficient $\beta_1$ if ESG performance reduces the cost of debt.

To account for firm-related factors affecting COD (Bacha, Ajina, and Ben Saad 2021; Ghouma, Ben-Nasr, and Yan 2018), we control for size (*SIZE*), leverage (*LEV*), growth (*GRW*), return on assets (*ROA*), fixed assets ratio (*FA*), cash ratio (*CASH*), cash flow ratio (*CFO*), dividend policy (*DV*), foreign ownership (*FOR*), largest shareholder ownership (*OWN*), international Big 4 audit firms (*BIG4*), and systematic risk (*BETA*). Year and industry fixed effects are included. Standard errors are clustered at the firm level. All variables are defined in the Appendix.

Hypothesis 2 is tested using interaction terms (*LN_ESG × IA*), with *IA* representing internal audit attributes (*IA_EST*, *IA_SIZE*, *IA_OUT*, and *IA_EXP*).

$$COD_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 LN\_ESG_t + \beta_2 LN\_ESG_t \times IA_t + \beta_3 IA_t + Controls + Year Fixed Effects + Industry Fixed Effects \tag{2}$$

In the above Equation (2), the dependent variable is the cost of debt, and the variable of interest is the interaction between *LN_ESG* and *IA* variables. The coefficient $\beta_2$ shows the incremental effect of internal audit attributes on the relationship between ESG performance and cost of debt. If the IA organizations effectively expose the ESG risks to credit rating agencies, we expect a positive and significant coefficient for the interaction term *LN_ESG × IA*.

## IV. Empirical findings

### The sample

We focus on Korean-listed companies within the KOSPI and KOSDAQ markets, spanning 2018 to 2021. Our sample begins in 2018 as this year aligns with the implementation of accounting reforms and changes in the audit report format. This creates a systematic disparity in the accounting landscape before and after 2018. Additionally, KCGS revised its evaluation categories in 2018. More importantly, audit committee guidelines were implemented in 2018, which likely resulted in changes in the effectiveness of internal audit organizations from that year onward.

We obtain financial data from the TS2000 and Value Search databases (formerly KIS-Value), provided by the Korea Listed Companies Association and NICE Information Service, respectively. Internal audit-related data are collected manually. Our sample consists of non-financial industry companies with a December fiscal year-end, yielding a total of 2,258 firm-year observations.

### Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of the variables.<sup>9</sup> The mean value of *COD*, as measured by the credit ratings of the subsequent year is 4.532, with a range spanning from one to ten. The mean (median) value of the proxy for ESG performance (*LN_ESG*) is 5.327 (5.255). The mean values of *LN_E*, *LN_S*, and *LN_G* that indicate E, S, and G performances are 2.542, 4.372, and 4.320, respectively. The mean values for the four audit committee attributes indicate that 46.2% of the firms in our sample have an audit committee. On average, there are approximately two internal audit members per firm (2.017). The average number of independent internal audit members is 1.838. Additionally, 63.6% of firms have accounting and financial experts among their internal audit members. Other controls such as *SIZE*, *LEV*, *GRW*, and *ROA* show mean values of 27.370, 0.444, 0.105, and 0.019. About 46.8% of firms are audited by international big 4 auditors (*BIG4*). Other descriptive statistics are generally consistent with those reported in previous studies using similar Korean stock market data.

Table 2 shows Pearson correlations among the main variables. *COD* is significantly and negatively correlated with all ESG performance variables, aligning with our expectations. Strong positive correlations exist among ESG variables, indicating interrelated ESG activities. Internal audits are negatively linked to *COD*, indicating lower debt costs. Moreover, the positive relationship between internal audit attributes and ESG performance shows that firms with an audit committee, more internal audit members, more independent internal audit members, and hiring an accounting and financial expert encourage greater ESG activities.

### The cross-sectional tests

Various cross-sectional factors may influence the results of this study. Specifically, we examine the resource-based theory of ESG performance, which posits that limited internal resources can hinder ESG investments (Islam, Ghosh, and Khatun 2021). We use operating cash flows and cash holdings to proxy for a firm’s resources.

#### The impact of operating performance
Previous studies suggest that a firm’s ESG investments are influenced by its operating performance (Tampakoudis et al. 2021). Firms align their ESG investment decisions with their operating performance to ensure that the costs of ESG investments do not outweigh the potential benefits (Hamdi, Guenich, and Ben Saada 2022). Consequently, we anticipate that firms with weaker operating performance may struggle to invest in ESG adequately, leading to intensified internal audit oversight of ESG activities. We measure operating performance using cash flows unaffected by accrual accounting (Banker, Mashruwala, and Tripathy 2014), enhancing performance measurement objectivity. Based on the median value of operating cash flows, we divide the sample into High_CFO and Low_CFO.

In Table 6, the coefficients of *LN_ESG×IA_EST*, *LN_ESG×IA_SIZE*, and *LN_ESG×IA_OUT* are significantly positive for firms with low operating cash flow (Columns 2, 4, and 6), aligning with our expectation that the impact of ESG on COD reduction is further diminished by the presence of effective IA functions in firms with weaker operating performance.

#### The impact of cash holdings
Next, we expect firms with low cash and ESG investments to be riskier. We analyse the role of internal audit in mitigating the ESG-COD relationship, particularly in low-cash firms. Cash holdings are used as a proxy for resources (Khatib et al. 2022). Although not tabulated, the results reveal that, except for *IA_EXP*, the internal audit attributes are more likely to reduce the effect of ESG on reducing COD in subsamples with low cash holdings. These findings demonstrate that when investing in ESG activities without sufficient cash holdings, the internal audit functions monitor these investments, leading to exposure that is subsequently reflected in the cost of debt.

### Mitigating endogeneity

Previous analyses addressed the potential endogeneity between ESG performance and COD by employing lead-lag regression with one-year ahead COD. In this section, we conduct the following three additional tests to address endogeneity concerns further.

First, we apply Heckman two-stage regression to address endogeneity concerns. In the first stage, we use *LN_ESG* as the dependent variable and calculate the Inverse Mills Ratio (IMR) by including all control variables as independent variables in the model. We find that *SIZE*, *LEV*, and *BIG4* are positively related to ESG performance, while *GRW*, *ROA*, and *CASH* show negative relationships. In the second stage, we test our hypothesis. The results in Table 7 show that ESG performance has a negative relationship with the COD in the analysis including IMR (Panel A). Additionally, effective IA also contributes to reducing COD (Panel B). Furthermore, the negative relationship between ESG and COD is incrementally weakened in the presence of effective IA (Panel C). These findings indicate that the results of this study remain robust even when using Heckman two-stage regression analysis.

Second, we employ an instrumental approach to mitigate the endogeneity. We use the industry mean value of ESG (*IND_LN_ESG*) and lagged value of ESG (*LN_ESG<sub>t-1</sub>*) as instrumental variables to estimate the expected value of ESG performance (Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021). The underlying premise of these instrumental variables is that the industry average ESG performance and the lagged ESG performance are related to the current ESG performance, but are not directly associated with COD.

Table 8 presents the results of the two-stage least squares regression, employing instrumental variables. Column (1) shows the results of the first-stage regression, which indicates that our instrumental variables (*IND_LN_ESG* and *LN_ESG<sub>t-1</sub>*) are positively related to *LN_ESG*, suggesting that industry average ESG performance and previous ESG performance of the firm have an impact on the current ESG performance.<sup>11</sup> The findings of the second-stage regression are shown in column (2). Using the fitted value estimated from the first-stage regression (*PRED(LN_ESG)*), we observe that *PRED(LN_ESG)* is significantly and negatively correlated with COD coefficient = -0.366, t-value = -4.069). Overall, the results demonstrate that the endogeneity-controlled outcomes align with the findings from the previous main tests.

Third, we address potential reverse causality by controlling for the previous year’s COD (Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021). Specifically, we include the lagged value of COD to account for its influence on current ESG performance. Although these results are not tabulated, they show that after controlling for the effect of the prior year’s COD, the coefficient for *LN_ESG* remains significantly negative. This suggests that ESG performance continues to reduce the cost of debt. Additionally, we observe that the coefficients for individual ESG components are generally negative. These findings indicate that the negative impact of ESG performance on COD remains robust, even when accounting for lagged COD to address reverse causality concerns.

### Other robustness tests

#### The effect on the long-term cost of debt
Given that ESG investments have a long-term impact, their effects can also manifest over an extended period. For instance, creditors might consider a firm’s historical ESG performance when assessing corporate bonds. Thus, we conduct a long-window test by examining the COD two years after the initiation of ESG investments. The results are shown in Table 9. In Columns (1) to (4), where COD is measured by the credit ratings of year t + 2, we find positive coefficients for *LN_ESG×IA_EST*, *LN_ESG×IA_SIZE*, and *LN_ESG×IA_OUT* while *LN_ESG×IA_EXP* is not significant. The results are similar to the previous findings of Hypothesis 2.<sup>12</sup> Hence, we can infer that even after two years, the risk assessment of ESG in the internal audit continues to influence the COD.

#### Using the alternative proxy of cost of debt
Previous research underscores the significance of debt repayment capacity and default risk as crucial factors influencing the costs of financing. The former focuses on the firm’s present financial position, while the latter centres on evaluating its ability to meet its obligations in the future. The credit rating, which we use as a proxy of COD, is a pivotal indicator aiding creditors in making informed investment decisions and represents default risk. Therefore, in this section, we use the interest rate as an alternative proxy of the cost of debt to examine the effect of ESG on the debt repayment capacity (Fortin and Pittman 2007; Jung and Kim 2020). Specifically, *COD_INTEREST* is an alternative variable that measures COD, calculated as the interest expenses divided by the average interest-bearing debt.

Table 10 shows that the coefficients for ESG performance proxies are generally negative but exhibit weaker statistical significance compared to the previous credit rating models (Panel A). Additionally, the IA variables show negative coefficients, indicating that effective internal audits reduce the COD (Panel B). Furthermore, we observe positive and statistically significant coefficients for the interaction terms involving ESG performance and internal audit attributes, except for *LN_ESG × IA_EXP* (Panel C). These results suggest that creditors consider the ESG risks highlighted by internal audits, leading to an increased necessity for COD. Overall, our findings support the idea that creditors can evaluate and address ESG-related risks through internal audit mechanisms.

#### Other un-tabulated tests
Although not tabulated, we conduct the following additional tests. First, recognizing that the leverage ratio (*LEV*) could systematically influence the COD, we exclude the *LEV* variable from the model and reanalyse the data. The results remain consistent with our main findings.

Next, we consider that external auditors might also impact the relationships outlined in our hypotheses. Using Big4 auditors as a proxy for external auditors, we find that the negative relationship between ESG and COD is diminished when accounting for Big4 auditors. This suggests that both external and internal auditors may play a similar role in these relationships.

Instead of using interaction terms, we conducted additional tests by segmenting the sample based on internal audit functions. Specifically, we divided the sample based on the following criteria: the presence or absence of an audit committee, whether a firm has two or more internal audit committee members or fewer, whether a firm has two or more independent internal audit committee members or fewer, whether a firm hires at least one accounting and financial expert or none. Un-tabulated results show that the negative relationship between ESG and COD is evident when a firm does not have an audit committee, or has fewer than two internal audit members, or has fewer than two independent internal audit members. This relationship is not observed in firms with the corresponding counter conditions. These results support our finding that ESG does not reduce COD when internal audit functions are effectively in place. There is no systematic difference in the results based on whether a firm hires an accounting and financial expert or not, which also supports our main findings.

## V. Conclusion

The ESG performance of firms emerges as a crucial concern directly tied to the survival of individuals and business entities. The extent to which creditors incorporate these ESG efforts into corporate assessments holds significant implications for enhancing transparency within the capital market. While numerous studies have investigated the impact of ESG on the equity cost in the stock market, only a limited number have explored the bond market. Consequently, this study investigates the influence of ESG performance on the cost of debt.

Using data from listed companies in Korea, we find that firms with high ESG performance have lower COD. In addition, the subcomponents of ESG are all significantly related to COD. These results suggest that credit rating assessments incorporate positive reflections of ESG performance. This study also investigates whether the internal audit organization has an incremental effect on this relationship. The results indicate that the presence of an audit committee, an independent internal audit organization, and a large internal audit organization incrementally reduce the negative relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt. These findings collectively underscore the important role that internal audit attributes play in evaluating ESG performance. Specifically, effective internal audit functions monitor and expose ESG risks to credit rating agencies. These results remain robust even after controlling for endogeneity by applying either the two-stage regression or the instrumental variable approach.

It is important to acknowledge that the impact of ESG on the cost of debt is not uniform across all countries. As demonstrated by Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh (2021), the effect of ESG on COD varies depending on the level of stakeholder orientation in different nations. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable to other capital markets or countries. Nevertheless, our study provides an initial evidence on the relationship between ESG and the COD with internal audit functioning as a mediator. Future research could build upon our findings by examining this relationship in other countries with different characteristics.
