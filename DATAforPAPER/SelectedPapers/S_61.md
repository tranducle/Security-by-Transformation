### ABSTRACT
This study examines the direct relationship between corporate governance (including board gender diversity, board independence, insider ownership, institutional ownership, and foreign ownership), ESG disclosure, and the cost of debt. Drawing on stakeholder, agency, and signaling theories, the study extends its analysis to examine the mediating effect of ESG disclosure in the relationship between corporate governance and the cost of debt. Using a sample from nine sectors of the Chinese economy, the results show that most board characteristics are negatively related to the cost of debt, except for insider ownership, which shows a significant positive relationship. In addition, ESG disclosure is found to significantly reduce the cost of debt and serves as a mediator between corporate governance and the cost of debt. This study makes a unique contribution by highlighting the mediating role of ESG disclosure in the relationship between corporate governance and the cost of debt.

## 1 | Introduction

In corporate finance, decisions regarding capital structure are critical, as they directly influence a firm's ability to secure cost-effective external financing, which is essential for sustaining growth and innovation (Chindasombatcharoen et al. 2022). In emerging markets, companies often rely more heavily on bank financing than on capital markets. While this reliance can reduce the cost of capital, it also exposes firms to increased financial risk, particularly if borrowing becomes excessive (Aksoy and Yilmaz 2023). As a result, firms strive to optimize their borrowing levels, seeking to balance financial risk with access to affordable funding, thus promoting long-term sustainability. Concurrently, lenders—especially financial institutions—are placing greater emphasis on corporate governance practices, board composition, and commitment to environmental, social, and governance (ESG) initiatives (Zhu et al. 2024). These factors are increasingly recognized for their direct impact on corporate sustainability (Karavitis et al. 2021).

The implementation of specific mechanisms of corporate governance and ESG practices is seen by lenders as a safeguard, ensuring transparency and accountability. In this context, the characteristics of corporate boards and ownership structures are of particular importance in ensuring accurate financial reporting and effective corporate decision-making (Anderson et al. 2004; Cucari et al. 2018). The governance role of the board facilitates the adoption of policies that support sustainable performance while safeguarding board and stakeholder interests (Jibril and Isa 2025). The relationship between board gender diversity and the cost of debt has attracted considerable attention in the literature, although findings remain inconclusive. For instance, Usman et al. (2019) observed a positive relationship between gender diversity and the cost of debt, while Kamil and Appiah (2022) found a negative relationship. Similarly, board independence has been identified as a key factor in influencing firm sustainability, as independent boards enhance oversight and protect stakeholder interests. Lenders often rely on audited financial statements to assess a firm's financial sustainability and its eligibility for financing (Dichev and Skinner 2002). Recent studies also emphasize the role of board independence in shaping ESG disclosure (Arayssi et al. 2019). Firms engage in ESG initiatives not only to differentiate themselves from competitors, but also to gain benefits such as enhanced customer loyalty and improved financing opportunities (Hossain et al. 2017). Building on these insights, this study aims to explore the effect of board diversity on the cost of debt, with a particular focus on the role of ESG scores. Furthermore, ownership structures significantly influence a firm's engagement in ESG activities, as firms must align with the expectations of various stakeholders (Wang et al. 2023).

Despite it, literature still presents conflicting views on the role of institutional investors in driving ESG practices. For example, Dyck et al. (2019) argue that institutional ownership fosters ESG engagement, whereas Borghesi et al. (2014) suggest that firms with higher institutional ownership may invest less in corporate social responsibility (CSR). This discrepancy underscores the need for further research on the relationship between ownership structures, ESG practices, and the cost of debt. Therefore, this study examines the influence of institutional ownership on ESG contributions and its impact on the cost of debt. Both equity and debt investors increasingly prioritize ESG behavior when assessing firms (Li et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2023). While existing research has explored the impact of ESG on the cost of capital (Apergis et al. 2022; Eliwa et al. 2021), the majority of studies have focused on equity, with less attention given to the cost of debt (Gigante and Manglaviti 2022). Financial institutions are increasingly considering both financial and non-financial factors, such as ESG metrics, in their assessments of corporate sustainability. Some studies have explored the indirect effects of ESG, including its moderating (Chen and Xie 2022) and mediating (Fan et al. 2023) roles. Given the growing importance of board characteristics, ownership structures, and non-financial information in shaping corporate sustainability, this study seeks to answer the following research question: *RQ: How do board characteristics and ownership structure affect the cost of debt, and to what extent does ESG mediate this relationship?*

To answer this question, we focus on non-financial companies listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange. Specifically, we examine A-share listed companies over the period 2016–2022. This timeframe is particularly relevant, as in 2016 the Chinese government mandated the disclosure of environmental information by large polluting companies (Meng-tao et al. 2023).

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it examines how board gender diversity and ownership structures improve transparency and reduce the cost of debt by improving governance practices. Board characteristics, particularly gender diversity and independence, are expected to strengthen monitoring functions and protect stakeholder interests in line with the principles of good corporate governance. This also supports the United Nations' Goal 5 by highlighting the benefits of gender inclusiveness for companies. Second, the study examines the role of ESG in mitigating the cost of debt. While ESG practices may not directly affect debt pricing, they signal a company's commitment to sustainability, which debt investors may value by offering lower borrowing rates. Finally, this study extends the debate on corporate governance, ESG, and sustainability by empirically examining these factors in China, one of the world's fastest growing economies. The findings provide policy recommendations for promoting female board representation and improving ESG disclosure. The results show that board gender diversity, board independence, foreign ownership, and institutional ownership significantly reduce the cost of debt, while higher insider ownership is associated with higher debt costs. Moreover, ESG mediates the relationship between these factors and the cost of debt, with firms' ESG investments signaling their commitment to sustainability to lenders.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the theoretical background and hypothesis development, Section 3 describes the data and methodology, Section 4 reports the findings, and Section 5 concludes with the limitations of the study and suggestions for future research.

## 2 | Theoretical Background

This study integrates Stakeholder Theory, Agency Theory, and Signaling Theory to explore the interplay between corporate governance, ESG practices, and the cost of debt. Each theory offers distinct insights into these relationships, collectively forming a comprehensive framework. Stakeholder Theory (Freeman 1984, 2010) posits that firms must address the expectations of all stakeholders, including creditors, to maintain trust and ensure long-term sustainability. Strong corporate governance, reflected in diverse and independent boards, enhances transparency and mitigates conflicts of interest, thus fostering positive relationships with lenders. Empirical evidence supports the notion that high ESG performance signals effective stakeholder engagement, which reduces information asymmetry and improves financing conditions (Kieschnick and Moussawi 2018; Derbali et al. 2022). Agency Theory (Meckling and Jensen 1976) provides a framework for understanding how corporate governance can mitigate agency conflicts between managers, shareholders, and creditors. Effective governance, including ESG disclosures, reduces information asymmetry and future cash flow uncertainties, thereby lowering the cost of capital (Arayssi et al. 2016; Suttipun 2021). Gender-diverse and independent boards are particularly effective in promoting transparency and curbing opportunistic managerial behaviors (Kamil and Appiah 2022). Additionally, institutional and foreign ownership structures play a significant role in fostering accountability and enhancing governance practices, further contributing to reduced borrowing costs. Signaling Theory (Spence 1973) emphasizes the role of ESG disclosures as signals of a firm's commitment to sustainability and governance excellence.

In markets characterized by information asymmetry, such disclosures serve to differentiate firms, enhance reputation, and build stakeholder confidence (Del Gesso and Lodhi 2024; Meng-tao et al. 2023). Companies with effective governance leverage ESG reporting to convey superior performance, thereby achieving market differentiation and fostering trust among creditors and other stakeholders (Figure 1).

## 3 | Hypothesis Development

### 3.1 | Corporate Governance Characteristics and Cost of Debt

The composition of corporate boards, particularly with regard to gender diversity, has emerged as a central focus in corporate governance research. Studies have highlighted the significant impact of gender diversity on financial outcomes, specifically in terms of debt costs. Aksoy and Yilmaz (2023) found a substantial association between the presence of female directors and reduced debt costs for Turkish firms, emphasizing the positive role of gender diversity in improving financial performance. Similarly, Francis et al. (2013) reported that firms led by female Chief Financial Officers (CFOs) experienced lower financing costs compared to those with male CFOs. On the similar side Sarang et al. (2024), the scholars linked gender diversity to reduced information asymmetry, further supporting its beneficial impact. However, conflicting findings complicate the overall narrative. For example, Benjamin and Biswas (2019) and Abdullah et al. (2016) found no significant relationship between gender diversity and the cost of debt, though the latter study emphasized the mediating role of contextual factors in the Malaysian context. Expanding the scope to an international setting, Koutoupis et al. (2024) reported a negative relationship between board gender diversity and the cost of debt, a conclusion consistent with Elmagrhi et al. (2018) in the UK and Sarang et al. (2024) in the French market. Another cornerstone of corporate governance is board independence, which has been extensively analyzed in the context of its influence on debt costs. Bradley and Chen (2015) demonstrated that board independence reduces debt costs, particularly in scenarios characterized by low leverage, underscoring the critical role of independent directors in mitigating agency conflicts and enhancing oversight. This finding is further corroborated by Anderson et al. (2004), who provided empirical evidence that independent boards contribute to lower debt financing costs for firms in Brazil and the S&P 500, respectively. Additionally, Gonzalez-Ruiz et al. (2024) identified board independence, gender diversity, and broader governance practices as critical factors in reducing long-term debt costs, highlighting the reputational advantages derived from effective governance mechanisms. The structure of ownership has also been closely examined as a determinant of debt costs: diverse ownership configurations revealing varying implications. Elyasiani et al. (2010) and Bhojraj and Sengupta (2003) observed a negative relationship between institutional ownership and debt costs, indicating that higher levels of institutional ownership generally translate to reduced financing expenses. Supporting this, Kim et al. (2019) reported similar findings in the context of U.S. firms. Despite this, insider ownership presents a more nuanced picture. Lugo (2019), in his analysis of large banks, identified a U-shaped relationship between insider ownership and debt costs, suggesting that while insider ownership may lower borrowing costs due to long-term stakes held by insiders, this effect diminishes beyond a certain threshold. Foreign ownership has also emerged as a critical factor, with Tran (2022) establishing a negative relationship between foreign ownership and debt costs for Vietnamese firms. He et al. (2013) further argued that foreign ownership strengthens monitoring mechanisms, which not only reduces borrowing costs but also enhances firm performance. These findings collectively underscore the integral role of corporate governance in influencing the cost of debt. Based on the present studies, we hypothesize that corporate governance characteristics, especially gender diversity, board independence, and ownership structure, greatly contribute to reduced debt costs.

In light of the aforementioned arguments, the present hypothesis emerged:

**H1a.** *Gender diversity in the board is negatively related to the cost of debt.*

**H1b.** *Board independence is negatively related to the cost of debt.*

**H1c.** *Insider ownership is positively related to the cost of debt.*

**H1d.** *Institutional ownership is negatively related to the cost of debt.*

**H1e.** *Foreign ownership is negatively related to the cost of debt.*

### 3.2 | Corporate Governance and ESG Disclosure

Board diversity and independence play a crucial role in influencing ESG disclosure practices. Studies have shown that diverse boards, particularly those with higher gender representation, are associated with improved ESG reporting. For instance, research on BRICS-listed companies demonstrates that board diversity and composition positively impact the quality and extent of ESG disclosures (Nuhu and Alam 2024). Women's distinct social and ethical perspectives are often linked to enhanced monitoring roles and a reduction in corporate misconduct (Gull et al. 2023). However, empirical findings on the relationship between gender diversity and ESG disclosure remain inconclusive. While some studies in the United States and Thailand have found no significant association (Suttipun 2021), others provide evidence of a positive correlation, particularly in contexts emphasizing governance structures (Arayssi et al. 2019). Independent directors also play a critical role in fostering effective decision-making and improving disclosure practices. Research highlights their capacity to mitigate agency problems, reduce information asymmetry, and promote transparency in corporate communications (Dah and Jizi 2018). Ownership structure is another key determinant of ESG disclosure. Institutional investors, for example, are found to enhance ESG transparency, thereby reducing information asymmetry and fostering investor confidence (Wang et al. 2023). Similarly, foreign ownership has been shown to positively influence ESG disclosure, with evidence from Indonesia indicating a significant contribution to firm value through enhanced reporting practices. The COVID-19 pandemic has further underscored the importance of non-financial disclosure, as investors increasingly prioritize sustainability considerations in response to heightened environmental and social challenges (Garel and Petit-Romec 2021).

In light of these discussions, we propose the following hypotheses:

**H2a.** *Gender diversity in the board positively affects ESG disclosure.*

**H2b.** *Board independence positively affects ESG disclosure.*

**H2c.** *Insider ownership negatively affects ESG disclosure.*

**H2d.** *Institutional ownerships positively affects ESG disclosure.*

**H2e.** *Foreign ownership positively affects ESG disclosure.*

### 3.3 | ESG Disclosure and Cost of Debt

ESG disclosure has been identified as a critical factor in the assessment of a firm's future risks and performance (Eccles et al. 2011). However, research examining the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt remains limited. For instance, Raimo et al. (2021) identified a negative correlation between ESG disclosure and cost of debt in their study of S&P 1200 companies, while Guidara et al. (2014) emphasized that ESG disclosure mitigates information asymmetry, enabling investors to more accurately assess a firm's default risk within the South African context. Lenders tend to penalize firms for opacity, and thus high levels of transparency are valued (Muttakin et al. 2020). Consequently, managers are encouraged to provide detailed information that minimizes the information asymmetry between firms and lenders, thereby reducing the cost of debt (Mazumdar and Sengupta 2005). Greater ESG disclosure benefits firms by improving transparency, which in turn reduces the cost of debt. The academic literature provides equivocal results regarding the relationship between board characteristics and the cost of debt (Benjamin and Biswas 2019). Consequently, non-financial disclosures, such as ESG, have become imperative in reflecting a firm's commitment to stakeholders, including addressing governmental, environmental, and social concerns. The benefits of ESG disclosure are twofold: it reduces information asymmetry and attracts more investors and cheaper credit (Eliwa et al. 2021). In addition, ESG disclosure enhances a firm's reputation, which further helps to reduce its cost of capital in the international setting (Martínez-Ferrero et al. 2015). To this end, it is incumbent upon managers to act ethically and provide transparent disclosure, leveraging their cognitive capital and specific expertise. This, in turn, suggests that ESG disclosure serves as a mediator in the relationship between corporate governance and debt costs. Consequently, corporate governance exerts both a direct and indirect effect on the cost of debt, with the indirect effect being mediated by higher ESG disclosure, a critical component of shareholder value. Based on the above, the following hypothesis is proposed:

**H3.** *There is a negative relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt.*

**H4.** *ESG disclosure mediates the relationship between corporate governance and cost of debt.*

## 4 | Method

### 4.1 | Data and Sample Selection

The present study encompasses non-financial companies listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange, with the exclusion of financial companies due to divergent reporting standards. The study considers A-share listed companies over the period 2016–2022, a timeframe selected because in 2016, the Chinese government mandated the disclosure of environmental information by large polluting companies (Meng-tao et al. 2023). With China being the world's second-largest economy, its market behaviors, regulatory frameworks, and institutional structures as the rapid economic growth, evolving corporate landscape, and increasing focus on ESG practices offer valuable insights into how governance and transparency are developing in emerged markets. An important aspect to note is that companies listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange (SSE) are not necessarily Chinese-owned. The SSE has attracted a significant number of foreign companies and international investors due to China's increasing integration into the global market. This phenomenon highlights the importance of considering the diverse nature of the firms operating within China's financial markets. Therefore, studying listed companies on the SSE provides a more comprehensive view of how corporate governance and ESG practices evolve in China's hybrid market environment, where domestic and international influences are intersecting. The corporate governance data was obtained from Chinese Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR), while the ESG score was obtained from Bloomberg. The sample consists of 314 companies from different industries, as categorized in Table 1.

The study examined a total of 314 companies, the majority of which were drawn from the petroleum, chemicals and plastics sector, accounting for 29.62% of the sample. The mining sector accounted for 13.28% of the sample, while the agricultural sector was the smallest, with 16 companies and 5.10% of the sample. #### 4.1.1 | Variables Definition

The variables used in this study are defined and measured as follows: the cost of debt, which serves as the dependent variable, is calculated as the natural logarithm of the firm's total financing costs divided by its total debt, which includes both short-term and long-term liabilities (Usman et al. 2019). The independent variables include board gender diversity, board independence, insider ownership, foreign ownership, and institutional ownership. The assessment of board gender diversity is determined by the percentage of female members on the board, thereby reflecting the influence of gender composition on decision-making processes (Pucheta-Martínez and Bel-Oms 2019). The concept of board independence is determined by the proportion of independent, non-executive directors, thereby highlighting the role of external oversight in governance. Insider ownership is measured as the percentage of shares held by individuals within the firm, with the potential to align managerial interests with those of shareholders. Foreign ownership is quantified as the proportion of the firm's equity held by foreign investors, offering insights into the internationalization of ownership structures (Gurbuz and Aybars 2010). Institutional ownership is defined as the percentage of a company's shares held by institutional investors, emphasizing the influence of professional stakeholders in corporate governance (Lin and Fu 2017). The present study incorporates the ESG score as a mediating variable, which is obtained from Bloomberg and ranges from 0 to 100. This score evaluates the firm's performance across environmental, social, and governance dimensions based on self-reported data.

Control variables include firm size and return on assets (ROA). Firm size is measured as the natural logarithm of total assets, thereby capturing the scale and capacity of the organization (Lin and Fu 2017). ROA, calculated as net income divided by total assets, serves as an indicator of financial performance and operational efficiency (Usman et al. 2019). These variables are integrated into the analysis to account for their potential influence on the relationships under investigation.

### 5.2 | Regression Results Governance and Cost of Debt

Table 5 provides a presentation of the regression findings in order to test the relationship between board governance and the cost of debt.

The coefficient relating to board diversity is negative (0.234***, 3.67). This is indicative of acceptance for H1a, which suggests a negative relationship between gender diversity on the board and the cost of debt. This indicates that the presence of women on the board has a beneficial effect in terms of lowering the cost of debt. This phenomenon may be attributed to the tendency of female board members to adopt a risk-averse approach and to prioritize the financial performance of the firm, resulting in a reduction in debt costs (Eliwa et al. 2021).

The present study contradicts the findings of previous research, for example that of Benjamin and Biswas (2019), which found no significant relationship between gender diversity and the cost of debt. The results suggest that debt investors, including financial institutions, are sensitive to and value gender diversity in boardrooms. This may be because women are more concerned about external debt (Aksoy and Yilmaz 2023). The results also support the signaling theory by demonstrating that the presence of women on corporate boards serves as a signal that reduces the cost of debt. The results not only support the presence of women on corporate boards on legislative, ethical and social justice grounds, but also on economic grounds, demonstrating that the presence of women on corporate boards can positively impact economic outcomes. In a broader context, the results support governance reforms regarding the inclusion of women in corporate boards, and specifically the Chinese government's reforms to corporate governance. The second model demonstrates that board independence is negatively associated with the cost of debt (−2.6*** (4.72)). Consequently, firms with more independent boards exhibit a reduced cost of debt, thereby supporting H1b. The findings concerning board diversity and board independence are consistent with the extant literature (Bradley and Chen 2015; Abdullah et al. 2016; Benjamin and Biswas 2019). This may be due to the fact that an independent board is regarded as a signal of effective corporate governance, thereby instilling confidence in debt investors and enabling firms to access more affordable financial resources (Kieschnick and Moussawi 2018; Derbali et al. 2022). Independent directors are considered the most effective custodians of stakeholders (Shailer and Wang 2015), and it has been demonstrated that board independence is most effective when minority shareholders are engaged (Shailer and Wang 2015). The findings of this study are consistent with this view in general, as board independence brings more transparency, tight control and protection of the interests of stakeholders, which reduces agency issues and ultimately leads to a lower cost of debt.

In contrast, the results pertaining to the ownership variable are intriguing. As anticipated, insider ownership has been demonstrated to have a positive correlation with the cost of debt (0.142***, 3.46), a finding that has been replicated in Model 3. This phenomenon can be interpreted in the context of agency theory, as insider ownership has the potential to accrue benefits to third parties at the expense of minority shareholders. This is further supported by the findings of Shailer and Wang (2015), who observed that board independence exerts a more pronounced effect on the cost of debt, particularly in instances where insiders leverage minority shareholders' interests. These observations are in alignment with the conclusions reported by Lugo (2019), who identified a U-shaped relationship between insider ownership and the cost of debt, while considering the control argument. The control argument posits that inadequate corporate governance enables insiders to exploit minority shareholders' interests for their own benefit. Consequently, recent governance reforms, including the inclusion of women in corporate boards and the appointment of a greater proportion of independent directors, have been empirically substantiated to result in enhanced performance and stakeholder protection (Sarang et al. 2024).

Conversely, institutional and foreign directors exhibited a negative and significant relationship with the cost of debt, as evidenced by Models 4 and 5, respectively. These findings substantiate the acceptance of H1d and H1e, suggesting that outside and foreign investors contribute to firm growth by reducing the cost of debt, thereby leading to enhanced wealth maximization. The presence of foreign directors may facilitate more effective monitoring of management due to their international experience and expertise (Tran 2022). The presence of foreign directors on the board conveys to financial institutions that the firm is reputable, as foreign directors are more likely to select reputable firms for investment (Tran 2022; Khan and Zhu 2025). Consequently, firms with a greater number of foreign directors may not have more access to external financing, but they may also have more affordable financing options. The role of institutional investors in firm governance is significant, as they can provide more financing and more affordable financing options for firms. For instance, Edmans et al. (2019) argue that institutional investors can influence firm governance through their influence and the ability to exit. Chen et al. (2023) emphasize the monitoring role of institutional investors, which enhances information transparency and mitigates agency issues. Consequently, the findings substantiate the pivotal function of institutional investors in Chinese firms, leading to a reduction in debt costs.

### 5.3 | Regression Results of Board Characteristics and ESG Disclosure

The regression results of the first path of corporate governance and ESG disclosure are presented in Table 6. The findings indicate that board diversity is positively correlated with ESG disclosure among Chinese A-share listed companies.

The results confirm the initial hypothesis, H2a, that gender diversity has a positive impact on ESG disclosure: 0.192*** (3.14) numbers in parentheses (e.g., 3.14) tell us how “strong” or “reliable” the relationship is. Higher numbers indicate that the result is more likely to be meaningful. The Prob>F values (in table bottom row) tell us the likelihood that these results are due to chance. A smaller value (less than 0.05, in most cases) means that the result is statistically significant. This relationship remains consistent across all individual pillars of ESG, indicating that enhanced governance leads to increased transparency in disclosure. The outcomes lend further support to the assertion of Eccles et al. (2020) that a lack of diversity hinders sustainability reporting and performance. ## 6 | Discussion and Conclusion

The results support all proposed hypotheses, demonstrating that specific mechanisms of corporate governance play a crucial role in enhancing corporate transparency and accountability through increased non-financial disclosures such as ESG. Similar results indicate that greater ESG disclosure enables debt investors to better assess a firm's default risk, as well as the environmental and social risks associated with its operations (Eliwa et al. 2021; Martínez-Ferrero et al. 2015). Specifically, the incorporation of women on corporate boards, a greater proportion of independent board members, and the presence of foreign directors have been associated with enhanced transparency and accountability, thereby fostering stakeholder confidence, including that of debt investors and banks (Torchia and Calabro 2016; Chiang and He 2010). Furthermore, the study considers the role of ownership structure and institutional investors in shaping the cost of debt, and the results suggest that institutional investors play a significant role in reducing the cost of debt, consistent with the findings of Anand et al. (2013). They argue that institutional investors contribute to risk diversification, signaling the firm's ability to manage risk effectively. Consequently, a higher presence of institutional investors not only serves to protect the interests of shareholders but also benefits debt investors by promoting a diversified risk profile, which ultimately lowers the cost of debt. This highlights the dual role of institutional investors in safeguarding both equity and debt stakeholders' interests through their influence on governance and risk management.

The present study addresses a significant gap in the literature by theoretically and empirically examining the mediating role of ESG disclosure in the relationship between corporate governance and the cost of debt among Chinese A-rated companies. The empirical results underscore the pivotal role of ESG disclosure in mitigating information asymmetry and fostering stakeholder trust (Waheed et al. 2024). The findings confirm that firms engaged in ESG activities tend to adopt strategies emphasizing transparency and accountability, attributes that are highly valued by various stakeholders, including debt investors. The observed negative relationship between board gender diversity, board independence, and the cost of debt suggests that specific governance mechanisms are instrumental in reducing information asymmetry (Tran 2022; Kim et al. 2019; Abdullah et al. 2016). The mediation analysis further strengthens this conclusion, revealing that ESG disclosure acts as a key enabler, enhancing the effect of governance mechanisms on the cost of debt. In particular, increased ESG transparency leads to a reduction in borrowing costs, emphasizing the value of non-financial information in shaping financial outcomes.

The study confirms underlined literature which states that having more females on board gives better input in the decision making and put efforts to gain the economic benefits of environmental investment like ESG. The female inclusion in the corporate board gives more information to the stakeholders and taking decision by considering the demands of different stakeholders, which cause reduce the agency issues in the companies. Therefore, females' presence is a great source to minimize the agency issues. Firms with females in the corporate boards send signals to the external environment regarding gender equality and diverse resources to make better decisions in the wider aspect of the stakeholders. Our results confirm that female inclusion provides signals to the debtholders who take this positive signal and offer debt at cheaper rates. Furthermore, results bear significant practical implications for corporate managers. The study highlights that improving the quality and transparency of ESG disclosures can directly reduce the cost of debt, thereby enhancing shareholder value. In this context, the study underscores the importance of adopting accounting strategies that prioritize non-financial disclosures, which can ultimately reduce borrowing costs and contribute to long-term financial sustainability. Policymakers are also encouraged to implement regulations and policies that promote corporate governance practices in alignment with ESG standards. Such policies could reinforce the role of transparency and accountability in corporate reporting, thereby fostering a more resilient and sustainable business environment.

In conclusion, this study emphasizes the critical role of ESG disclosure in enhancing corporate transparency and information quality. The findings suggest that firms that focus on improving ESG disclosures are likely to experience lower borrowing costs and higher firm value, contributing to a more sustainable and accountable corporate ecosystem.

### 6.1 | Limitations and Proposal for Future Research

The present study acknowledges several limitations that underscore significant avenues for future research. While it contributes preliminary insights into the relationship between board diversity, corporate governance, and the cost of debt, particularly in the context of ownership structure and the role of foreign and outside ownership, there remain numerous aspects to be explored. Future research could delve deeper into how specific dimensions of board diversity, such as ethnicity, and professional background or other specific characteristics. Another limitation is that this study focused exclusively on ESG disclosure as the mediation variable. This study employed a single measure of ESG disclosure; however, future research could test the robustness of these findings using alternative ESG metrics, such as ESG scores or performance-based measures. Future research could benefit from exploring additional mediating variables, such as organizational culture, sector and different law systems (civil law vs. common law). In addition, it is important to mention that the context of this research is based on a single country's financial listed companies. While the results are meaningful, generalizing them to other regions, particularly Western economies, requires caution. Furthermore, expanding the scope of this study could involve examining the relationship between corporate governance, ESG disclosure, and the cost of debt in different geographical contexts, through cross-regional or cross-cultural comparisons, such as between Western and Asian markets across various regulatory environments. This would enhance our understanding of how local contexts influence the effectiveness of governance mechanisms and ESG disclosures in reducing the cost of debt. Finally, extending the sample to include both listed, as A and B, and non-listed companies as small and medium enterprises or family firms would provide a more comprehensive perspective on the relationship between corporate governance and the cost of debt.
