# Are corporate social responsibility disclosures relevant for lenders? Empirical evidence from France

**Abstract**

**Purpose** – The purpose of this paper is to test whether or not CSR disclosure (i.e. aggregate as well as its three sub-indicators) reduces the cost of debt for French corporations listed in the SBF 120 index between 2010 and 2015.

**Design/methodology/approach** – CSR disclosure ratings of firms were collected from the Bloomberg database under three dimensions such as environmental, social and governance (ESG). Then, a pooled regression analysis was run.

**Findings** – The results indicate that overall CSR disclosure score as a combination of ESG disclosure scores has a negative effect on the cost of debt (i.e. lowers the cost of debt). While environmental disclosure is negatively associated with the cost of debt, social disclosure is unexpectedly positively associated, and governance disclosure has an insignificant association with the cost of debt.

**Research limitations/implications** – The study has two main limitations. First, the analysis does not consider contractual constraints and obligations that might exist in debt contracts (Jung et al., 2018). Second, the analyses cover a specific time period (i.e. between 2010 and 2015) for a specific country (i.e. France) excluding utilities and the financial sector.

**Practical implications** – Overall, it is inferred from the results that financial markets for lenders take into account CSR disclosure when assessing the creditworthiness of borrowers. Specifically, environmental disclosure is the only subdimension of CSR that is influential on creditors’ decisions to offer favorable interest rates. In line with this outcome, companies can assess their processes and be more aligned with eco-friendly practices, and investors are particularly advised to invest in those types of firms.

**Originality/value** – This study extends scant literature on the association between CSR and the cost of debt by exploring how creditors treat CSR dimensions dissimilarly in granting loans to firms. The findings of this study have particular importance as financial debt is one of the most predominant forms of external financing.

**Keywords:** Governance, Disclosure, Social, France, Cost of debt, Environmental

## Introduction

CSR reports cover a wide range of environmental, social and governance (ESG) information that is not reported in financial statements but could have significant implications for assessing investment efficiency and firm value (Dhaliwal et al., 2014; Zhong and Gao, 2017). While information in CSR reports is currently more abundant and diversified than ever before, there are doubts about the relevance of these publications. Certain academic researchers (Magnanelli and Izzo, 2017; Gong et al., 2018) ask whether firms use CSR disclosure as “window dressing” to “appear” socially responsible and thus improve their image without undertaking any effective activities. Hence, there is an intriguing question about the economic consequence of CSR disclosures. This paper questions the advantage of CSR disclosure by investigating the usefulness of this type of disclosure for lenders in granting loans. In particular, it focuses on their effect on the cost of debt borrowers incur. It aims to check whether lenders pay attention to CSR reports when assessing the creditworthiness of borrowers.

In authors’ opinion, banks are likely to apply better conditions on loans granted to transparent firms as compared to their opaque counterparts. CSR reports may provide extra-financial indicators relevant in evaluating firm risks and value, which in turn, may reduce the cost of debt financing. This study aims to investigate whether or not CSR disclosure reduces the cost of debt within the French context. It addresses the following research question:

*RQ1. Does the extent of the ESG information that is disclosed by French companies affect their cost of debt?*

Although a fair amount of empirical research regarding the effect of CSR on the cost of capital and the cost of equity has been published, few papers have been published regarding the cost of debt (Goss and Roberts, 2011; Gao et al., 2016). Thus, the aim of this study is to fill this void in the CSR literature by learning how CSR disclosure is perceived by creditors when granting loans to firms. Moreover, most prior studies have tested the influence of composite CSR variables (Cooper and Uzun, 2015; Gao et al., 2016) or a single CSR dimension (i.e. environmental or social) on the cost of debt (Kleimeier and Viehs, 2018; Jung et al., 2018). This study considers an aggregate score along with three separate individual CSR scores (i.e. social, environmental and governance) so that it enables to test which individual CSR score is more consequential. Actually, the need to investigate which aspects of CSR add value and which do not was previously voiced by Goss and Roberts (2011).

France is one of the world’s largest economies and its economic structure is still quite different from those of the Anglo-Saxon countries. However, little is known internationally about CSR practices and disclosures of French companies because few research works have been published on the subject. This paper examines the relationship between CSR disclosure and the cost of debt financing in the French context. France is a particularly interesting case to explore CSR issues for several reasons. It is among the countries that are the most committed to CSR and very involved in the development of European and international CSR standards (Hamrouni et al., 2019). In 2001, the French parliament adopted a law on “new economic regulations,” requiring nearly 700 publicly listed companies to communicate in their annual reports almost 60 indicators related to their CSR engagement and the environmental and social impacts of their activities (The Report of Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2012). Moreover, “in 2008, during the French presidency of the EU, France invited the European Commission to begin work on a European policy in this field, taking inspiration from French legislation adopted in 2001. In this way, France played a key role in the adoption of Directive 2014/95/EU of 22 October 2014, which obliges certain large undertakings and groups to disclose non-financial information. The directive was transposed into French law on 21 July 2017” (The Report of Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2017, p. 2). In 2009 and 2010, the Grenelle Acts adopted by the French Parliament make the production of an annual report on CSR matters for all large companies with activities in France mandatory. Provisions for the implementation of these laws were adopted by the government in April 2012 (The Report of Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2012).

The remainder of this study is organized as follows. The next section reviews the relevant literature and theoretical discussions extensively. The third section outlines the empirical specification followed by the results section. The fifth section discusses the findings considering past literature, which is followed by the conclusion section.

## Literature review and hypotheses development

According to the literature, the motivations that may explain the relationship between CSR disclosure and the cost of debt are disparate and relate to transparency, a firm’s risks, stakeholder engagement, and legitimacy and reputation.

The ability of a lender to assess factors that affect a borrower’s ability to repay debt is the key to successful lending practices (Jung et al., 2018). Opacity mostly constrains the lender’s ability to properly assess the firm’s repayment ability. In contrast, transparency around CSR performance is important in reducing capital constraints (Cheng et al., 2014). The engagement in CSR activities can contribute to firm financial performance (Servaes and Tamayo, 2013) by, for example, providing competitive advantages or enhancing its reputation (Saeidi et al., 2015). Thus, CSR disclosure could be an effective way to reduce uncertainty and improve transparency. They are likely to provide relevant extra-financial information for the assessment of the risk associated with a firm’s future performance and the level of its expected future cash flows (Verrecchia, 2001).

Prior studies have built on stakeholder theory (Freeman, 1984; Davis, 1960; Donaldson and Preston, 1995) to investigate the relationship between CSR and access to finance. Cheng et al. (2014) examine how stakeholders’ engagement could affect the relationship between CSR and access to finance among publicly listed firms. They argue that firms with superior CSR performance have better stakeholder engagement and less opportunistic behavior (Benabou and Tirole, 2010; Eccles et al., 2012). These firms have lower contracting costs (Jones, 1995) and, as a result, better access to finance. The findings of Cheng et al. (2014) confirm those of Waddock and Graves (1997) and Carroll (1999) which show that firms that pay attention and address the needs of their stakeholders (employees, community, customers and regulators) are more likely to access external financial resources. Recently, Magnanelli and Izzo (2017) examined the impact of CSR performance on the cost of debt based on the instrumental view of stakeholder theory. They suppose that CSR engagement helps firms to create value, be accountable to larger groups of stakeholders and gain reputational advantages. According to the authors, this could affect firm performance and consequently reduce debt costs. However, their empirical findings contrast with their predictions and show a positive relationship between corporate social performance and the cost of debt, suggesting that CSR is not a value driver with an impact on the firm’s risk profile.

It is essential for a firm’s survival and sustainability to preserve its legitimacy and improve its reputation, particularly in a competitive and dynamic environment where reputation may have a major impact on the firm’s economic value. The increasing public attention to CSR puts great pressure on firms to communicate information regarding their social and environmental activities to preserve their legitimacy. As a result, CSR disclosures become a key tool for firms to establish and enhance their legitimacy (Chen and Roberts, 2010). From this perspective, prior literature demonstrates that CSR reports can improve a firm’s legitimacy (Kuo and Chen, 2013; Mathuva et al., 2017; Cho and Patten, 2007). This mitigates its reputational risk (Kleimeier and Viehs, 2018; Chava, 2014) regarding environmental externalities) and leads to better access to debt financing (Cheng et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2018). However, there is no unanimous position in the literature, as CSR disclosure can be perceived by certain practitioners as marketing or “window dressing” to appear socially responsible without any real and effective engagement (Magnanelli and Izzo, 2017). In this context, if stakeholders or, *inter alia*, banks do not recognize the potential of CSR reports to improve firm reputation, firms having a high level of CSR disclosure could suffer reputational disadvantage.

The effectiveness of CSR disclosure in reducing the financing cost relies on whether it can capture the attention of funds providers. If CSR disclosure attracts lenders by providing them relevant extra-financial indicators useful for firm assessment, a high level of CSR disclosure implies a reduction of information asymmetry between lenders and firms, and, subsequently, an improvement in transparency. Hence, banks will apply better conditions to loans granted to the firm. In contrast, if the financial market does not recognize the relevance and potential of CSR reports to reduce uncertainty, firms disclosing extensive environmental and social information may suffer from a competitive disadvantage due to the additional costs that it will incur from the resources spent for these reports. Khlif et al. (2015) state that banks evaluate the firms’ interest rate with respect to the future risks including the adverse effect of future regulatory costs imposed by the government and other stakeholders. That is why the authors suggest that firms disclosing social and environmental information can enjoy a lower cost of debt, which, accordingly, increases its performance. Only a few studies empirically examine the relationship between CSR disclosure and the cost of debt. Sengupta (1998) demonstrates that firms with high disclosure ratings from financial analysts enjoy a lower effective interest cost of issuing debt. According to the authors, a policy of timely and detailed disclosure reduces lenders and underwriters’ perception of default risk for the disclosing firm, reducing its cost of debt. Gao et al. (2016) find that firms with higher quality CSR disclosures attract greater analyst coverage, higher levels of institutional ownership, and greater stock liquidity, which in turn implies higher valuations in equity offerings and lower yields to maturity in bond issuances. Orens et al. (2010) confirm the negative empirical relationship between social and environmental disclosure and the cost of debt. Using a sample of Chinese firms, Gong et al. (2018) provide evidence that firms with high CSR disclosure quality are associated with lower costs of corporate bonds, suggesting that CSR disclosure can offer incremental information beyond credit ratings. Focusing on carbon-risk, Jung et al. (2018) check whether lenders have favorable perceptions of firms with greater carbon awareness. They demonstrate that penalties are effectively negated for firms that publicly disclose carbon-related information, suggesting that lenders incorporate a firm’s exposure to carbon-related risk into lending decisions through the cost of financing.

In more general CSR terms, the findings are mixed. Several empirical studies demonstrate that the adoption of CSR increases the availability of debt (Cheng et al., 2014; Ansong, 2017) or reduces its cost (Sengupta, 1998). Cheng et al. (2014), Sharfman and Fernando (2008) and Ansong (2017) provide evidence that superior CSR performance results in easier access to external funds such as bank loans, debt and equity issues. Attig et al. (2013) and Jiraporn et al. (2014) find that better CSR performance is associated with higher credit ratings and lower bond yield spreads. In addition, Oikonomou et al. (2014) examine the impact of corporate social performance on the pricing of corporate debt and the assessment of the credit quality of specific bond issues. They find that higher levels of corporate social performance can lead to improved credit quality and lower perceived credit risk. One year later, Ge and Liu (2015) examine how CSR performance is associated with the cost of new bond issues and find that better CSR performance is associated with stronger credit ratings. In a supplementary analysis, the authors examine CSR strengths and concerns separately and find that CSR strengths are associated with lower yield spreads while having many CSR concerns is directly related to higher spreads. However, other empirical studies document a positive relationship between CSR performance and the cost of debt. For instance, Goss and Roberts (2011) provide evidence that superior CSR performance resulted in higher loan spread. They find that firms with social responsibility concerns pay between 7 and 18 basis points more on their bank loans than firms that are deemed more responsible. Similarly, Chava (2014) and Du et al. (2017) show that firms with social responsibility or environmental concerns are associated with higher costs of bank debt. Sharfman and Fernando (2008) find that firms with good environmental performance bear higher bond yields but also have higher leverage.

In light of the literature review, this study proposes that firms disclosing extensive CSR information are likely to provide relevant extra-financial indicators useful for the firm’s assessment of, *inter alia*, its repayment ability. This mitigates information asymmetry, improves transparency and reduces risks. In this context, banks can be incited to apply better conditions to loans granted to the firm, which translates into lower cost of debt. Accordingly, this study assumes a negative relation between the cost of debt and the level of ESG information disclosed by firms; the following hypotheses are drawn:

*   **H1.** CSR disclosure level has a negative association with the cost of debt.
*   **H2.** Social disclosure level has a negative association with the cost of debt.
*   **H3.** Environmental disclosure level has a negative association with the cost of debt.
*   **H4.** Governance disclosure level has a negative association with the cost of debt.

## Empirical specification

### Sample
The initial sample for this study consists of French corporations listed in the SBF 120 index between 2010 and 2015. Following prior studies (Chen et al., 2018; Oh et al., 2019; Riyadh et al., 2019), two industries (i.e. regulated utilities and financial firms) were excluded because they are exposed to specific disclosure and accounting regulations and financing policies. In addition, after excluding companies with a large amount of missing data, 80 firms were on hand to run the analyses.

CSR disclosure ratings of firms were collected from the Bloomberg database under three dimensions such as ESG. The Bloomberg database has been the data source for numerous prior studies examining the effects of CSR disclosure on various business practices (Giannarakis, 2014; Dardour and Husser, 2016; Hamrouni et al., 2019).

### Empirical model
As formulated below, econometric regressions on panel data were conducted to study the impact of CSR score on the cost of debt on French firms, where *i* and *t* represent the firm and time vectors, respectively:

$$Cost\ of\ Debt_{i,t} = f(CSR\_Score_{i,t}, Control\ Variables_{i,t}) + \epsilon_{i,t}$$

Consistent with Magnanelli and Izzo (2017), the cost of debt was measured by the ratio of financial interests’ expenses on total financial debt. In the Bloomberg database, the ESG scores range from 0 for companies that do not disclose ESG information to 100 for companies that disclose extensively.

Several firm-specific characteristics including size, leverage, profitability and price to book ratio were controlled. Firm size is measured by the natural logarithm of total assets. Leverage is the ratio of total debt to total assets. Profitability is measured by the ratio of net income to total assets (ROA). The price to book ratio is measured by the market value divided by the book value of equity.

Certain tests were run to determine the appropriate estimation method. The result of the Hausman (1978) test shows that the random-effect models are more suitable than fixed-effect models ($p > 5$ percent). Heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation tests were conducted in order to verify the absence of bias likely to alter the significance of coefficients. The results of modified Wald tests reveal the presence of group-wise heteroskedasticity between errors. Finally, the test of Wooldridge indicates the presence of autocorrelation of errors of order 1. For the empirical estimation, it is possible to use the Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) method, which allows relieving the problems of heteroskedasticity across panels. This method offers potential efficiency gains in the presence of heteroskedasticity (Miller and Startz, 2019). Consistent with Samet and Jarboui (2017), Fonseka et al. (2019) and González et al. (2019), this specification was preferred because it is a more efficient estimator than the random effect and produces more robust results.

### Regression results
First, the regression analysis was run to test the composite effect of CSR score (i.e. ESG_Score) on cost of debt. The statistically negative significant coefficient of ESG_Score at 0.05 level shows that overall effect of CSR on the cost of debt is negative (i.e. it lowers the cost of debt), and thus *H1* is accepted (Table III). This implies that CSR plays a positive role in enhancing companies’ reliability and reducing interest on debt (La Rosa et al., 2018). This result confirms several recent studies in other jurisdictions such as in China (Huang et al., 2018), Australia (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2019) and the USA (Bae et al., 2019). Subsequently, in order to test which individual CSR dimension is more consequential, CSR sub-dimensions were incorporated individually into the analysis. The results are not straightforward and are slightly surprising. While ENV_Score has a significant negative coefficient at the 0.05 level, SOC_Score, against expectations, has a significant and positive coefficient at the 0.05 level, and the GOV_Score coefficient has a non-significant association with the cost of debt. Thus, *H3* is accepted but *H2* and *H4* are rejected. Therefore, while environmental disclosure decreases the cost of debt which is consistent with Fonseka et al. (2019), social and governance disclosures increase and have no effect on the cost of debt, respectively. The increase in the cost of debt as a result of social disclosure might be attributable to the fact that shareholders are likely to perceive these kinds of engagements as opportunistic behaviors of managers (Magnanelli and Izzo, 2017), or wasteful, excessive and costly consumption of scarce firm resources. Although the findings encourage firms to continue investing in overall CSR initiatives (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2019), they remind firms that creditors consider individual CSR indicators in a different way. Particularly, creditors are more likely to offer lower interest rates to firms that show greater environmental disclosure but higher interest rates to firms which exhibit greater social disclosure, and they do not differentiate firms in suggesting higher or lower interest rates based on their governance disclosure.

## Discussion

In the following paragraphs, the findings are discussed regarding the composite CSR variable and individual CSR variables separately, considering the prior literature.

The results support the argument that overall CSR disclosure results in favorable borrowing conditions. The findings are in line with prior studies that have found a negative association between both CSR performance and the cost of debt (Oikonomou et al., 2014; Cooper and Uzun, 2015), as well as CSR disclosure and the cost of debt for firms (Anis and Utama, 2016; Gao et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2018). In an interview-based study, 9 out of 16 interviewees confirmed that disclosure to a bank or rating agency would affect the cost of bank loan or a bond issue in such a way that it would make access to debt easier and reduce the cost of debt (Armitage and Marston, 2007). Drawing conclusions from a sample of SMEs, Ansong (2017) proved that CSR activities help firms better position to access external financing. The reason why CSR activities and disclosure reduce the cost of debt is that creditors view them as a self-regulation mechanism which mitigates potential future regulatory costs (Khlif et al., 2015). Second, firms that engage in CSR activities or disclosure perform better in credit ratings and have a lower credit risk (Hsu and Chen, 2015).

Even though significant positive association between social disclosure and the cost of debt is surprising and unexpected, this study is not the first to find this contrasting evidence within the theoretical background. Previously, Magnanelli and Izzo (2017) found this contra-evidence[1], and they attributed this relation to the costly diversion of firm resources or the opportunistic behaviors of private benefit-seeking managers. Villarón-Peramato et al. (2018) voiced the argument recently that managers may invest in CSR to seek private benefits and maximize their own utility function. Another reason might be because firms engage in social activities more than the necessary compliance level so that the debt market punishes them by imposing high interest rates (Sharfman and Fernando, 2008).

Some studies have paid particular attention to the relationship between environmental disclosure and the cost of debt. For example, recent studies of Kleimeier and Viehs (2018) and Jung et al. (2018) proved empirically that there exists a significant and negative association between the voluntary disclosure of carbon emission levels and the cost of bank loans. Thus, the findings confirm this expected negative relationship between environmental disclosure and the cost of debt.

The expected negative association between governance disclosure and the cost of debt is based on agency theory such that sound governance practices can mitigate information asymmetry between managers and stockholders and constrain opportunistic managerial behavior (Byun, 2007), which in turn, reduces the cost of capital. However, the study could not prove this inverse relationship and found an insignificant association between the governance dimension of CSR and the cost of capital, which is contrary to the findings of prior studies (Byun, 2007; Anderson et al., 2004; Bhojraj and Sengupta, 2003). To explain this unexpected outcome, it would be useful to refer to descriptive studies. They indicate that the mean value of governance scores (58.25) is somewhat higher than the means of other CSR dimensions. Additionally, the standard deviation of governance scores (9.25) is considerably lower than the other CSR dimensions’ scores. Therefore, a relatively high mean value of governance might imply that French firms reached some level of maturity in this CSR dimension, and a low standard deviation shows a low level of variation among firms and high homogeneity. As a result, these two factors might have caused the insignificant coefficient of governance variable in the regression equation.

Overall, what can be inferred from the results is that lenders are selectively supporting and providing modest incentives for firms and may be punishing some firms for paying too much attention to stakeholders beyond threshold levels (Goss and Roberts, 2011).

## Conclusion

The purpose of this study is to investigate whether or not CSR disclosure of French firms lowers their cost of debt. For this purpose, the CSR data were derived from the Bloomberg database for listed French firms covering a time period between 2010 and 2015. Thus, this study provides a recent evidence on this subject matter.

The results indicated that overall CSR disclosure as a combination of ESG disclosure scores have a negative effect on the cost of debt (i.e. lowering the cost of debt) which is in line with expectations. However, the individual effects of CSR dimensions on the cost of debt are inconclusive. Contrary to expectations, social score is positively associated with the cost of debt (i.e. increasing the cost of debt), which was surprising. In line with expectations, environmental disclosure is negatively associated with the cost of debt (i.e. lowering the cost of debt). A non-significant effect of governance performance on the cost of debt was also unexpected.

Although the results are not totally as expected, they have implications for firms, policy-makers and investors. The findings of the study have particular importance as financial debt is one of the most predominant forms of external financing and as CSR investments trend increasingly upwards (Magnanelli and Izzo, 2017). Overall, it can be inferred from the results that financial markets for lenders take into account extra CSR disclosure when assessing the creditworthiness of borrowers. This is probably due to better estimation of future risks of potential debtors. Moreover, environmental disclosure is the only subdimension of CSR that is influential on creditors’ decisions to offer favorable interest rates. According to this outcome, firms are advised to review their operations if they want to lower their cost of debt. They can elaborate on their processes and be more aligned with eco-friendly practices. In this respect, overall governance and in particular board structure of the firms might be redesigned, and necessary human financial and human resources reallocated. Furthermore, investors should consider the outcome of the study in making investment decisions and formulating their portfolios. They are particularly advised to invest in firms which have high environmental performance and disclosure. Concerning social disclosure, if companies are punished by the lenders for addressing stakeholders’ concerns too much, Goss and Roberts (2011) suggest that this situation requires the government’s intervention to mitigate negative externalities. Otherwise, borrowers who perform high in social issues but are charged higher interest rates relative to their counterparts might change their corporate CSR policies and decide to reduce social engagements. This finding also highlights a discrepancy between the importance of corporate social engagement as promoted by the French market authority and the perception of lenders to this engagement. Thereby, this study may be of interest to French market authorities looking to converge the perceptions of all stakeholders, including lenders, to the importance of social engagements of French companies. On the other hand, the governance indicator has the highest mean value but the lowest standard deviation among three individual ESG indicators. This implies that governance scores of firms are closely distributed around the mean value of this indicator which means firms disclose almost equivalent information. The non-existence of much variation among firms in this respect might be the reason for not having a significant association between governance indicator and cost of debt. The findings lend support to theoretical discussions as well. The results might imply that CSR disclosure contributes to the reduction of information asymmetry, reduces legitimacy concerns, helps investors better assess firms’ political and social risks, reflects firms’ long-term perspectives, and helps companies build a favorable reputation, particularly in a stakeholder-oriented environment such as France.

The study is not free from limitations. First, the analysis does not consider contractual constraints and obligations that might exist in debt contracts (Jung et al., 2018). Second, the analyses cover a specific period of time (i.e. between 2010 and 2015) for a specific country (i.e. France) excluding utilities and the financial sector. A further cross-country study can be carried out to test the validity of the results. Moreover, the study can be replicated in other countries such as emerging and less developed areas where interest rates and the perception of CSR activities might differ so that the outcomes might provide different implications.
