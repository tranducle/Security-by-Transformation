### ABSTRACT

As sustainable investing becomes central to capital allocation in many markets, the Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) metrics and scores have become of critical importance. This study aims to analyze the relation between the cost of debt and ESG scores. We provide evidence that the cost of debt for borrowing firms is associated with their ESG score/rating. Firms that have low ESG scores are considered to be riskier, in the sense that they are exposed to liabilities related to Environmental, Social and Corporate factors that ultimately increase their probability of default. In case of a bankruptcy unsecured bondholders' claims may be subordinate to those liabilities. Using a framework for evaluating ESG performance developed by Refinitiv, this study investigates whether firms within S&P 500 over the period 2010–2019 that exhibit strong ESG scores benefit from lower bond spreads and better bond ratings relative to firms with weaker ESG scores. After controlling for other risk characteristics our analysis points that better ESG rating is associated with lower cost of unsecured debt in the primary bond market. Our findings are consistent over the aggregate metric and all E, S, and G pillars. Furthermore, they support the ongoing calls for improved transparency, which would allow for the reporting of quantifiable ESG information in firms' disclosures.

## 1. Introduction

Sustainability has become a topic of rising importance across the world, mobilizing governments, civil society, and business to adopt a wide range of new practices. Following the call to action of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the rising focus of investors to non-financial reposting, an increasing number of US companies are measuring, disclosing, and managing sustainability risks and opportunities. Performance over Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) metrics is considered to be an important factor that reflects companies' ability to generate value and execute effective strategies.

Capital markets and the financial sector are already reflecting this tendency and are more and more embracing the integration of ESG factors into their business practices, as well as their respective corporate reporting requirements. This is evidenced by the increasing ESG assets under management, which are estimated at $30 trillion in 2019, above $35 trillion in 2020 and are on track to exceed $50 trillion by 2025. They therefore represent more than a third of the projected $140.5 trillion in total global assets under management, according to Bloomberg Intelligence's (BI) ESG 2021 Midyear Outlook report (Bloomberg, 2021). This report also notes that the $3 trillion ESG debt market could grow rapidly to $11 trillion by 2025, assuming it expands at half the pace of the past five years. Growth is driven by companies, development projects and central banks focused on the pandemic and green-recovery efforts, the push to net zero emissions and record low interest rates. Investors and exchanges are leading the integration of ESG metrics in the mainstream practices of the business and financial sectors, thus, enhancing transparency and encouraging sustainable investment practices. A growing number of firms are also placing sustainability and long-term value creation high on their agenda. An increasing number of them have already adopted transparent corporate ESG disclosure and are positioning capital flows towards sustainable investments.

Nowadays, ESG has become more relevant than ever especially for the E pillar. More specifically, over the recent years the increasing concerns by investors (as well as the regulators, the policymakers, the citizens, and – as a result – the investees) for the risks associated with the E pillar (environmental and climate risks) have resulted in the expanding usage of E scores/ ratings in investment analyses and decisions. However, E pillar metrics may not be able to capture the full spectrum of related risks. E scores are affected by the availability and the quality of the reporting data provided by the firms and are mainly focused onto GHG emissions and resource use (i.e. water usage/ management, waste management). Furthermore, in terms of actual environmental impact, E-metrics structure fails to distinguish differences between pollutants (e.g. particulate matter and oxides of sulfur and nitrogen versus greenhouse gases). Finally, E-scores comparison limitations arise across different providers as well as from variations in industry groupings across different ESG providers (S&P, Refinitiv etc.).

Partly materialized or not fully incorporated in the E pillar scores/ ratings environmental and climate risks, may also lead to significant financial implications both for businesses and investors and consequently affect securities prices and returns. From the three components of climate risk (physical, transitional and regulatory), regulatory risk has started to materialize (Krueger et al., 2020), and has become a major channel through which environmental risks get priced in securities. However, the regulatory risk is not well accommodated by the E-pillar metrics.

Businesses that are dependent on long-term investment are likely to face larger regulatory and environmental risks in the future, because the consequences of climate change increase over time. Hence, the industries of energy, construction, real estate, transport and infrastructure are likely to be most affected by climate change impacts. Particularly, the energy sector is most affected by regulation on fossil fuels that is threatening oil, gas and coal industries. Environmental Protection Agency regulations in the U.S. have already contributed to a significant decline in coal use by U.S power plants. In 2021, about 546 million short tons (MMst) of coal were consumed in the United States and this was the lowest amount since 1964 and the second-lowest percentage share after 2020 of total U.S. annual energy consumption since 1949 (US Energy Information Administration). Moreover, divestment campaigns by large institutional investors, including university endowments are growing fast, with a total of 1485 institutions representing $39.2 trillion in assets worldwide already committed to a divestment from fossil fuels (Stand.earth, 2021). Another risk is the concept of stranded assets that also affects industries such as steel, aluminum, cement, plastics, and greenhouse horticulture. Large scale projects (like off-shore oil rings) and major fossil fuel deposits are at risk of losing market value due to unanticipated breakthroughs in renewable technology and governments stepping up climate policies (Van der Ploeg and Rezai, 2019).

This paper offers a fresh look into the implications of ESG performance for the bond investors. The conceptual framework is based on the view that high ESG scores that reflect high level of ESG practices relate positively to the solvency of a firm. Firms with high ESG scores can control more effectively a significant part of environmental, social, legal, reputational, operational, and regulatory risks. Firms that perform poorly in ESG metrics can engage in activities and practices that result to an increased amount of liabilities which ultimately affect their default risk and thus the value of their fixed income securities. Bond investors, who need to protect themselves, require a better assessment tool of how the different corporate activities related to ESG factors impact credit risk– and thus yield, before making their decision. This study aims to provide a better insight into this issue. The analysis focuses on newly issued, fixed-rate US bonds, which offers a series of advantages as (i) the risk premium can be easily measured through spreads over the risk-free rate of T-Bills; (ii) bond prices at issuance are more likely to beat a fair, representative spread - rewarding for the associated risks (as is usually concluded in cooperation with investors and investment banks); (iii) primary bond markets are less influenced by other market factors, such as liquidity and momentum, which becomes more important in the secondary markets (Chen et al., 2007); and (iv) the credit rating of a bond at issue date, in most of the cases, is attributed specifically to the issue and as such it is more representative of and likely to effectively control both for issuer and bond specific characteristics.

The afore mentioned discussion unveils the novelty introduced by this paper, which lies in the realization that the comprehensive ESG score as well as the individual E, S, and G pillars have a negative (and thus beneficial for the borrower) impact on the bond yields (for the sample and time period under examination). Consequently – and as will become evident from the literature review that follows, it is the only study (to the best of the authors' knowledge) that provides solid evidence of this effect. Its findings can be of value to all stakeholders; investors, investees and regulators who are more and more considering ESG-compliant investing as a means to direct the disposable funds to the desired activities from an environmental, social and governance perspective.

## 2. Literature review

The literature review is structured in a way that addresses first the studies capturing the relation of ESG (management) and the risk borne by bond investors, followed by the research pertaining to the impact of ESG (scores) on bond performance (yield).

### 2.1. ESG context and bond investor risks

In the last two years the Covid-19 pandemic and the worsening climate crisis have proved without doubt that bond investors should, among others, be concerned with the quality of ESG management of borrowing firms. Companies nowadays are increasingly exposed to ESG potential risks and liabilities that arise from their operations and from the legal and regulatory framework. This has become eminent in the time period of the pandemic, during which particular sectors of the global economy such as tourism, oil, plastics, mining, transportation, airlines etc. have been hit hard and proved most vulnerable on ESG liabilities and regulatory risks that ultimately affected their solvency. The ESG context is unveiled via two dimensions; the ESG management within the firm and the ESG regulation and legislation within its operating environment. They both influence the stance of investors as they affect the assumed risk of their investments.

### 2.2. ESG management

To understand the importance of ESG (management) let us consider the E (Environmental) pillar and the airline industry as an example, which is one of the benchmark investment sectors of our globalized economy and closely related to growth in several other sectors of the world economy. Nevertheless, the airline industry is considered responsible for 12% of the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions emitted by transport in total around the world, but also for 2% of all GHG emissions worldwide. Long before the pandemic, the pressure of the public opinion about the need to limit such heavy polluting air travel had already begun to develop. When in 2020 the Covid-19 pandemic hit hard the global airline industry, governments globally saved several iconic carriers; but these carriers are now facing the aggravating climate change and direct pressure to reduce their climate footprint, which is also hitting their activities hard.

The airline industry may be an example; however it indicates that firms that perform poorly on ESG management are more vulnerable and can incur significant costs, fines, and damage claims, for example for environmental violations. Such liabilities increase the default risk of borrowers, and they may also rank higher to the claims of the debt holders. Furthermore, if these liabilities turn out to pose a significant risk to the firm's financial health, management may resort to a strategic bankruptcy in an attempt to avoid them. Poor management of ESG associated risks increases the likelihood of future liabilities, and bond investors should expect a higher default risk for borrowers and a consequently higher expected loss of their investments.

Beyond the direct costs that can incur from poor ESG management there are also more general implications that can affect the firms. Stakeholders, who are sensitive to ESG issues or are supervised on such issues by competent state authorities (e.g., financial institutions, insurance companies, pension funds, etc.), highly avoid reputational risks resulting by ESG mismanagement in firms that are related with. A downgrade in key stakeholder relationships can affect the value of the firm and increase its default risk. Konar and Cohen (2001) document that the size of legally emitted toxic chemicals is inversely correlated with the intangible asset value of the firm.

Additionally, poor ESG management in firms can result in difficulties when seeking cost-effective funding either from banks or investors or even negatively affect the trading volumes of their securities. In financing firms with poor ESG practices, banks are becoming increasingly cautious not only of the associated reputational and credit risk but also the risk to find themselves liable to environmental obligations (Balkenborg, 2001; Kroszner and Strahan, 2001). Goss and Roberts (2011) report that firms with worse social responsibility scores pay a little bit higher bank rates than the most responsible firms.

Furthermore, a literature strain has suggested that ESG compliance and/or high ratings correlate-with and may even serve as a surrogate for managerial traits responsible for the low-volatility anomaly and Bowman's paradox. ESG management refers to challenging environmental impact (E), social impact (S; workers' health and safety, and diversity), and governance (G; corporate ethics, shareholders' rights, and executive compensation policies) by using non-financial factors. In his respect, it incorporates a broad range of management and risk strategies that may enhance a firm's financial performance and reduce its volatility. The ongoing research on the correlation between ESG and performance covers investment, financial and management angles, exploring such topics as low volatility anomaly and Bowman's paradox (Chari et al., 2019).

Within the ESG context, the low volatility anomaly transforms to the question whether ESG compliant/ highly rated companies present lower volatility and better risk-adjusted returns in their securities over the long term. Respectively, Bowman's risk paradox transforms to the question whether for ESG compliant/ highly rated companies, there is a negative correlation between the financial performance and its corresponding variance. The research on both streams remains vivid and contributes interesting ideas and very promising fields of future ESG research, among others, new risk measures for ESG, investment selection, management goal selection, diversification strategy on risk and return.

Ashwin Kumar et al. (2016) demonstrate that companies that incorporate ESG factors present lower volatility in their stock performances than their peers in the same industry, that each industry is affected differently by ESG factors, and that ESG compliant/ highly rated companies generate higher risk-adjusted returns. This positive correlation between ESG and risk-adjusted returns presents to be stronger for the better ESG-rated stocks. Furthermore, a report from NYU Stern Center for Sustainable Business and Rockefeller Asset Management (Whelan et al., 2021), which examines the relationship between ESG and financial performance in >1000 research papers from 2015 to 2020, finds a positive relationship between ESG and financial performance for 58% of the “corporate” studies focused on operational metrics such as ROE, ROA, or stock price with 13% showing neutral impact, 21% mixed results (the same study finding a positive, neutral or negative results) and only 8% showing a negative relationship. For investment studies typically focused on risk-adjusted attributes such as alpha or the Sharpe ratio on a portfolio of stocks, 59% show similar or better performance relative to conventional investment approaches while only 14% find negative results. The report also finds positive results when reviewing 59 climate change, or low carbon, studies related to financial performance. On the corporate financial side, 57% arrive at a positive conclusion, 29% a neutral impact, 9% mixed and, 6% a negative conclusion. Finally, looking at investor studies, 65% show a positive or neutral performance, compared to conventional investments with only 13% indicating negative findings.

### 2.3. ESG regulation

The public demand as well as regulatory driven requirements to limit investments with questionable or irresponsible ESG practices inevitably leads institutional investors to adopt transparent ESG criteria when building their portfolios. The increasing unwillingness of the institutional investors to finance, buy and hold securities of non-ESG compliant firms increases the risk and the premiums asked for these firms (e.g., Garber and Hammitt (1998) find that the Superfund liability (CERCLA) in the US imposes financial risk on investors and thereby increases the firms' costs of capital).

Laws and regulation on ESG dimensions cover a wide range of existing both corporate and investment activities. However, the risk of regulatory and supervision expansion has increased considerably in the last decade and will remain at high level for the following years. Growing concerns over climate change and sustainability have led to new agreements, regulation and laws internationally, such as the Paris Climate Agreement (2015), EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (2019), EU Taxonomy Regulation (2020), etc.

Tightening international regulation, increased ESG coverage in media, public pressure on US policy makers and recent US's decision to reinstate the Paris Agreement will eventually lead the US's states and federal government to stricter regulation and criminal and civil penalties for violations. Consequently, bond investors in high-risk ESG industries particularly in the environmental pillar, should take a very conservative approach and incorporate in their investment decisions the appropriate ESG metrics that are able to recognize and quantify the associated risks.

Expanding regulation and stricter laws result in increased compliance and operational costs that burden a firm's earnings and cash flows. At the same time, they can impose significant new investments that need additional fund raising. In the future, it is expected that the importance of ESG to investment decisions will increase further across all industries and will also necessitate consistent and more accurate ESG reporting from the firms. However, ESG in a standalone basis should not be used as an exclusion criterion for investments and investors should not in principle abstain from firms with low(er) ESG scores; instead, when they invest in them, they should try to influence them so that they improve their ESG performance.

### 2.4. ESG and bond performance

In the literature there are two strands of research on the relation between securities' risk premia and their ESG ratings. The first focuses on the association of ESG performance- the E and S pillars being on the front research line-with the corporate cost of debt, measured mostly in terms of bond spreads over risk free treasury bonds derived from data in primary or secondary markets. The analysis further extents to the association of ESG performance with a firm's bond and long-term ratings. The second strand studies the effect of ESG performance on stock returns and market values (Matos, 2020). As this paper investigates the impact of ESG performance on the cost of debt we present the literature of the first strand only. In this strand though, we can still identify four main directions; namely, the studies that address one of (i) the E, (ii) the S, and (iii) the G pillars, as well as (iv) the overall ESG score.

On the E pillar the literature is conclusive on an inverse relation between a firm's cost of debt and its performance on the environmental issues. The better the Environmental performance of the firm is, the lower its cost of debt becomes. This is a cross industry characteristic that is consistent over different time periods. Furthermore, expanding environmental regulation is a driving factor behind the strengthening of this relation in the recent years. The implications of this relation are also evident on the assessment of a firm's overall credit risk. The firms with better environmental performance also have better credit ratings.

Early studies report on a credit risk effect for misreported environmental liabilities (Graham et al., 2001; Graham and Maher, 2006), and show that the environmental practices of firms are reflected on the pricing of newly issued bonds in anticipation of the associated risks. Chava (2010) investigates loan facilities made to US-based firms and finds that corporations with several environmental concerns need to pay significantly higher interest rates on their loans. Schneider (2011) discusses how accurately the financial reporting for environmental issues according to accounting standards and practices reflect a firm's past or present environmental liabilities to find that the understatement or omission of environmental liabilities increases credit risk, because when these obligations are finally realized they may significantly exceed the ones reported on the balance sheet.

Going to bond performance as expressed by bond yields/ spreads, Bauer and Hann (2010) explore the effects of environmental scores on bond issue spreads on newly issued bonds (of 582 U.S. public companies across multiple industries between 1995 and 2006), to conclude that environmental concerns are related to higher spreads and proactive environmental practices are associated with a lower cost of debt. Schneider (2011) studies bond prices for bonds in the secondary market in two high-polluting industries in US (pulp and paper, and chemical), to find evidence of a significant relationship between environmental performance and bond spreads, which however, fades with bond quality. Chava (2014) analyzes the impact of a firm's environmental concerns on its cost of equity and debt capital, to observe that firms with such concerns are charged by investors by higher expected cost of equity (using an accounting-based implied cost of equity measure, using analysts' earnings forecasts) and by lenders by higher cost of debt (measured loan data). Baker et al. (2018) analyze the pricing of U.S. municipal and corporate green bonds (purposed to raise money for climate and environmental projects), to realize that municipal green bonds are issued with lower spreads, whereas green bonds are held more than ordinary bonds. Seltzer et al. (2020) using data both from the primary and secondary bond markets document that climate and other environmental regulatory risks affect corporate bond pricing. They also notice that a firm's environmental profile and regulatory environment have a statistically and economically significant effect on credit ratings and spreads. Lower credit ratings (and higher yield spreads) for low environmental score firms and high-emission firms are more noticeable for firms operating in a state where environmental regulations are enforced more stringently. Further, exogenous events such as the signing of the Paris Agreement and the subsequent U.S. withdrawal under President Trump provide evidence for causal effects.

On the social (responsibility) front, the anticipated inverse association of the S pillar with the cost of debt and the credit risk is not conclusive. The literature provides mixed findings over different industries that also depend on the datasets used and the time periods under consideration. Menz (2010) examines the relationship between the valuation of Euro corporate bonds (secondary markets) and the standards of CSR of mainly European companies to find no link between CSR and credit spreads concluding that CSR has apparently not yet been incorporated into the pricing of corporate bonds. Goss and Roberts (2011) realize that social responsibility concerns result in an increase in bank loan costs as lenders are more sensitive to CSR concerns in the absence of security. The study also documents a mixed reaction to discretionary CSR investments. Stellner et al. (2015) using data from secondary markets for European bonds analyze whether there is an inverse relationship between corporate social responsibility (CSR) results and credit risk to find only weak evidence that high CSR performance results to reduced risk. However, the ESG score of the country that a firm operates in affects the results, in the sense that a high CSR score for a firm is only rewarded via lower credit spreads if the firm's country of origin also has a good country-level ESG score. Amiraslani et al. (2017) study whether a firm's social responsibility is favorably assessed by bondholders in the secondary markets to see that corporate social capital only matters for corporate bond characteristics (i.e., spreads, principal raised, maturities) during the 2008–2009 financial crisis while no relation is documented over the period 2006–2019. During the crisis, firms with high social capital benefited from being able to raise more capital at lower spreads, and for longer maturities. Bannier et al. (2022) studies both the E and S pillars using data for the period 2003–2018 to conclude that only environmental aspects reduce different measures of credit risk for U.S. firms, whereas both environmental and social aspects do so for European firms. Further they find that credit ratings do not reflect these credit-risk reducing effects of corporate social responsibility.

The G pillar studies are rather limited and exhibit similar results to the S pillar. Gao et al. (2021) use the staggered state-level adoption of constituency statutes by firms, which allow directors to consider stakeholders' interests when making business decisions, in order to examine the causal effect of stakeholder orientation on loan spreads to suggest that stakeholder orientation could reduce borrowing cost through the governance channel. Amiraslani et al. (2017) – mentioned also in the S pillar - finds no evidence that the governance element of ESG is related to bond spreads.

As far as the comprehensive ESG score is concerned the existing research presents mixed results. Barth et al. (2022) study the impact of ESG on CDS credit spreads in markets and find a reduction in credit spreads for firms with good ESG scores. The economic magnitudes, however, are small (for example, in the social factor higher performance is related to increased spreads) and vary across industries. Yang (2020) studies the link between ESG scores and credit ratings by taking advantage of the time lag in the adoption of ESG issues in the credit agency ratings (2015 by Moody's and S&P and later in 2017 by Fitch), to realize that the recognition of ESG issues contains some useful information in terms of predicting future default. Nevertheless, there is no evidence suggesting that these ratings have become better or worse in terms of predictive quality.

The findings of the just-presented literature need to be interpreted with the understanding that the way the ESG metrics are built and calculated is not uniform. As a result, different pillar constituents and weightings produce different scores and relevance across industries. Thus, the research outputs and their strength may deviate in terms of comparability as well as a cross different industries, weighting and scoring techniques. Furthermore, the bond spread panel data from primary market that feeds in the regression models, may contain discrepancies such as multiple bond issues of different maturities for a firm at the same issue date, that complicate calculations with panel regression models because the assumptions of independence of observations and residuals may be violated. Lastly, there is an inherent correlation between the credit ratings and the ESG scores, as ESG considerations are partly incorporated in the calculation of credit ratings. In particular, the G pillar's questioned relation with the cost of debt probably distorts the strength of correlation of the two other pillars with it and results to a reduced strength for the total score. However, we may intuitively support the ambiguity in G pillar's relation (as proxied by its constituent metrics) with the cost of debt, as the credit rating/ scoring methods employed in practice for lending incorporate also an assessment of the quality of the management of a firm that goes beyond its payment credibility.

The aforementioned presentation of the relevant literature revealed the gap that the present study aims to address; namely to provide supportive evidence that the ESG scores (standalone and by pillar) are cross sectional determinants in the assessment of credit risk as depicted by bond yields/ spreads. This realization lifts any potential ambiguity on the impact that these variables may have. Its findings are in line with these of Bauer and Hann (2010), Chava (2014), and Seltzer et al. (2020). It further expands the analysis by Sharfman and Fernando (2008), Bauer and Hann (2010), Schneider (2011), and Amiraslani et al. (2017) to all ESG pillars and a combined ESG score, for newly issued bonds.

## 3. Data description & statistical analysis

The main aim of our paper is to explore the relation between ESG performance of issuing firms and bond spreads in the primary markets. Before we elaborate on our analysis, we briefly describe our sample and perform some univariate tests.

### 3.1. Data description

For our analysis we use ESG, financial and bond data for S&P 500 companies derived from Refinitiv for the period 01.01.2010–31.12.2019. It is well known that ESG ratings differ considerably among alternative raters as discussed in Berg et al. (2019). According to this study the divergence of ESG ratings constitutes a challenge for the investors in their investment assessment. In this respect, it actually reduces the amount of ESG performance that is reflected on corporate stock and bond prices. Additionally, the divergence hinders the efforts of the companies to improve their practices, because of the mixed signals from different rating agencies about which actions are better valued by the market. The ambiguity around ESG ratings also represents a challenge for public policymakers trying to contribute to an environmentally sustainable and socially just economy. Finally, the divergence of ratings is a challenge for empirical research, as the choice of a rater versus another may even alter a study's results and conclusions.

We analyze only newly issued (at issue) unsecured fixed-rate corporate bonds (senior or subordinated). We exclude bonds with options or other non-standard components (callable, puttable, convertible, exchangeable, preferred, perpetual, asset-backed, private placements etc.). Refinitiv reports 3605 bonds in total (FRNs included for the period. However, the sample for fixed-rate bonds with reported yield spreads and bond ratings (either from Moody's or S&P) at issue date is reduced to 1540 bonds. These bonds come from 157 companies and the maximum number of bonds for a company is 392. About 72% of the companies have 1–4 bonds issued. We further cross check our data items excluding registries with missing values or “0” across all ESG and other explanatory variables. On certain cases with missing financial values, we cross-check inputs with companies' annual reports. Although a “0” value for an ESG score is valid and actually represents a non-reporting status - which is a negative ESG indicator, we exclude it from our analysis. Such a data item may result from a number of different causes including reporting delays since sustainability reports usually take time to finalize and can create discrepancies in our analysis that is seeking to measure the strength of association between yield spreads and ESG performance.

We pool the firms of our sample into 8 groups of industries (2-digit SIC). The large majority of bonds comes from the Finance, Insurance, Real Estate sector (53.51%), followed by the Manufacturing industry (29.81%) and the Transportation & Public Utilities (8.44%). ### 3.2. Dependent variables

The dependent variable is the market quoted yield spread of newly issued bonds which expresses the firm's cost of debt. The yield spread equals the difference between the offering yield to maturity of a bond at issue date and the yield to maturity of a corresponding treasury bond. It reflects the premium that the market charges to borrowers above the risk-free rate for the risk that they may default on their debt obligations. We analyze the cost of debt in its log form, ln(Spread), because of the significant positive skewness in the yield spread distribution. In methodological terms, the model we plan to estimate for each sample bond $i$ with yield spread $y_{i,t}$ at date $t$ is as follows:

$y_{i,t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG_{i,t} + \beta_2 Issuer Characteristics_{i,t} + \beta_3 Bond Characteristics_{i,t} + s(i) + \gamma(t) + \delta(j) + \varepsilon_{i,t}$

where:
* ESG represents 4 different measures either aggregate or dimensional according to Table 5. No specific pillar categories scores are tasted.
* Issuer Characteristics is a vector of independent variables according to Table 6.
* Bond Characteristics is a vector of independent variables according to Table 6.
* $s(i)$, $\gamma(t)$ and $\delta(j)$ denote industry sector fixed effects, time fixed effects, and country of issue fixed effects, respectively.

As a bond rate we use the grade that is available on the Refinitiv database either from Moody's or S&P. When both ratings exist, we use the highest of the two. We then transform the ratings to a numerical 7-grade scale (7: highest, 1: lowest). Table 4 presents how the grades are transformed to our (ordinate) numerical bond rating scale.

We observe that only 0.32% of the bond issues in our sample receive rating grade 7 in our numerical rating scale (that is AAA/Aaa), while 12.14% receive grade 6, 50.65% grade 5 and 34.03% grade 4 (grades from 4 and above correspond to investment grade bonds).

### 3.3. Independent variables

As ESG performance metric, we use Refinitiv ESG pillar scores that outline a company's ESG performance in a single E, S or G pillar. Each pillar score is based on the aggregate of three to four critical category scores depending on the pillar. The ESG Combined Score provides a rounded and comprehensive evaluation of a company's ESG performance based on the reported information in the ESG pillars, with ESG controversies overlay captured from global media sources. The table below presents the different aggregate ESG scores we test as control variables in our models and how they breakdown to categories.

The ESG scores are actually percentile rank scores (available in both percentages and letter grades from D- to A+), benchmarked against Thomson Reuters Business Classifications (TRBC – Industry Group) from Refinitiv – for all ESG categories as well as the controversies score – and against the country of incorporation for all governance categories. The pillar score is a relative sum of the category weights which vary per industry for the Environmental and Social categories. For Governance, the weights remain the same across all industries. The pillar weights are normalized to percentages ranging between 0 and 100.

In our analysis we also incorporate two more vectors of control variables, one for the financial characteristics of the firm and one for the bond. Both vectors have been identified as robust cross-sectional determinants of credit risk in several studies (e.g., Bauer and Hann, 2010; Ashbaugh-Skaife et al., 2006; Bradley et al., 2008; Bhojraj and Sengupta, 2003; Cremers et al., 2007). Table 6 below presents the independent variables that account for issuer and bond characteristics.

The explanatory variables for the financial characteristics of issuers are expected to capture the different risk factors for the yield spreads in the primary corporate bond market as well as the bond ratings at issue dates.

Interest Coverage denotes the interest coverage ratio, i.e., earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) divided by interest expense. For banks interest expense equals interest on Fed Funds Purchased/Securities sold under Repurchase Agreement plus interest on other borrowing plus interest on deposits. Interest expense for Utility and Insurance sectors represents the sum of operating and non-operating interest expense net of capitalized interest for the year.

The bond-specific variables should also account for the yield differences across different bonds issues. By including these variables, we investigate how the credit risk, the size/liquidity risk, bond seniority and maturity, all together, affect yield spreads.

Time to Maturity is calculated in years to avoid scale discrepancies. For the same reason we use the natural logarithm of the issue size.

Subordinate is a Boolean variable that equals one if a bond's order of repayment classifies as senior-subordinated. The rank in terms of payment is negatively correlated with the spread of the yield of a bond.

The expected relation between the issue size of a bond and its default risk is ambiguous. Large debt issues are generally associated with higher liquidity, and thus expected to reduce the issue yield spread. However, higher debt obligations also imply a higher default probability for the bond issuer, and higher expected absolute loss for the bondholder in case of insolvency. ## 4. Empirical analysis: baseline results - all bonds considered

The analysis estimates a panel data model using the multilevel mixed-effects Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) regression using Kenward–Roger for calculating the degrees of freedom. More specifically, the dominant methodological approach to the coefficient model estimates is based on the principle of maximum likelihood (ML) estimations (Bickel, 2007). The justification comes from the argument that when the assumptions of independence of observations and residuals are violated as in the case of varying parameter estimates, maximum likelihood estimators provide parameter estimates that are relatively consistent, asymptotically normal and efficient (Card, 2001).

However, the ML estimator of variance components can be biased downwards because it does not adjust for the degrees of freedom lost by estimating the fixed regression coefficients. Patterson and Thompson (1971) introduce the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to address the limitations of the ML. This methodological approach adjusts for the degrees of freedom lost due to the estimation of the fixed effects parameters by maximizing the likelihood of linearly independent residual error (Laird and Ware, 1982; Lindstrom and Bates, 1988). With the REML method only the variance components are included in the likelihood function.

The empirical results are reported in Table 9 with models one through four considering the four alternative proxies for the ESG Score, i.e., total, environmental, social and governance, respectively. The results indicate that across all four model specifications, the ESG variants exert a negative impact on bond yields. In terms of the other controls, the estimates are in accordance with theoretical expectations. More specifically, the drivers of time to maturity and subordinate exert a positive impact on bond yields. By contrast, the drivers of issue size, interest coverage (statistically insignificant), size of the firm and assigned bond ratings have a negative effect on bond yields. In terms of diagnostics, evaluation with the REML convergence criterion indicates that with a value closer to zero indicates better model fit, while smaller values of the AIC (Akaike criterion) indicate overall better model fit and lower out-of-sample predictive error.

The fact that all ESG pillars, as well as the total ESG scores are significantly negatively correlated at the 1% level with the bond yields indicates that investors already privilege issues (or issuers) that have adopted a policy that respects the relevant dimensions. As a matter of fact, the magnitude of the coefficients indicates that the total score results in the maximum impact on the bond yields, followed by social, governance and environment. This is probably due to the fact that environment is already high in the agenda, which means that it is already priced in more compared to the other two dimensions. Governance follows, as potentially investors expect the management of the investees to apply good management practices. Social is most likely not fully embedded in the cultures of the companies; hence, the firms that have implemented policies with a positive societal impact enjoy lower borrowing costs.

Time to maturity seems to have comparable effects in all four models, as evidenced by the size of the coefficients. As anticipated the longer the time to maturity, the higher the yield (positive and statistically significant at 1%), as investors expect a higher remuneration for lending the issuer for extended periods of time.

Subordination has the biggest positive (significant at 1%) impact in all four models as judged by the size of the coefficients. The impact seems to be bigger in model 4 (governance), followed by model 2 (environmental), followed by model 3 (social) and finally by model 1 (total). As a matter of fact, besides ESG scoring, it is the credit risk that primarily affects yields; hence subordinate issues enjoy less protection and thus investors require/ expect higher yields. A potential explanation of the order could be that investors consider subordinate issuance primarily a governance issue, as it is the management/ board of directors that takes such decisions. Environmental issues are definitely broadly communicated and the risks emanating by not fostering environmentally friendly policies have already captured the attention of the investors who anticipate higher yields from subordinate issues. Social issues have also been promoted; hence a similar rationale applies. Finally, ESG has only recently been widely considered and to some is not even well defined; therefore, its importance to investors seems to be lower than the other 3 as expressed by the respective coefficient.

The issue size and the size of the firm both have a negative and statistically significant at the 1% level effect on the bond yields. They are almost zero for all four models. This means that investors are willing to receive lower yields for larger firms, which are potentially the ones that offer the larger issues, thinking that their investment carries comparatively lower risk.

The assigned bond rating has a negative and statistically significant impact at the 1% level to the bond yields for all four models. This reflects the fact that the higher the credit rating the lower the bond yield. Their order of magnitude follows precisely the order of ESG and at the same time it is the second after subordination. This potentially reflects the realization made in our discussion in the literature review section that ESG ratings are to a great extend embedded to the total credit ratings. In addition, it could possibly mean that firms that exhibit high ESG scores have already embedded their principles into their policies/ strategies. At the same time, looking at investors, it could be that they consider that firms with high credit rating have room to improve their ESG policies and thus ESG scores.

A natural question that could be raised by an investor is: Considering the ESG scores next to the financial position of a company, which one does prevail in the shaping of the bond yield? To answer such a question (for which we thank the anonymous reviewers) we first realize that the credit rating of a company with a weak financial position will (most likely) be lower than the credit rating of a company with a stronger financial position. Consequently, we can make the comparison based on the credit score and the ESG score, which are comparable, in the sense that they are both ratings. Having established that, we consider two directions; the first relies on the way ESG scores are taken into account in ESG ratings, whereas the second is based on the outcomes of our models.

When it comes to credit ratings, it is apparent that nowadays (post 2019) rating agencies have integrated ESG into their credit analysis (Fitch, 2022; Moody's, 2022; and S&P Global, 2022). As a matter of fact, 27 credit rating agencies (among which the major ones) have signed the “ESG in credit risk and ratings” statement as drafted in the Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI, 2022). The statement has also been signed by almost 180 fixed income investors, whose assets under management amount to over USD 40 trillion. The statement considers that ESG factors can influence the cash flows of the borrowers and their probability of defaults. As such, ESG factors have a material impact on the evaluation of the creditworthiness of the said borrowers (both corporate and sovereign). At the same time, ESG and credit analyses are considered as separate and should not be used interchangeably (PRI, 2022). The effect of the inclusion of ESG metrics in credit ratings has already been captured by academic research as well (see for example Chodnicka-Jaworska, 2021), to realize that ESG risk changes do have a strong negative impact on the credit rating changes (for the considered sample). Furthermore, the G component is an important constituent of the credit analysis as it reflects the governance principles implemented by the management of the company, as well as its capacity to manage the company successfully and thus repay its debt. As a result, we can claim that credit rating – and thus the global financial position of a company – does prevail over the ESG score, which is a part of the analysis of the financial position of the company.

When it comes to the results produced by our models, we realize that both ESG scores (either the individual environmental, social and governance or the comprehensive ESG score) and bond credit ratings have a negative and significant impact (at the 1% level) on the bond yields (Table 9). When comparing the coefficients of the models, we observe that in all four models the coefficient of the credit rating is approximately 9 times the coefficient of the E, S, G and ESG score in models 2, 3, 4 and 1 respectively. More precisely, the E, S, G and ESG score coefficients are −0.626, −0.942, −0.774 and −1.162, respectively, whereas the corresponding assigned bond rating coefficients are −5.485, −8.809, −6.911 and −10.244 (Table 9). This implies that credit rating is by far more important than the ESG score – as judged by the magnitude of their coefficients - in the formation of the bond yield (as evidenced by the sample under investigation). We can thus infer that the financial position of a company – as depicted by its credit rating - prevails over the ESG score of the company in the determination of its bond yield.

All in all, the very definitions and calculations of the credit ratings and the ESG scores, as well as the evidence provided by our analysis indicate that the overall financial position of the company prevails over the E, S, G and ESG scores.

Based on one of reviewer's concerns about the employment of the log transformation in our variables, the analysis reruns the estimates without using this transformation, with the new findings being reported in Table 10. The new results clearly provide solid empirical support to those in Table 9, although the size of the estimated coefficients now is highly larger than before.

The findings can be valuable to the hands of all stakeholders; namely, issuers, investors, regulators as well as policymakers and the society. Issuers can realize that by fostering ESG aligned strategies can enjoy lower borrowing costs. Investors can expect that ESG compliant firms will offer them a lower yield, which somehow though reflects more prudent management and thus justifies the lower return. Regulators can enforce the introduction of ESG criteria in the investment portfolios and in such a way steer investors to firms with high ESG score quality and at the same time induce investee firms to adopt ESG friendly management actions (which can also be beneficial to their borrowing cost). Policymakers can promote legislation (even at a global level) that fosters the principles of ESG investing so as to immediately realize the benefits to the environment and the society. Last but not least, the society can enjoy the merits of the compliance with the ESG dimensions as firms will return value to the environment and the society as a whole.

## 5. Conclusions and future research

The investment decision is a selection process based on return considerations, market conditions, and risk assessment. ESG is a tool and a framework that is able to broaden the investors' risk universe and improve the quality of their investment decision. For the firms, it comprises a reporting and compliance requirement and a conceptual framework to analyze and manage the associated risks. In this respect, ESG is an important facilitator for better investment decisions and management. However, how is ESG performance related to value and what are the causal factors? How do we define good ESG performance when looking at solvency and returns? Does ESG contain more useful information for investors and lenders than credit ratings? Do high ESG scores for a firm lead to higher returns in the equity market and reduce the cost of financing? If so, then our models should all be revised accordingly, and the investors and the CEOs of the firms should base their decisions on a new risk-return universe fully incorporating ESG. Moreover, regulators should take decisive steps in order to increase transparency and standardize ESG reporting, and lawmakers should use it as an incentive policy tool for promoting sustainability.

Nevertheless, the answers are still open-ended. Intuitively, low scores for the environment, community, human rights and governance may actually be linked to a higher cost of debt, although the results especially for the secondary market are inconclusive. Some ESG factors matter significantly, some to a lesser extent and others do not. The factors themselves may variate among industries in terms of materiality. For example, environmental scores are better linked to lower bond yields to certain industries such as agriculture, forestry, pulp, fishing, chemical and mining industries. But in other industries such as transportation, communication, and trade sectors, high community scores may actually lead to a higher cost of debt. An explanation may be that they signal to investors that the company is focusing on immaterial issues. Lumping ESG to single scores may also create statistical insignificance while decomposing overall performance into its individual components may deliver a statistical relevance to a particular pillar or category. Finally, the significance of some specific ESG metrics may vary overtime. For example, time-employee relations scores seem to be insignificant during recessions, but they can reduce bond yields in booming times.

However, the relation between ESG and the cost of debt is clearer than what is perceived by most investors. The primary market proves to be efficient in incorporating ESG risks in pricing. In most of the cases, the price of a bond at issue is a negotiation outcome between the firm and the underwriters (mainly investment banks) who are sensitive and increasingly supervised by their competent authorities to ESG matters. In this respect, the spread of the bond is more likely to reflect ESG risks than the spread of the same bond in the secondary market, where several investors are unwilling or unable to incorporate in their return profile the ESG associated risks. Our findings provide evidence that all pillars of ESG (E, S, G and Total) have a negative and significant impact to bond yields. Furthermore, the drivers of time to maturity and subordination have a positive and significant effect on bond yields. By contrast, the drivers of issue size, size of the firm and assigned bond ratings have a negative and significant effect on bond yields. Finally, interest coverage has a negative but statistically insignificant influence on bond yield.

Although this paper supports and advances earlier available research in the field, there are still items that need to be further investigated. These include, among others, the differences in the strength of ESG relevance among industries, the evolution over time, and the impact of changing regulation. Our future analysis will further elaborate on the causal factors of the relationship of ESG with bonds and explore how ESG scores along credit ratings can be used by the investors to reduce credit risk and the firms to reduce borrowing costs.

Finally, we acknowledge sample limitations in our dataset. The low availability of bond spreads at issue date on our database, presents potential problems as to sample size and the ability to generalize the results. However, our research fully supports earlier findings that ESG is a critical investment consideration on bonds.
