### ABSTRACT

This study reveals a negative correlation between the quality of ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) disclosure and the cost of debt financing among Chinese listed companies from 2011 to 2020. Furthermore, this relationship is only significant for non-SOE firms, firms in areas with higher marketization level, firms with lower accounting transparency, firms voluntarily publishing social responsibility reports, or firms operating in non-high-pollution industries. These results are robust to a series of tests including two-stage regressions based on instrumental variable and alternative measures of disclosure quality based on textual analyses.

## 1. Introduction

Chinese listed companies began engaging with ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) information disclosure relatively late but have increasingly recognized its significance for the capital market in recent years. As stated by the global head of MSCI ESG, Brian, “A substantial increase in response rates to ESG ratings from Chinese companies: from less than 14% in 2017 to 30% in 2020, reaching a level almost on par with the emerging market average by the first half of 2021.” Brian also observed that most efforts by Chinese listed companies to enhance their ESG ratings are focused on strengthening the disclosure of ESG-related information.

To encourage corporate disclosure of ESG-related information, the Chinese government has developed a series of normative policies. In 2015, the revised “Environmental Protection Law” of China was implemented. This law explicitly requires heavily polluting enterprises to publicly disclose detailed environmental information. Concurrently, in the same year, the China Securities Regulatory Commission standardized the environmental information disclosure in annual reports of key polluting units among listed companies, and also encourage others to voluntarily disclose environmental information. In 2016, the People’s Bank of China, the Ministry of Finance, and five other ministries jointly issued the “Guiding Opinions on Building a Green Financial System”, requiring financial institutions to incorporate corporate environmental risks into their credit assessment processes. This collaborative effort reflects China’s growing commitment to integrating environmental considerations into its broader economic and financial policies. It is evident that China’s current policy approach to ESG disclosure emphasizes mandatory disclosure for key enterprises and voluntary disclosure for others.

This paper aims to examine the relationship between ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) disclosure quality and debt financing costs in China, and try to delve into the specific mechanisms of action and the heterogeneous impacts of this relationship. This relationship is pivotal as it reflects how investors and financial institutions perceive the risk and sustainability aspects of a company. Specifically, we utilize A-share listed companies in China from 2011 to 2020 as research samples and adopt the ESG disclosure scores for A-share firms from Bloomberg to measure firms’ ESG disclosure quality.

Firstly, we find that there is a negative correlation between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. Higher ESG disclosure quality leads to lower corporate debt financing costs. This finding supports the theory of information asymmetry theory. High-quality ESG disclosure reduces the level of information asymmetry between banks and enterprises and lowers the risk that credit institutions need to bear, thereby reducing the corporate debt financing costs. Furthermore, high-quality ESG disclosure can be perceived as a positive signal differentiating a company from those with poorer ESG performance, indicating lower ESG risk and, consequently, lower debt financing costs.

Secondly, in the cross-sectional analyses, we show that the nature of a company’s ownership (state-owned vs. non-state-owned) and the degree of marketization in its operating environment significantly influence how ESG disclosure quality affects debt financing costs. In state-owned enterprises and in less marketized regions, the impact of ESG disclosure quality on debt financing costs is less pronounced. This suggests that the mitigating effect of ESG disclosure quality on debt financing cost is closely related to the nature of the enterprise and its operating environment. When market factors dominate, with less government intervention and political objectives, ESG disclosure quality better manifests its information value in helping firms reduce their debt financing costs. And we also find that accounting information transparency also moderates the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. When a company’s accounting information transparency is high, the relationship becomes insignificant. These results indicate that financial information transparency continues to dominate credit institutions’ lending decisions. However, with the ongoing development of ESG investment, non-financial information represented by ESG is expected to play an increasingly important role.

Next, we explored the impact of regulatory policies on the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt. First, we demonstrate that the negative relationship is only significant in companies not mandated to disclose social responsibility reports. This result suggests that banks and other credit institutions place more emphasis on voluntarily disclosed ESG information, as the incremental information value contained in mandatory ESG disclosures could be relatively less. Second, we examine the difference between heavily polluting and non-heavily polluting listed firms based on the official classification. Specifically, we follow two official guideline lists, i.e., “Listed Company Environmental Protection Inspection Industry Classification Catalogue” and “Listed Company Environmental Information Disclosure Guide”, to classify firms in 16 industries as heavily polluting. We find that the negative relation between ESG disclosure quality and cost of debt is not significant for these heavily polluting industries. The potential reason for these findings is that heavily polluting companies disclose ESG information primarily due to regulatory pressures, to demonstrate their fulfillment of basic legitimacy. These disclosures are considered by banks and other lending institutions as “necessary” basic information. Therefore, such disclosures do not earn these companies “extra rewards” from lending institutions, and thus do not reduce their debt financing costs. These findings also highlights the direction for policy-making, suggesting a need to also focus on heavily polluting companies and those mandated to disclose social responsibility reports, to enhance their willingness to improve ESG disclosure quality.

Lastly, we conduct three types of robustness tests. First, we adopted a different methodology to assess the quality of corporate ESG disclosures. Specifically, we collected more than 7800 independent ESG reports from all A-share companies from 2011 to 2020, and conducted a textual analysis to count the total numbers of characters or words in these reports as alternative measures of ESG disclosure quality. While counting the characters or words of reports is admittedly simplistic, this method has been widely used in previous literature. For example, Peterson (2012) the number of words in the revenue recognition footnote, Filzen and Peterson (2015) count the number of words in the accounting policies footnote, and Guay et al. (2016) count the number of words in a 10-K report. We expect that this method provides a straightforward means to assess a company’s disclosure efforts. Empirically, we assume that a higher count of characters or words reflects a higher quality of ESG disclosure and the tests show highly significant results consistent with our main findings. As ESG reporting is a disclosure practice by companies themselves, as opposed to third-party quality scoring, this approach offers a unique perspective and serves as a valuable supplement.

Second, we replace the level of debt financing cost with the annual changes in debt financing costs and find that better ESG disclosure quality is also linked to the decrease in debt costs. This finding lend further supports previous results, suggesting that high-quality ESG disclosure help reduce debt financing costs.

To further address potential endogeneity, we also adopt an instrumental variable approach and use Confucian culture as an instrumental variable, employing a 2-stage least square regression model. The rationale is that ESG disclosure aligns with Confucian principles emphasizing social concern over purely economic gains. The influence of Confucian culture is measured by the prevalence of imperial scholars (jinshi) from the Ming and Qing dynasties within 100 km of a company’s registered address. The logic is that areas with more imperial scholars had a stronger Confucian educational environment, which likely influences current corporate behaviors, including ESG disclosure. And this IV passes both the unidentifiable and the overidentification tests. The regression results remain similar, confirming the validity of our main conclusion.

The contribution of this paper mainly lies in two aspects: First, this research contributes to the existing literature on the economic consequence of ESG disclosures. For instance, many studies find higher firm value for firms that voluntarily make ESG disclosures (Matsumura et al., 2014; Khan et al., 2016; Martin and Moser, 2016; Naughton et al., 2019; Bucaro et al., 2020;). More recently, Chen and Xie (2022) find a positive correlation between ESG disclosures and corporate performance, especially in companies with ESG-focused investors, long-standing establishments, those receiving significant media coverage, and those with higher operational costs. Li et al. (2022) observed a U-shaped relationship between ESG disclosures and green innovation in highly polluting companies, suggesting an initial cost burden before yielding positive effects. Xi and Wang (2022) identified that ESG disclosures could reduce stock price crash risk by lessening information asymmetry and investor sentiment. He et al. (2022) further supported this by confirming that ESG disclosures, as an additional non-financial information source, can significantly reduce companies’ unique risks. Our research further supplements the aforementioned studies by discovering that the beneficial effects of transparent and comprehensive ESG reporting, not only in terms of enhancing corporate reputation and investor confidence but also in facilitating more favorable conditions for debt financing.

Second, this study enriches the limited international studies on the relation between ESG disclosure and cost of debt. Eliwa et al. (2021) provide evidence that the impact of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt is significant in 15 EU countries and is more dominant in the stakeholder-oriented countries. Raimo et al. (2021) also shows a negative effect of the ESG disclosure on the cost of debt financing, using the constituent firms of S&P 1200 Global Index, with only 3 firms from China. In another study, Feng and Wu (2023) focus on the global REITs and find that REITs with higher levels of ESG disclosure have lower cost of debt, higher credit ratings, and higher unsecured debt to total debt ratio. Overall, this stream of literature is quite limited and there is little evidence from emerging markets such as China. As we know, China has some noticeable differences from Europe and the United States regarding the institutional environment for ESG reporting, mainly reflected in aspects such as regulatory frameworks, reporting standards, and transparency requirements. Thus, our study also tries to contribute by exploring this relation among public firms in China, where the capital market currently places emphasis on the disclosure of corporate ESG information. For example, In June 2018, the China Securities Regulatory Commission issued guidelines for corporate governance, laying the foundation for ESG reporting standards for listed companies. This move was further advanced in 2021 with revisions to annual and semi-annual reporting rules, encouraging more firms to disclose ESG information.$^1$ This development of these standards not only contributes to the global discourse on ESG but also enhances the comparability and reliability of ESG data, facilitating more informed decision-making by global investors. Our study provides empirical evidence that policies promoting greater transparency in corporate ESG behavior positively influence financial outcomes. Specifically, it demonstrates that companies adhering to these policies tend to incur lower debt financing costs.

The rest of this article is as follows: the research background and literature review are presented in Section 2. Section 3 shows the data sample and research design. Empirical results are reported in Section 4. Section 5 shows that additional analyses of regulatory policies and Section 6 contains robustness test. Section 7 conclude the paper.

## 2. Research background and literature review

### 2.1. Institutional background

To encourage companies to actively fulfill social responsibilities and disclose information regarding environmental, social performance, and corporate governance, the Chinese government has enacted a series of regulatory documents and policies. The disclosure of environmental and social performance information by listed companies began late. In 2002, the China Securities Regulatory Commission, in conjunction with the Ministry of Commerce, issued the “Corporate Governance Guidelines for Listed Companies,” which for the first time incorporated social responsibility into the scope of corporate governance, emphasizing its importance. In 2008, the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges required companies listed on the Corporate Governance Sector, Shenzhen 100, those issuing foreign-listed foreign shares, and financial companies to disclose social responsibility reports and encouraged other qualified listed companies to voluntarily disclose such reports. This development marked a milestone in the policy evolution of mandatory ESG or CSR reporting in China.

The practice of environmental information disclosure among Chinese enterprises began later and was mostly voluntary, with companies having autonomy over the content. In March 2015, President Xi Jinping chaired a meeting of the Political Bureau of the Communist Party of China Central Committee, where the “Opinions on Accelerating the Development of Ecological Civilization” were adopted. This meeting marked the official incorporation of the concept “Lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets” into central government documents. Concurrently, in the same year, the revised “Environmental Protection Law” of China was implemented, significantly strengthening the legal framework for environmental protection. This law explicitly requires heavily polluting enterprises to publicly disclose detailed environmental information. In 2016, the People’s Bank of China, the Ministry of Finance, and five other ministries jointly issued the “Guiding Opinions on Building a Green Financial System.” This collaborative effort reflects China’s growing commitment to integrating environmental considerations into its broader economic and financial policies. Subsequently, in October 2017, the philosophy of “Lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets,” was included in the report of the 19th National Congress of the Communist Party of China. This inclusion reflects a significant shift in policy focus towards environmental sustainability and the integration of ecological principles into China’s developmental strategy. In 2021, the “Guidelines for Content and Format of Information Disclosure No. 2” first included encouragement for the disclosure of environmental and social responsibility. This shows that in policy-making related to social performance and environmental information disclosure, China adopts a principle of mandatory disclosure for key enterprises and encourages voluntary disclosure for others.

### 2.2. Literature review

Previous studies have predominantly examined the economic consequences of corporate ESG performance. For example, Starks (2009) suggests that a company’s ESG performance can influence corporate risks, including supply chain, product and technology, compliance, and reputational risks. El Ghoul et al. (2011) posits that responsible companies face lower risks and cost of equity capital due to their broader investor base compared to less responsible firms. Cheng et al. (2014) found that companies with better CSR performance face fewer financial constraints due to (1) stakeholder engagement reducing agency costs, and (2) increased corporate transparency decreasing information asymmetry. They also discovered that both environmental and social dimensions of CSR positively contribute to reducing financing constraints. During the financial crisis of 2008–2009, Lins et al. (2017) discovered that firms with higher social capital had higher stock returns, profitability, growth potential, and sales per employee. This indicates that strong relationships between a company and its stakeholders and investors can reveal their value when overall trust in the company and market declines. Hong et al. (2019) found that companies with better social responsibility were granted more lenient court decisions and had higher market valuations post-judgment in the context of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. Albuquerque et al. (2019) argue that companies with strong ESG or CSR performance, due to differentiated product strategies, have less price elasticity of demand.

A subset of research has specifically addressed the impact of corporate ESG actions on financing costs. For example, Chava (2014) demonstrated that companies with environmental issues (such as hazardous chemicals or significant pollutant emissions) incur higher borrowing costs, less institutional investor ownership, and lower bank participation, with investors demanding higher stock returns. Focusing on the impact of corporate environmental performance on financing behavior, El Ghoul et al. (2018) found that companies with better environmental responsibility performance have lower capital costs, a conclusion that holds even after controlling for endogeneity. In terms of corporate social performance, Truong et al. (2021) conducted an empirical study using customer satisfaction scores of U.S. firms from 1995 to 2015, revealing that companies with higher customer satisfaction also have lower equity capital costs, and the impact of customer satisfaction on the cost of equity increases with the degree of information asymmetry.

While existing research is primarily focused on ESG performance, there is a scarcity of literature providing direct evidence on the impact of ESG information disclosure quality on corporate financing costs. In the context of China’s bank-dominated financial system, debt financing has become an extremely important external financing channel for enterprises. Consequently, the cost of debt financing has become a decisive factor for the survival and development of enterprises. This paper, controlling for corporate ESG performance, concentrates on the influence of ESG information disclosure quality on the cost of debt financing. It further analyzes the moderating effects of ownership characteristics, accounting information transparency, and marketization levels, thus expanding and innovating upon the existing literature on ESG information disclosure quality.

### 2.3. Hypothesis development

The significance of reduced debt financing costs is paramount for a company’s viability and growth. Lower costs improve capital efficiency and cash flow for production and investment, while also diminishing the risk of insolvency and bankruptcy, ensuring long-term corporate growth. Companies, therefore, prioritize minimizing debt financing costs. In this context, ESG investment has emerged as a key area, with stakeholders like governments and MSCI advocating for voluntary disclosure by companies. This trend has set new standards in transparency.

Information asymmetry theory suggests that disparities in information among enterprises significantly impact their debt financing costs. When firms disclose high-quality information, they lessen the information gap between banks and themselves, leading to reduced debt financing costs. Importantly, non-financial data, such as ESG performance, holds economic significance, enabling lenders to evaluate a firm’s potential ESG risks and address information asymmetry.

According to economic disclosure theory, comprehensive ESG reporting allows firms to indicate robust ESG performance to banks and other stakeholders. This distinction helps firms present themselves as lower ESG risks, potentially resulting in decreased debt financing costs. Under legitimacy theory, firms use ESG disclosures to cultivate a positive social image and reputation, which helps in acquiring lower-cost financing and mitigating non-compliance litigation risks. Therefore, effective ESG disclosure can lower lending risks for banks and, consequently, firms’ debt financing costs. Accordingly, we develop the following Hypothesis 1:

**Hypothesis 1.** Enhanced ESG disclosure quality significantly reduces a firm’s cost of debt financing.

We further examines the role of property rights heterogeneity in the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. Empirical evidence shows that state-owned enterprises (SOEs) in China, with government backing, gain more trust from banks regarding repayment, leading to better credit efficiency and lower debt financing costs compared to non-SOEs. Moreover, the SOE status often lessens the importance of accounting information in lending decisions, indicating that banks might not heavily weigh ESG information for SOEs. Conversely, for non-SOEs, banks rely more on ESG disclosures to assess risks. This leads to our second hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 2.** The negative impact of ESG disclosure quality on debt financing costs is more significant for non-SOEs than SOEs.

Accounting information transparency is also crucial in influencing investor decisions. Superior accounting information boosts capital allocation efficiency and reduces information asymmetry. Dhaliwal et al. (2012) found that disclosing corporate social responsibility reports improves analysts’ forecast accuracy. They argue that financial and non-financial information provided by companies offer distinct implications and values. Non-financial data, particularly ESG information, offers additional insights, aiding creditors in interpreting a company’s financial health and risks. ESG disclosures are thus vital in providing a comprehensive view of a company’s status, aiding informed decision-making by investors and creditors. Thus, we propose the third hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 3.** Lower accounting information transparency intensifies the negative impact of ESG disclosure quality on debt financing costs.

In China, marketization levels vary across provinces and cities due to differences in resources, geographical locations, and policies. These variations affect enterprises’ debt financing costs. In more marketized areas, banks focus on profitability, adhering to market principles and emphasizing enterprises’ intrinsic factors. Conversely, in less marketized areas with government intervention, banks may provide favorable loan terms to support local goals. Sun et al. (2005) observed that enterprises in highly marketized areas face higher debt compliance costs, while government intervention in less marketized areas reduces these costs. Additionally, in highly marketized areas with intense bank competition, banks are more receptive to ESG investing and seek ESG information for lending decisions. This observation forms the basis of our fourth hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 4.** The negative effect of ESG disclosure quality on debt financing costs is more pronounced in areas with higher marketization levels.

## 3. Data sample and research design

### 3.1. Data and sample

This study focuses on Chinese A-share listed companies, selecting observations from 2011 to 2020 as the sample. In the empirical analysis, the impact of ESG disclosure quality on debt financing costs may be delayed; therefore, the dependent variable is lagged by one period, making the study period 2012–2020. The corresponding explanatory variables, control variables, and moderating variables cover the period from 2011 to 2019. To comprehensively assess a company’s ESG disclosure quality, this paper adopts the ESG disclosure scores from the Bloomberg database, following the approach of Christensen et al. (2022).$^2$ The score is based on 100 out of 219 original data points collected by Bloomberg, encompassing 120 ESG indicators such as carbon emissions, climate change, pollution, renewable energy, political contributions, discrimination, diversity, community relations, and human rights. Scores range from 0.1 to 100 and represent the extent of voluntary or mandatory disclosures a company makes to all stakeholders in the realm of ESG. The more ESG information a company discloses, the higher its ESG disclosure score. Moreover, each data point’s score is weighted according to its relevance to the industry in which a company operates. For example, greenhouse gas emissions are a more significant variable for the materials sector than for other industries, whereas this might not be the case for the real estate sector. Other financial data are sourced from the CSMAR and Wind databases.

The sample is constructed through the following steps: (1) Exclusion of companies in sectors such as insurance and finance, which have accounting systems markedly different from other industries; (2) Exclusion of ST and ST* companies, as these companies are operationally abnormal and their behavior may significantly differ from that of other firms; (3) Removal of companies with evidently erroneous or abnormal data; (4) Exclusion of samples with missing values in key variables; (5) Tailoring of continuous variables. After these filtering processes, a total of 7969 observations are obtained for the sample. The detailed process of sample construction is illustrated in Table 1.

### 3.2. Research design

A multivariate regression equation is employed to test hypotheses and the estimation standard errors are clustered at the firm level. Given the lag effect of the primary variable of interest (ESG disclosure quality) and other corporate characteristics on debt financing costs, we applies a one-period lag to the dependent variable (debt financing costs). Additionally, we controls for year and industry fixed effects. The model is formulated as Model (1).

$$DebtCost_{i,t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESGDS_{i,t} + \sum \beta_k Controls_{i,t} + \sum Firm + \sum Year + \varepsilon_{i,t} \quad (1)$$

Drawing on the methodologies of Wei et al. (2012), Pittman and Fortin (2004), and Minnis (2011), we measure debt financing costs using the ratios of interest expense (Debtcost1) and net financial expense (Debtcost2). The interest expense ratio is defined as the proportion of interest expenses to the company’s total liabilities. Total liabilities equal the sum of current and non-current liabilities, encompassing items such as bonds payable, short-term loans, long-term loans, accounts payable, employee remunerations due, and taxes payable. The net financial expense ratio expands upon Debtcost1 by considering a broader definition of debt financing costs. It is calculated by summing interest expenses, handling fees, and other financial expenses to derive the net financial expense, and then dividing this total by the company’s total liabilities.

As for control variables, following Wei et al. (2012), Zhou et al. (2016), we add various indicators affecting a company’s financial condition, including company size (Size), tangibility of assets (PPE), growth potential (Growth), cash flow adequacy (Flow), performance growth (NEWS), return on assets (ROA), interest coverage ratio (Interest), leverage ratio (Lev), profitability (Loss), and whether the company is audited by one of the top ten accounting firms (Top10). We also collect the corporate social responsibility scores from Hexun, a Chinese financial website, as the proxy variable for a company’s ESG performance. The definitions of variables used in the final empirical study are presented in Appendix. To avoid the influence of extreme values, all continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% and 99% percentiles.

## 4. Empirical results

### 4.1. Baseline regressions

Table 4 presents the regression results examining the impact of ESG disclosure quality on future debt financing costs. Panel A reports the baseline regressions, with ESG ratings data from Hexun. The results for Debtcost1 and Debtcost2 are reported in the columns 1 and 2 of Panel A, respectively. In both columns, the estimated coefficient for ESG disclosure quality is -0.011, significant at the 1% level. This indicates that for each unit increase in ESG disclosure quality (equivalent to the release of one significant industry-specific ESG piece of information), there is a 0.011 percentage point decrease in a company’s debt financing costs in the following year. Economically, this magnitude is also non-trivial. Panel B reports the results using alternative ESG ratings as control variable and show similar results. Specifically, HuaZheng is the ESG score from Shanghai HuaZheng Index Information Services, SytaoGreen is ESG score from SynTao Green Finance. These findings support Hypothesis H1, demonstrating that enhancing the quality of corporate ESG information disclosure could reduce the information acquisition costs for lenders such as banks, thereby lowering the corporate debt financing costs.

### 4.2. Cross-sectional heterogeneity of state ownership

Next, we test Hypothesis 2 on the heterogeneity of state-owned property rights. Table 5 shows the grouped regression results. For non-SOE enterprises, the coefficients of ESG information disclosure quality (ESGDS) are significantly negative at the 1% level in columns 2 and 4. However, for SOE, ESG disclosure quality is not significant. These results validate Hypothesis 2, indicating that state ownership moderates the negative relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. We also compare two groups by adding the interaction term in the full sample, and find similar results. For brevity, the results are available upon request. The rationale behind this phenomenon may be that most commercial banks, influenced by political objectives in their credit resource allocation, lack independence in lending to state-owned enterprises. The implicit government guarantees lead to relaxed credit monitoring for these entities. In such a context, lending decisions by banks and other financial institutions are less influenced by corporate information disclosures, resulting in lower sensitivity to ESG disclosure quality. In contrast, lending to non-state-owned enterprises is driven more by profit motives, demands higher scrutiny of financial performance and information disclosure. Therefore, when these enterprises disclose more ESG-related information, it significantly aids in alleviating information asymmetry for banks and financial institutions, consequently helping to reduce their debt financing costs.

Also, non-SOEs are typically subject to higher scrutiny from investors and regulatory bodies compared to SOEs. Investors often perceive non-SOEs as having greater flexibility and autonomy in their decision-making processes, including those related to ESG practices. Consequently, when non-SOEs demonstrate a higher quality of ESG disclosure, investors may interpret this as a sign of better management transparency, risk management, and corporate governance, leading to increased investor confidence and lower perceived default risk. This, in turn, can result in lower borrowing costs for non-SOEs with higher ESG disclosure quality.

And non-SOEs often compete with other private firms for investor capital in more competitive market environments. In such contexts, firms with superior ESG disclosure quality may gain a competitive advantage in attracting socially responsible investors who prioritize sustainability considerations. These investors may be more willing to lend capital to non-SOEs with strong ESG performance, perceiving them as more sustainable and less risky investments compared to non-SOEs with poor ESG practices.

Overall, the significant negative relationship between ESG disclosure quality and the cost of debt for non-SOEs reflects the importance of transparency, governance, and sustainability practices in shaping investor perceptions and risk assessments within competitive market environments.

### 4.3. Cross-sectional heterogeneity of accounting transparency

In the subsection, we examine the impact of accounting information transparency on the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. Empirically, we use the absolute value of accrual earnings to measure corporate accounting information transparency. The discretionary accrual is estimated using the modified Jones model on an annual basis.

$$\frac{TA_{i,t}}{A_{i,t-1}} = \beta_1 \frac{1}{A_{i,t-1}} + \beta_2 \frac{\Delta Sales_{i,t} - \Delta AR_{i,t}}{A_{i,t-1}} + \beta_3 \frac{PPE_{i,t}}{A_{i,t-1}} + \varepsilon_{i,t} \quad (2)$$

TA represents total accrual profits, which is the difference between net profit and cash flow from operating activities; A stands for total assets at the end of year t - 1; Sale is the company’s main business income; AR refers to accounts receivable; and PPE denotes fixed assets. The residual obtained from the regression represents the manipulable accrual items.

Since we focus on the scale of earnings management rather than its direction, it measures the degree of earnings management by taking the absolute value. A smaller value indicates less earnings manipulation and higher accounting information transparency. We then divide the sample into groups with higher and lower accounting information transparency, based on whether the accrual is below or equal to/above the median, respectively. The cross-sectional analyses is presented in Table 6.

According to Table 6, the coefficient for ESG disclosure quality (ESGDS) is significant at the 1% level in the group with lower accounting transparency. In contrast, in the group with higher accounting transparency, the coefficient for ESGDS is not significant. The striking difference indicates that ESG disclosure quality impacts corporate debt financing costs only when a company’s information transparency is relatively low. These results support the Hypothesis 3, confirming that accounting information transparency indeed plays a moderating role in the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. It also further validates that non-financial information can complement the quality of financial information. We also compare two groups by adding the interaction term in the full sample, and find similar results. For brevity, the results are available upon request.

### 4.4. Cross-sectional heterogeneity of marketization level

In this subsection, we investigate the role of marketization level. For a long time, there have been significant differences in the level of marketization across provinces in China, and the lending behavior of banks and other financial institutions is inevitably influenced by the local level of marketization. Therefore, we also try to examine whether marketization level plays a moderating role in the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. We use the relative index of regional marketization progress from the “China Provincial Marketization Index Report”, which spans up to the year 2018. To more accurately measure the marketization level of each company’s location, we predict the marketization index for the years 2019 and 2020 based on the average growth rate of the theoretical marketization index. Additionally, combining relevant data from various prefecture-level cities and following the index construction method of this index, we further calculate the marketization index for each prefecture-level city. The sample is then divided according to the median of this index into groups with high and low levels of marketization.

Table 7 report the regression results and show that in the higher marketization level group, the coefficient of ESG information disclosure quality (ESGDS) is negative and significant at the 1% level. In contrast, in the lower marketization level group, the coefficient for ESGDS is not significant. This comparison indicates that the higher the marketization level of the area where the listed company is located, the more significant the negative correlation between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs, thereby validating Hypothesis 4.

The likely reason is that in areas with higher marketization levels, government intervention is less, banks have greater autonomy in making credit decisions, and are more sensitive to corporate information disclosure. When companies disclose more ESG information, the risks faced by banks and other lending institutions decrease, and consequently, the debt financing costs that companies need to bear are reduced.

Firms in areas with high marketization levels may have easier access to capital markets, allowing them to tap into a broader pool of lenders and investors. Lenders in these regions were exposed to ESG concepts earlier and are more willing to use them as lending criteria, an show preferences for firms that demonstrate transparency, sustainability, and strong corporate governance practices. Consequently, firms in high marketization areas with better ESG disclosure quality may enjoy lower borrowing costs as lenders perceive them as lower risk and more financially sustainable.

And in highly competitive markets, firms may also leverage their ESG performance as a source of competitive advantage and reputation management. Firms that prioritize ESG disclosure and demonstrate sustainable business practices may enhance their reputation among investors, customers, and other stakeholders. This positive reputation can translate into lower cost of debt for these firms as lenders perceive them as more trustworthy and resilient in the face of environmental and social challenges.

In summary, non-SOE firms typically have more flexibility in their operations and governance structures compared to SOEs, which are subject to more stringent regulations and oversight. Therefore, the significance of the relationship primarily among non-SOE firms implies that investors place greater importance on governance practices and transparency within these companies.

The significance of the relationship among firms with lower accounting transparency supports the transparency interpretation. Lower accounting transparency may raise concerns about the reliability of financial reporting practices, making high-quality ESG disclosure more valuable for mitigating these concerns and reducing borrowing costs.

The presence of a significant relationship in areas with higher marketization levels might suggest that environmental factors play a role. Areas with higher marketization levels may have more sophisticated investors who could value environmental performance, leading to lower borrowing costs for companies with better ESG disclosure in those areas.

In summary, the stronger significance of the relationship among non-SOE firms, less polluting firms, and firms with lower accounting transparency suggests that the concerns over governance (transparency) are likely the primary drivers of the observed relationship between ESG disclosure quality and the cost of debt financing among Chinese listed companies.

## 5. Additional analyses of regulatory policies

China’s ESG regulatory framework is relatively new compared to Europe and America and is mainly led by government departments. In recent years, China has set ambitious targets to peak carbon emissions before 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality by 2060. This involves strict regulations on industries with high emissions and incentives for green technologies. And various government bodies, including the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) and the Ministry of Ecology and Environment, play a vital role in enforcing ESG standards. Policies like the Green Credit Guidelines encourage banks to finance environmentally friendly projects. Chinese companies are increasingly required to disclose ESG-related information. ### 5.2. Mandatory V.S. voluntary ESG disclosures

This subsection examines the impact of mandatory versus voluntary ESG disclosures on debt financing costs. In December 2008, Chinese regulatory bodies mandated certain companies, including those listed on the “Shanghai Stock Exchange Corporate Governance Sector,” foreign-listed companies, financial companies, and those in the “Shenzhen 100 Index,” to disclose CSR reports along with their annual reports. We aim to understand whether the ESG disclosure quality of companies mandated to disclose CSR reports influences bank lending decisions differently compared to other companies. According to disclosure theory, voluntary information disclosure by companies is intended to differentiate them from poorer-performing firms, serving as a signaling mechanism. However, for companies obligated to disclose CSR reports, such information might lose its signaling power to banks, as it’s seen as a compliance effort rather than a genuine reflection of the company’s ESG performance.

We thus divide the sample into sub-samples of companies mandated and voluntarily to disclose CSR reports, conducting regression analyses for each. As indicated in Table 9, banks and financial institutions are more likely to view ESG information from voluntary companies as a positive performance signal. This could be due to the following reasons. For firms not mandated to disclose ESG information, if some of them still voluntarily disclose ESG information of high quality, this could be likely perceived as more transparent and proactive in managing environmental, social, and governance risks. As a result, creditors may perceive these firms as lower risk, leading to lower cost of debt. Conversely, other firms do not disclosre ESG information or only disclosure low-quality ESG information may be viewed as less transparent and potentially carrying higher risk, resulting in a higher cost of debt. And Companies that voluntarily disclose ESG information may do so as part of a broader strategy to differentiate themselves in the market. By demonstrating a commitment to sustainability and responsible business practices, these firms may attract socially responsible investors who prioritize ESG factors. This increased investor interest can contribute to lower borrowing costs for these firms.

High-quality ESG disclosure can signal to creditors that a firm is focused on long-term sustainability and resilience. Creditors may view such firms as better positioned to manage future risks and disruptions, reducing the perceived riskiness of lending to them and thereby lowering the cost of debt. Conversely, firms with poor or no ESG disclosure may be perceived as less prepared for future challenges, leading creditors to demand higher returns to compensate for the perceived risk.

### 6.3. Instrumental variable

To address potential endogeneity in the study, we also employ an instrumental variable approach to explore the causal relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. Following the methodology of Wang and Xie (2022), we selects Confucian culture (Confu) as an instrumental variable and uses a 2SLS method for estimating the primary regression model. Wang and Xie (2022) argue that ESG disclosure behavior is a manifestation of corporate social responsibility, reflecting a business philosophy that aligns with traditional Confucian thought, which emphasizes social concern and not blindly pursuing economic benefits. This philosophy resonates with Confucian teachings like “Wealth and rank obtained through immoral means are to me like floating clouds” (Confucius, Analects).

Building on the works of Wang and Xie (2022), we measure the influence of Confucian culture based on the density of imperial scholars (jinshi) from the Ming and Qing dynasties in the vicinity of a company’s registered location.$^4$ The more imperial scholars a region had, the stronger its Confucian educational atmosphere. As the Ming and Qing dynasties each lasted only about a century, while Chinese listed companies have only existed for over thirty years, using this instrumental variable can significantly alleviate the issue of reverse causality. Specifically, we use data from the China Biographical Database to obtain the geographical coordinates of the hometowns of Ming and Qing dynasty scholars. It then calculates the number of scholars within a 100-km radius of a company’s registered address, adds one to this number, and takes the logarithm to create the Confucian culture (Confu) index. A higher value of this index indicates a stronger Confucian cultural atmosphere. Consequently, companies in these regions are likely to possess a stronger sense of morality and mission, making them more prone to disclose high-quality ESG information.

Second, we also consider whether A-share listed companies are also listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange as an instrumental variable. The Hong Kong Stock Exchange requires listed companies to issue ESG reports, which generally improves the ESG disclosure quality of A-share companies listed in Hong Kong. However, it is challenging to determine if A-share companies are listed in H shares specifically to enhance ESG disclosure quality. We only retained companies that were listed on the A-share market first and then on the H-share market, excluding those listed first on the H-share market and then on the A-share market, to ensure the correct temporal sequence for improving ESG disclosure quality. Panel B of Table 12 shows these results. We set a dummy variable, H-listing, which equals 1 if A-share companies issue H shares, and 0 for these companies before issuing H shares or for companies that have never issued H shares. Based on this, we used a two-stage regression model. The first column’s regression reveals that A-share companies going public in H shares significantly improve the ESG disclosure quality of enterprises, as expected. The second and third columns of the second-stage regressions use two proxies for debt cost, and the results show that the IV is negatively significant, supporting our hypothesis.

Additionally, we used whether A-share companies have been included in the MSCI index as a second instrumental variable. MSCI constructs China A-share market indices but only includes a number of companies with strong performance and industry representation. We hypothesize that MSCI’s ESG rating generally encourages these selected companies to make higher-quality ESG disclosures. However, MSCI does not decide whether to include a company in an index based on its ESG disclosures, so the reverse causality problem is not significant. Empirically, we set the instrumental variable, MSCI. This variable equals 1 if a company is included in the MSCI index, and 0 for the period before inclusion or for companies that have never been included. Specific regression results are shown in Panel C of Table 12, where we find that companies included in MSCI significantly reduce their debt costs. This test also supports our main results. Therefore, these findings based on two alternative IVs bolster our main conclusion that ESG disclosure quality affects debt costs.

## 7. Conclusion

In this study, we utilize the ESG disclosure scores from the Bloomberg database, conducts a comprehensive and detailed exploration of the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. It expands the breadth of research on the relationship between non-financial information and debt financing costs.

It also examines the moderating roles of property rights, accounting information transparency, and marketization levels in this relationship. State-owned enterprises, with implicit government guarantees, are less sensitive to the quality of ESG information compared to non-state-owned enterprises, as banks and other credit institutions place more emphasis on ESG information quality in non-state-owned firms. The transparency of accounting information also plays a moderating role. Non-financial information represented by ESG can complement accounting information, and its value is more pronounced when accounting transparency is low. This reflects the dominance of financial information over non-financial information in decisions made by banks and other credit institutions when accounting information is highly transparent. Marketization level further moderates the relationship between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs. In regions with higher marketization, banks and other lending institutions behave more self-interestedly and are more cautious in their lending decisions. We also find that a significant correlation between ESG disclosure quality and debt financing costs is only observed in sample excluding heavily polluting industries and companies voluntarily disclose social responsibility reports.

To sum up, the article enriches research in the field of non-financial information disclosure and its impact on debt financing costs, clarifying the specific mechanisms through which ESG disclosure quality affects debt financing costs. It also presents recommendations regarding corporate ESG disclosure practices, lending decisions of banks and other credit institutions, and the direction of policy-making for ESG information disclosure. This work not only contributes to academic understanding but also has practical implications for businesses and policymakers.
