### ABSTRACT

This study investigates how Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) performance is linked to the corporate default risk. Using listed Indian firms from 2016 to 2024, this study finds that ESG performance and its individual dimensions are negatively related to default risk via improved Altman Z-score. The advanced serial mediation analysis using the PROCESS model 6 suggests that ESG information is transmitted to lower default risk via enhanced corporate efficiency (CEF), improved financial performance indicated by return on assets (ROA), and lowered the cost of borrowing indicated by the cost of debt (COD). Applying the Propensity Score Matching (PSM) and Difference-in-Difference (DID) technique, this study also documents that firms that are part of the NIFTY100ESG index have significantly better financial resilience than companies not part of the index after the COVID-19 crisis. Through ex-post analysis in the case of defaulted firms, this study finds that ESG performance can predict corporate default events if combined with other parameters effectively. The findings supported by the legitimacy theory indicate that firms can increase corporate sustainability and legitimacy not only through improvements in corporate efficiency and profitability but also through external perceptions, responsibility, and ethical practices, which function as an ‘insurance effect’. Thus, policymakers should encourage lenders to integrate ESG factors into business models and promote sustainable business practices to reduce financial risks. This will accelerate the firms’ adoption of cleaner technologies, enhance governance standards, and strengthen overall financial stability and corporate sustainability.

## 1. Introduction

The Going Concern Concept (GCC), one of the fundamental accounting concepts, argues that the business should run indefinitely without defaulting, bankrupting, halting operations, or liquidating assets (Fremgen, 1968). A corporate default or bankruptcy is a situation where a firm finds it difficult to meet its financial obligations arising from any contract. A corporate default not only affects the stakeholders of that particular corporation but also creates a contagious domino effect on other firms that are linked with it, like in the case of the Lehman Brothers default during the Global Financial Crisis of 2008 (Peckham, 2013). The GCC is supported by the legitimacy theory, which asserts that business must align their operations with societal expectations to maintain their license to operate and, thus, should focus on corporate sustainability (Dowling & Pfeffer, 1975). Corporate sustainability is particularly relevant in developing countries like India, where investors’ confidence in corporations has deteriorated due to environmental degradation, social crises, and corporate governance scandals (Siddiqui, 2009). There are numerous instances in India where corporations that have ignored the business responsibility, legitimacy, and sustainability component have defaulted and gone bankrupt. Recent high-profile defaults, including IL&FS in 2018, DHFL in 2019, and Yes Bank in 2020, show the inadequacies of traditional financial metrics in foreshadowing financial distress. These failures frequently arise from both financial mismanagement and broader ESG-related issues. Therefore, the discussion on corporate responsibility has grown in recent years, moving beyond moral behavior or image issues to become an actual component affecting corporate sustainability. In an era of severe environmental crises, social issues, and governance challenges, the growing demand for ESG disclosure from the stakeholders has resulted in a fundamental shift in transforming corporate sustainability (Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, & Kumar, 2024).

A corporation’s investment in ESG practices affects its profitability and cash flows, ultimately affecting default risk (Cheng & Micale, 2022; Wang & Yang, 2023). Various perspectives exist regarding the relationship between business performance in ESG and default risk. One perspective suggests that adopting ESG practices increases the likelihood of corporate default (Nasrin & Sreejith, 2023). They contend that investing in ESG initiatives leads to greenwashing, is superfluous, and distracts the corporation’s core attention from conducting business. It raises operational expenses, disclosure costs, and narrows the cash flow, resulting in an increased risk of corporate default. The probability of default can also arise in excess use of fixed-income securities with a high cost of funds, which puts a firm in a challenging financial distress situation where it finds it difficult to meet its financial obligations (Vivo et al., 2025). The likelihood of default is further contingent upon the business cycle, crisis periods, and corporate lifecycle (Atif & Ali, 2021; Vivel-Búa et al., 2023; Wang & Yang, 2023).

Conversely, other perspectives identify a negative correlation between ESG and default risk. For example, Brogi et al. (2022) claim in their study that corporations with better ESG scores have stronger credit profiles, indicating that a significant commitment to sustainability can change how lenders perceive a borrower’s risk. Some studies suggest that a positive ESG profile opens a possibility for better financing arrangements, forward-thinking risk management solutions, and capabilities that strengthen a company’s competitive position (Giese et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2024). According to Atif and Ali (2021), ESG disclosure helps companies avoid default by reducing financial distress, regulatory fines, and reputational damage. Concerning financial performance, most ESG studies discovered a positive relationship with profitability metrics such as ROA and ROE. These studies view ESG as a means to improve corporate efficiency, market goodwill, and profit margins by achieving cost reductions and increased sales (Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, & Kumar, 2024; Mushafiq et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2018). Regarding the cost of business finance, Raimo et al. (2021) found that superior ESG performance can result in lower financing costs as investors prefer companies that demonstrate sustainable practices and responsible governance. This is because of the lower involvement of risk components in their business practices. Consequently, ESG-focused businesses have better financing and borrowing power than non-ESG firms (You et al., 2025).

Previous research on ESG practices has largely concentrated on their direct financial implications, such as stock performance, cost estimation, and corporate valuation, with fewer studies examining the impact on default risk (Aouadi & Marsat, 2018; Biju et al., 2025; Wong et al., 2021). Of those studies, the focus is primarily on developed countries, leaving a gap in the understanding of developing economies like India (Korzeb et al., 2024; Palmieri, Ferilli, Altunbas, et al., 2023). Over the last decade, the importance of these developing countries and their contribution to the global economy have increased significantly (Dobers & Halme, 2009). These economies have many multinational corporations that are systematically so important and globally integrated that the failure of one can have a chain reaction and domino effect, resulting in a ‘too big to fail’ situation. Therefore, studying the ESG-default risk link from a developing country’s perspective, like India, is crucial.

Studying India offers a compelling and unique institutional context for exploring the relationship between ESG performance and corporate default risk. First, Indian corporations possess distinct structural features that differentiate them from firms in other countries. Indian firms exhibit concentrated family ownership, high promoter holdings, and a prevalence of promoter-CEOs, which may affect ESG implementation and financial outcomes (Kansil, 2021). These ownership patterns can cause conflicts of interest and governance, making ESG more crucial for reputation and a legitimacy-enhancing tool. In developed economies, dispersed ownership and stronger regulatory oversight partially mitigate these risks, thus altering the role and impact of ESG. Second, unlike the Western developed economies, where ESG regulations and bankruptcy codes are more mature and standardized, India is still evolving in both dimensions. India’s ESG regulatory framework has undergone substantial changes in recent years (Panda, 2023). The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) has introduced the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) framework, thereby mandating ESG disclosures for the top 1000 listed entities by market capitalization (Panda, 2023). This contrasts with voluntary or industry-specific disclosures, which are still prevalent in many developing Asian economies. Compared to the European Union’s rigorous ESG taxonomy or the UK’s Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) alignment, India’s ESG disclosure regime is relatively recent and still maturing, providing a novel setting for empirical exploration. Third, India’s 2016 Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) introduced a creditor-driven resolution process, accelerating timelines and improving recovery rates. This contrasts with jurisdictions like the US, where Chapter 11 promotes debtor-led restructuring, or Australia and many EU countries that emphasize pre-insolvency mechanisms. India’s bankruptcy framework, with its emphasis on time-bound resolution, alters firms’ incentives toward risk and sustainability, making the role of ESG especially salient. Besides, Indian capital markets are characterized by asymmetric information, a growing ESG investing culture, and varying levels of stakeholder activism. These features make ESG performance a more informative signal to investors and creditors in India than in more transparent markets. Thus, India presents a rich setting to assess whether ESG performance can be an early warning mechanism for corporate distress. The findings from this context expand the global understanding of ESG-default dynamics in emerging economies and offer policy insights for other developing nations undergoing similar regulatory transitions and changes.

So far, many studies have investigated the direct link between ESG performance and default risk; however, there is still a notable absence of research exploring the mediating role and indirect effect. The academic literature is silent on the mechanism, path, and channel of how ESG reduces default risk. Specifically, this study addresses the gap by investigating the mediating roles of corporate efficiency (CEF), financial performance (measured by ROA), and the cost of debt (COD) in the relationship between ESG integration and default risk. While it is hypothesized that ESG enhances CEF, which improves ROA and, in turn, reduces COD, the extent to which these factors explain the link between ESG and default risk remains underexplored. Apart from overall ESG, understanding how the individual ESG dimensions and different sectoral variations of the economy impact default risk is still missing in the current literature. Further, previous research has shown that sustainable indices in India outperformed traditional indices in terms of risk, risk-adjusted returns, and volatility during both pre-COVID-19 and post-COVID-19 periods (Mahanta, Sahu, & Behera, 2024; Tripathi & Kaur, 2021). These studies are carried out to measure the investment performance, specifically from the investors’ perspective (Jasuja et al., 2021; Seth & Singh, 2022). However, from the firms’ perspective, whether companies’ inclusion in these sustainable indices, like India’s NIFTY100ESG index, benefits corporate sustainability is completely absent. This is also an evolving study area regarding how the ESG & default risk relationship dynamic reacts to overall market risk and varies in situations like the COVID-19 crisis.

Furthermore, the Indian market differs from developed economies in terms of ownership structure, institutional settings, and regulatory practices. As a result, the implications from those of developed markets cannot be directly applied. Thus, a comprehensive heterogeneity analysis is needed in India, considering distinct corporate characteristics such as promoter holding, institutional holding, affiliation to business groups, and promoter CEOs. Finally, to our knowledge, no prior studies have conducted an ex-post analysis to examine whether ESG can predict a corporate default event, and this study attempts to address this gap. These identified limitations in the prior research indicate a more thorough investigation of the ESG-default risk relationship across overall ESG, multiple ESG dimensions, and different market scenarios.

There are numerous theories, like stakeholder theory, agency theory, resource-based view, legitimacy theory, etc., to explain this link and behavior. These theories have integrated corporate sustainability into their core principles by offering a unique perspective on ESG’s role in mitigating corporate default risk. First, stakeholder theory emphasizes that companies must effectively engage and manage stakeholder relations to maintain corporate reputation and financial stability (Freeman, 1984). Stakeholders demand legitimacy in ESG policies and promote transparency and ethical decision-making, which reduces financial and reputational risks. Agency theory highlights conflicts between management (agents) and shareholders (principals), where ESG initiatives serve as governance mechanisms to align interests, reduce information asymmetry, and enhance long-term stability (Jensen & Meckling, 1979). Furthermore, the resource-based view (RBV) positions ESG as a strategic asset in which sustainable practices create long-term value and resilience, strengthening a firm’s competitive advantage (Lin & Wu, 2013). While these theories highlight the relevance of ESG, the legitimacy theory provides a comprehensive framework by emphasizing the need for firms to comply with society norms and expectations to sustain their market position. In contrast to agency and RBV, which focus on internal governance and resource efficiency, legitimacy theory emphasizes the importance of maintaining social trust and regulatory compliance, lowering reputational risks, and guaranteeing long-term operational sustainability. Given the growing regulatory pressure and stakeholder audits on ESG, legitimacy theory appears to be a better approach to addressing corporate sustainability.

This study builds on legitimacy theory to provide new understandings of the relationship between ESG performance and default risk. It fills the research gap by exploring the underlying link using advanced econometric tools, such as the panel quantile model, two-step system GMM, panel IV 2SLS regression, PSM technique, DID technique, and KRLS model. Furthermore, whereas previous research has mainly ignored the mechanism underlying this relationship, this study takes a novel approach, employing Hayes’ (2017) serial mediation methodology to analyze the roles of ROA, COD, and corporate efficiency (CEF) as mediating variables. This study goes beyond the base analysis by analyzing the impacts of individual ESG dimensions, ESG index inclusion, the role of the COVID-19 crisis, sectoral impact, and ownership heterogeneity on the probability of default. Also, an additional purpose is to conduct an ex-post analysis in case of defaulted firms in order to draw inference by demonstrating how poor ESG performance is retrospectively associated with actual default events. Collectively, these analyses broaden the study’s scope and contextual depth, providing theoretical and practical implications in emerging markets like India.

The present study makes several unique contributions. First, this study adds to the current literature by examining the relationship between ESG and default risk from the perspective of a developing country, specifically India, which remains an under-researched area in this context. Second, unlike previous models that mostly explain the relationship in a one-dimensional manner, this study explains two channels regarding how ESG performance lowers the probability of default through mediators like corporate efficiency (CEF), ROA, and COD. Such an approach narrates the dynamic nature of how ESG gives financial benefits, as ESG not only mitigates risk but also positively links using financial channels to enhance corporate sustainability. Using an advanced serial mediation paradigm adds a methodological contribution to this study and informs empirical insights of how ESG influences financial risk variably through interrelated financial indicators. Third, this research adds to the expanding body of literature by assessing the causal effects of ESG performance on firms’ financial stability through applying Propensity Score Matching (PSM). This study estimates the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATET) to evaluate the impact of a firm’s inclusion in an ESG index on the Altman Z-score. Fourth, the ex-post analysis using PSM shows that defaulted firms have significantly lower ESG scores compared to matched non-defaulted firms with similar financial and firm characteristics, thus indicating that ESG performance can effectively predict actual default events. Fifth, this research employs a range of proxies of dependent and independent variables, and advanced econometric models, including panel quantile regression, two-step system GMM, serial mediation analysis, PSM & DID technique, and DKSE estimators, to ensure a robust and credible analysis. This research extends the scope of econometric techniques by incorporating Kernel-based Regularized Least Squares (KRLS) estimators, a machine learning algorithm, to evaluate consistency. Blending econometric methods with machine learning techniques enhances this research’s methodological rigor and approach. Finally, the study also goes further to incorporate stakeholder theory and legitimacy theory by arguing that firms that are ESG credible will acquire legitimacy, which boosts investor confidence, enhances creditworthiness, and promotes financial stability.

## 2. Review of literature and hypothesis formation

Corporate default imposes significant economic and social costs on a wide range of stakeholders, including shareholders, creditors, customers, and employees (Sun & Cui, 2013). Several financial parameters, such as increased credit risk, financial distress, high debt costs, and poor financial performance, are primary drivers that can cause a company to default. Corporate governance failures, such as scams and scandals, also contribute to corporate default. These days, ESG parameters are increasingly used to assess a company’s risk profile. Therefore, along with financial parameters, investors are screening companies based on non-financial parameters such as ESG factors (Mahanta, Sahu, & Behera, 2024). According to studies, integrating ESG practices into business operations improves financial performance, creates long-term value, and thus has a positive impact on society (Stobierski, 2022). Effective ESG strategies can help improve a company’s reputation, operational efficiency, and stakeholder relationships.

Despite extensive research into ESG and firm financial performance, literature on ESG and default risk remains scarce. There is an ongoing debate among researchers about whether ESG reduces corporate default risk. The available studies have produced mixed results and varied effects. Some research has found a positive correlation, implying that ESG investments may increase operational costs and reduce financial flexibility, increasing default risk. For instance, Nasrin and Sreejith (2023) report a positive relationship between ESG performance and default risk among Indian firms, potentially due to greenwashing. It raises operational expenses, disclosure costs, and narrows the cash use, resulting in corporate default (Buallay, 2019). While other research has found a negative relationship between ESG performance and default risk, implying that companies with stronger ESG practices are less likely to default. For instance, Maquieira et al. (2023) investigated the top 500 global family firms from 2015 to 2021 and discovered a positive relationship between ESG and Altman Z-score. Similarly, DO and Vo (2023) found that mandatory ESG guidelines reduced the likelihood of default in a sample of 17 emerging economies. According to research, strong ESG strategies protect reputational risk, stabilize financial performance, and reduce the possibility of corporate insolvency (Chasiotis et al., 2023). Truong et al. (2025) investigate the impact of ESG ratings on financial distress in US firms between 2005 and 2020. Their findings show that firms with higher ESG ratings experience less financial distress. Many other studies have found that high ESG ratings correspond to low credit risk and a lower likelihood of default, as evidenced by measures such as the Altman Z-score, Merton Distance to Default, and credit ratings (Cohen, 2022; Li & Hu, 2024; Palmieri, Ferilli, Stefanelli, et al., 2023). Palmieri, Ferilli, Stefanelli, et al. (2023) studied 211 European listed firms from 2013 to 2022 and discovered that improving the environmental dimension reduces the risk of default. On the other hand, high environmental and social risks can undermine financial stability (Kabir et al., 2021; Korzeb et al., 2024). Based on this, we formulate our hypotheses as follows:

**H1.** Overall ESG performance has a negative influence on corporate default risk.

The impact of ESG on default risk may vary depending on mediators, moderators, or factors such as financial performance, firm size, industry, and economic conditions (Li et al., 2022; Vivel-Búa et al., 2023). For instance, Mushafiq et al. (2023) show that CSR has an indirect impact on forward default risk through goodwill. Atif and Ali (2021) demonstrate that ESG reduces default risk by increasing ROA, decreasing performance variability, and lowering debt costs. Furthermore, Wang and Yang (2023) emphasize the importance of ESG in reducing cashflow uncertainty and profit volatility to mitigate credit risk. Adequate ESG disclosure positively affects ROA and ROE, demonstrating that transparency can improve long-term results (Van Hoang et al., 2023). Furthermore, Xie et al. (2018) have highlighted the importance of corporate efficiency as a proxy for internal firm performance. They demonstrate how ESG performance improves corporate efficiency, and governance disclosure has had the most significant positive impact on efficiency (Xie et al., 2018). Furthermore, Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, and Kumar (2024) demonstrate that corporate efficiency plays a positive mediating role between ESG and improved financial performance. Operational efficiencies achieved by ESG methods, such as energy saving, waste reduction, and supply chain improvement, also increase firms’ profitability and stabilize their cash flow, thereby mitigating overall financial risks (Gao et al., 2024). Firms can increase profitability and effectiveness by increasing brand awareness, reducing capital expenditures, and improving operational efficiency (Hasan et al., 2016). In line with the results, studies reveal that ESG scores significantly improve profitability measured in corporate efficiency terms and correlate with stock prices and lower financing costs (Serafeim & Yoon, 2022a; Wang et al., 2025). Based on this, we formulate our hypotheses as follows:

**H2.** Corporate efficiency (CFE) and Return on Assets (ROA) serially mediate the ESG and default risk relationship.

Prior research on ESG and cost of business finance studies demonstrates how ESG integration contributes to substantially decreasing a firm’s cost of capital by alleviating ESG-related controversies (Hampl & Linnertová, 2024). Investors perceive the company with better ESG performance to be less risky and, as a result, require a lower return on equity (ROE), consequently imposing a lower cost of capital (Hampl & Linnertová, 2024). Besides, firms rated high in ESG by external agencies enjoy better credit ratings from these agencies, thereby reducing their cost of debt by qualifying for more favorable lending terms and lower interest rates (Shin et al., 2024). This effect is further augmented by growing demand for ESG investments since institutions and socially responsible funds are actively investing in firms that work for sustainability, thereby boosting valuations and reducing costs of equity capital (Chang et al., 2022). Also, regulatory incentives such as tax exemptions and the offering of green bonds or sustainability-linked loans are a bonus for firms that meet ESG standards (Debrah et al., 2021). Integrating ESG valuation in credit audits often improves risk evaluations, reduces loan costs, and lowers default risks (Asante-Appiah & Lambert, 2022). High ESG ratings can lead firms to move from public debt to private credit, reducing financial burdens and default risk (Feng & Wu, 2021). Together, all these mechanisms indicate the strong potential of ESG integration to shape the financing structures of firms for better long-term viability and reduced cost of capital. Based on the above discussions, we formulate the following hypothesis:

**H3.** Return on Assets (ROA) and Cost of Debt (COD) serially mediate the ESG and default risk relationship.

Studies conducted in various economic conditions quantify the impact of ESG on default risk; for example, in stable economic contexts, ESG practices may increase firm value and stock valuations (Nguyen & Nguyen, 2024). Vivel-Búa et al. (2023) studied 990 companies in the Eurozone during three phases of the 2008 global financial crisis. Their findings show that ESG scores are significantly related to Z-scores during the pre-crisis period, while environmental scores are related to Z-scores during crisis periods, with no relationship after the crisis. They also show that ESG has a net positive impact on default risk for small and medium-sized firms while having the opposite effect on large Eurozone firms. In another study, Gianfrate et al. (2024) found that firms with ES ratings, particularly in North America, demonstrated significant financial resilience during the COVID-19 crisis. In an index-level study conducted in India, Mahanta, Sahu, and Behera (2024) discovered that sustainable indices such as the BSE100ESG index outperformed traditional indices in terms of risk, return, and volatility during the COVID-19 crisis. Similarly, Debnath and Dinda (2023) discovered that the Indian ESG index outperformed the benchmark, implying higher returns on sustainability-focused investments. Furthermore, green investors’ wealth increased significantly both during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, with higher returns on green indexes following the pandemic. This shows that firms in the ESG index exhibit lower volatility and better stability during crisis periods. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

**H4.** Companies part of the ESG index are less prone to default risk than companies not part of the index.

**H5.** Companies part of the ESG index are less prone to default risk than companies not part of the index after COVID-19.

The ESG’s impact on default risk varies depending on the institutional setting. For example, Truong et al. (2025) demonstrate that in the face of fierce competition, dedicated institutional investors and small businesses use ESG to mitigate financial distress. According to Vivel-Búa et al. (2023), ESG positively impacts default risk for small and medium-sized firms but negatively impacts large Eurozone firms. Shang et al. (2024) argued that in China, the mitigating effect of ESG on debt risk is stronger in state-owned enterprises, large corporations, and corporations with strong internal controls, particularly under low marketization and adverse macroeconomic conditions. Regarding ownership, Maquieira et al. (2023) found an inverted U-shaped relationship between ownership concentration and a negative relationship between family participation on the board of ESG and default risk.

Despite increasing scholarly interest in the relationship between ESG performance and corporate default risk, the existing literature is primarily focused on developed economies such as the United States, the European Union, and the United Kingdom. There is still a notable lack of empirical evidence from emerging markets, such as India, where institutional frameworks, regulatory settings, and ESG maturity differ significantly from those in advanced economies. Second, previous research has primarily focused on direct effects, with little investigation into the underlying mechanisms or transmission channels by which ESG influences default risk. Specifically, there is a lack of research on the serial mediating roles of corporate efficiency (CEF), financial performance (e.g., ROA), and cost of debt (COD)—factors that are critical in translating ESG efforts into risk-reduction outcomes. The use of corporate efficiency as a latent internal performance construct has not been tested in the ESG-default literature. Integrating these mediators can provide a better understanding of the ESG-default nexus by revealing the structural pathways involved. Third, while some studies have looked into the role of ESG performance on default risk during the 2008 global financial crisis, there is still a significant gap in understanding firm-level ESG effects during the more recent COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in the Indian context. The economic and operational disruptions caused by COVID-19 provide a unique opportunity to test the resilience-promoting role of ESG factors in real-time crises. Another significant gap is the underuse of ESG indices, such as the NIFTY100ESG index, in default risk modelling. Prior research has rarely considered how inclusion in such indices can serve as a proxy for strong ESG performance, reducing insolvency risk, particularly during volatile periods.

## 3. Data and methods

The present study examines the relationship between ESG performance and corporate default risk in Indian stock markets. Further, the mediating role of three variables, viz., ROA, COD, and CEF, in the relationship between ESG and default risk is also assessed. This study goes beyond the base analysis by analyzing the impacts of individual ESG dimensions, ESG index inclusion, the role of the COVID-19 crisis, sectoral impact, and ownership heterogeneity on the probability of default. Also, an additional purpose is to conduct an ex-post analysis in case of defaulted firms in order to draw inference by demonstrating how poor ESG performance is retrospectively associated with actual default events. Collectively, these analyses broaden the study’s scope and contextual depth, providing theoretical and practical implications in emerging markets like India.

### 3.1. Variables and data

The dependent variable in this study is the corporate default risk. The Altman Z-score, introduced by Professor Edward Altman in 1968, is widely regarded as a key metric relied on by various entities, including researchers, credit firms, banks, and financial institutions, to assess corporate financial distress and default risk (Altman et al., 2016; Balboula & Shemes, 2025; Sareen & Sharma, 2021). Subsequent to the accurate forecasting of the 2008 financial crisis, the Z-score has been widely employed in contemporary research to explain the likelihood of default (Almamy et al., 2015; Boďa & Úradníček, 2016; Li & Hu, 2024; Postiglione et al., 2025). The Z-score fundamentally integrates various balance sheet metrics into a singular metric, including liquidity, retained earnings, operating returns, and the ratio of market equity to total liabilities, to comprehensively assess a firm’s financial stability. A lower Z-score generally suggests an increased likelihood of financial distress, while a higher Z-score reflects enhanced stability and a diminished risk of default. This research uses the Z-score as a dependent variable within multiple econometric models to analyze the impact of ESG performance on the financial stability of individual companies. Further, this study also uses the Distance to Default, a forward-looking market-based measure, as a proxy for default risk to check the robustness of the results.

The independent variable is the ESG insight scores obtained from FactSet’s Truvalue Lab database, an increasingly recognized database in both academic and professional spheres for ESG measurement. This AI-driven database has gained wide acceptance for its ability to extract real-time, stakeholder-based ESG signals using machine learning and natural language processing techniques. In recent literature, this FactSet ESG database has been used in various domains worldwide on ESG and corporate finance research, including business diversification and investment performance (Isogai et al., 2025), spillover impacts across the value chain (Tran & Coqueret, 2023), market reactions to ESG news (Serafeim & Yoon, 2022b), and retail investor responses (Sloan, 2024), and mapping commercial ties (Li, Lou and Zhang, 2024). Despite its growing adoption globally, this dataset remains underutilized in the Indian context. Thus, this study addresses this gap by employing Truvalue Lab’s ESG scores to test their applicability in emerging markets like India and assess the consistency of ESG-default risk findings in a novel institutional setting. Given a data-driven and forward-looking approach, FactSet makes it particularly suitable for emerging markets, where ESG disclosure practices are uneven and in a developmental stage.

FactSet provides ESG data, unique from many other providers, by providing comprehensive coverage for both public and private markets. Truvalue Labs represents a pioneering effort by applying artificial intelligence (AI), encompassing machine learning and natural language processing, to supply data related to ESG factors (Arthur et al., 2025). This system extracts semantic big data from over 100,000 sources, which includes the perspectives of analysts, advocacy organizations, and government regulators, as reported by independent media in 13 different languages (Truvalue Labs, 2025). The data have been categorized according to various frameworks established by the Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB). Subsequently, the data undergo normalization to produce ESG scores ranging from 0 to 100. The integration of FactSet’s entity-based security master through Data Management Solutions (DMS) significantly enhanced the ESG scoring capabilities, including the hedge funds.

Further, it uses ROA, COD, and CEF as mediating variables for serial mediation analysis. Following the studies of Atif and Ali (2021) and Arango and Gaitan (2021), board independence (BI), current ratio (CR), interest coverage ratio (ICR), debt-to-equity ratio (DE), and the log of total assets (TA) are used as covariates to control their effect. The company-related financial data from 2016 to 2024 are extracted from the Refinitiv database for Indian companies. ### 3.2. Methodology

#### 3.2.1. Measuring the Altman Z-score
This paper estimates the probability of default using the Z-score proposed by Altman (1968). A score of 1.8 signifies a significant likelihood of bankruptcy, whereas scores above 3 indicate a low likelihood of bankruptcy. The Altman Z-score is computed using the formula in Eq. (1):

$$Zscore = 1.2 \times \frac{\text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}} + 1.4 \times \frac{\text{Retained Earnings}}{\text{Total Assets}} + 3.3 \times \frac{\text{Earnings Before Interest and Taxes}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$
$$+ 0.6 \times \frac{\text{Market Value of Equity}}{\text{Total Liabilities}} + 1.0 \times \frac{\text{Total Revenue}}{\text{Total Assets}} \quad (1)$$

#### 3.2.2. Conceptual model and baseline framework
After calculating the Z-score, based on the above objective, this study frames the baseline functional model in Eq. (2) and the econometric model in Eq. (3). The models are given as follows:

$$\text{Corporate Default Risk} = f(\text{ESG, BI, CR, ICR, DE, FS}) \quad (2)$$

$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{10} + \beta_{11}ESG_{it} + \beta_{12}BI_{it} + \beta_{13}CR_{it} + \beta_{14}ICR_{it} + \beta_{15}DE_{it} + \beta_{16}FS_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (3)$$

#### 3.2.3. Measuring corporate efficiency
Recently, the concept of corporate efficiency (CEF) has been extensively studied in the ESG literature (Arsenopoulou et al., 2025; Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, & Kumar, 2024; Moskovics et al., 2023; Veltri et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2018). Particularly, CEF as a mediator in the relationship between ESG and corporate financial performance has been first identified in the study of Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, and Kumar (2024). In their framework, corporate efficiency represented internal firm performance, while profitability captured external performance. Their findings show that corporate efficiency drives profitability and mediates the ESG factors and corporate financial relationships. However, the possible mediating role of corporate efficiency in the ESG and corporate default relationship is still unexplored. This gap in the literature suggests a need for further investigation. Building on Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, and Kumar (2024), the present study examines whether corporate efficiency serves as an intermediary mechanism through which ESG influences default risk.

This study applies the Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) to measure corporate efficiency. DEA is a popular non-parametric method used for efficiency analysis, which can account for multiple inputs and outputs without assuming any functional relationship between them. Following Mahanta, Sahu, Behera, and Kumar (2024) and Xie et al. (2018), the DEA model is constructed using the number of employees, fixed assets, and cost of revenue as inputs, while total revenue is the output. Then, an output-oriented DEA model with variable returns to scale is applied to estimate the corporate efficiency. The Eq. (4) for the output-oriented DEA model with variable returns to scale with $a$ inputs and $b$ for $n$ DMUs outputs is as follows:

$$\max_{\phi, \delta} \phi \quad (4)$$

Subject to:
$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} \delta_j X_{ij} \le X_{i0} \forall i = 1, \dots, a$$
$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} \delta_j Y_{rj} \ge \phi Y_{r0} \forall r = 1, \dots, b$$
$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} \delta_j = 1$$
$$\delta_j \ge 0 \forall j = 1, \dots, n$$

The output-oriented DEA seeks to maximize the output level for a given constant level of inputs, thus assessing how efficiently the DMUs transform the input resources to output. The DEA technique solves the above linear programming problem to identify the optimal efficiency scores subject to the constraints. The efficiency scores are calculated as the ratio of the weighted sum of outputs to the weighted sum of inputs for each variable. These efficiency scores range from 0 to 1, where 1 means fully efficient and values below 1 indicate relative inefficiency.

#### 3.2.4. Serial mediation model
Earlier mediation analysis investigations into the link between ESG performance and default risk have relied on conventional mediation techniques like the coefficient-based approach and structural equation modelling (Azmi & Hendranastiti, 2025; Da Fermo et al., 2025; Habib, 2023; Mushafiq et al., 2023; Zeng & Lau, 2025). Although these methods are well established, they struggle to accommodate multiple mediators and moderators within a single analytic framework. By contrast, Hayes (2017) PROCESS model’s serial mediation approach offers distinct advantages. First, it allows and can simultaneously handle the inclusion of multiple mediators, moderators, or their combinations in one integrated model, thus enhancing the analytical flexibility and dynamic nature. Second, Hayes’ PROCESS model frames the mediation modelling with logic for decomposing total, direct, and indirect effects, which differs from Baron and Kenny’s (1986) model (Hayes, 2009). These strengths place Hayes’s serial mediation superior to earlier models and address a critical methodological gap in the literature.

Accordingly, this study uses the PROCESS model 6 and proceeds with the serial mediation model as proposed by Hayes (2017). The serial mediation allows for multiple mediators in a causal chain linking a predictor (X) to an outcome (Y) through multiple mediators (M1 and M2). It can assess how X influences M1, which influences M2, ultimately affecting Y. We designed two serial mediation models in this study. The conceptual and statistical diagrams are presented in Fig. 1.

Based on the PROCESS model 6, the following models have been formulated:

$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{20} + \beta_{21}ESG_{it} + \beta_{2n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (5)$$
$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{30} + \beta_{31}ESG_{it} + \beta_{32}ROA_{it} + \beta_{3n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (6)$$
$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{40} + \beta_{41}ESG_{it} + \beta_{42}COD_{it} + \beta_{4n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (7)$$
$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{50} + \beta_{51}ESG_{it} + \beta_{52}CEF_{it} + \beta_{5n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (8)$$
$$ROA_{it} = \beta_{60} + \beta_{61}ESG_{it} + \beta_{6n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (9)$$
$$COD_{it} = \beta_{70} + \beta_{71}ESG_{it} + \beta_{72}ROA_{it} + \beta_{7n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (10)$$
$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{80} + \beta_{81}ESG_{it} + \beta_{82}ROA_{it} + \beta_{83}COD_{it} + \beta_{8n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (11)$$
$$CEF_{it} = \beta_{90} + \beta_{91}ESG_{it} + \beta_{9n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (12)$$
$$ROA_{it} = \beta_{10,0} + \beta_{10,1}ESG_{it} + \beta_{10,2}CEF_{it} + \beta_{10,n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (13)$$
$$Zscore_{it} = \beta_{11,0} + \beta_{11,1}ESG_{it} + \beta_{11,2}CEF_{it} + \beta_{11,3}ROA_{it} + \beta_{11,n} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (14)$$

#### 3.2.5. Diagnostic tests and regression
Various panel diagnostic tests are performed to determine the presence of multicollinearity, heteroscedasticity, serial correlation, cross-sectional dependence (CSD), and non-stationarity in the data. First, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) test is performed to check for multicollinearity. The Wooldridge test of serial correlation and modified heteroscedasticity tests are conducted to check for serial correlation and heteroscedasticity in the data, respectively. Further, the Pesaran CD test checks for cross-sectional dependence in the data (Pesaran, 2004). Moreover, this study employs the Cross-sectional Im-Pesaran (CIPS) tests to see whether the data are stationary. CIPS, a second-generation unit root test, is performed for robust results in the presence of CSD (Pesaran, 2007).

#### 3.2.6. Propensity score matching (PSM)
This study uses the PSM to investigate whether firms that are part of the NIFTY100ESG[^1] index demonstrate a significant difference in default risk when contrasted with those not included in the ESG index. The PSM method serves as a robust analytical tool for evaluating the effects of a specific treatment or intervention on a designated group by comparing it with an untreated or control group. The PSM method proves advantageous in scenarios where random trials or experiments are not feasible for the data. The process of matching is conducted through the application of propensity scores. The propensity scores represent the conditional probabilities derived from various covariates. The PSM reduces the complication of multidimensional covariates into a single-dimensional score. The matching process is conducted based on these scores, which helps minimize selection bias.

Initially, all the variables are defined. The treatment variable is the NIFTY100ESG index, which is a dummy variable. In this study, firms in the NIFTY100ESG index are assigned a value of 1, while those not included are assigned a value of 0. The Z-score represents the dependent variable. The covariates are the same as the control variables used in the previous section. The estimation of propensity scores is predicted using a probit model. The propensity scores represent the probability of an individual receiving treatment given the covariates.

$$Probit (P = 1) = \beta_0 + \beta_{it} \sum Controls_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \quad (15)$$

Following the estimation of propensity scores, the subsequent steps involve matching treated firms with control firms that exhibit similar propensity scores. This research uses a one-to-one matching approach, applying the nearest-neighbor technique. Following the matching process, it is essential to assess the presence of common support, as this guarantees that the treated and control firms exhibit overlapping propensity scores. To evaluate the quality of the match, balance tests are performed, comparing the mean differences in covariates before and after the matching process. A good match should significantly reduce differences between the treated and control groups. The next step involves estimating treatment effects, particularly the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATET), which measures the impact of ESG performance on the default risk for firms that are part of the NIFTY100ESG index compared to those that are not. A statistically significant positive ATET indicates that ESG improves financial stability, while a negative ATET suggests a detrimental effect.

This PSM technique is also used in the ex-post analysis to compare the ESG scores of actual defaulted firms to those of matched non-defaulted firms, thereby examining whether poor ESG performance is associated with and predicts actual corporate default events.

### 4.3. Addressing the endogeneity issue

While determining the model, the endogeneity problem might pose a significant issue. The results may be biased by endogeneity, which can arise from reverse causality or omitted variable bias (Atif & Ali, 2021). First, many variables exist but are omitted from the model and affect the default risk. Further, capturing all the variables affecting the Z-score in a single model is difficult. This leads to omitted variable bias. Second, some variables in the model may be determined within the model, and the direction of causation is unclear. Therefore, this study applies the two-step system GMM model, panel IV two-stage least squares (2SLS), and lagged regression model to address the endogeneity issue.

The study estimates the baseline model using two-step system GMM, panel IV 2SLS, and lagged regression model, and the results are presented in Table 8. Column 1 presents the results of the two-step system GMM model. This result shows that ESG performance has a statistically significant positive impact on Z-score. Again, this is similar to baseline regression. Regarding the model’s validity, results show that the prerequisite conditions for robust results are satisfied. First, the lagged value of the Z-score is statistically significant in all the models. Second, there is a first-order serial correlation but no second-order serial correlation, as AR (2) is insignificant as per the Arellano-Bond test statistics. Third, the p-values for the Hansen test in all the models are insignificant, implying that the instruments are valid. Finally, the number of instruments is less than the number of groups. These results confirm the model’s dynamic nature.

Regarding the panel IV 2SLS approach, this method requires the selection of an appropriate instrumental variable for the endogenous regressor. A valid instrument must satisfy three key conditions: relevance (correlated with the endogenous variable), exogeneity (uncorrelated with the error term), and being unaffected by confounding variables. Previous research, like Sun and Cui (2013) and Ali et al. (2018), has used the industry median as an instrument for the endogenous variables. Following their studies, this study identifies the industry median ESG (ESG-MED) score as the instrumental variable. Moreover, the use of industry-level median ESG as an instrument is well-documented in prior research by Atif and Ali (2021), supporting its validity for this analysis. The justification for using the industry median ESG is that a firm’s ESG score is closely related to the ESG profile of its industry due to similar operational activities and competitive dynamics. In contrast, the industry median ESG is unlikely to directly affect an individual firm’s default risk.

The IV 2SLS results are presented in column 2 of Table 8. In the first stage regression, where firm-level ESG is the dependent variable, the industry median ESG (ESG-MED) exhibits a significant positive impact, confirming the relevance of the instrument. Further, the F-statistic is also very high and greater than 10, suggesting that the instrument is not weak. Then, in the second stage, the ESG demonstrates a significant positive impact on the Z-score. These results, calculated using 2SLS, are highly comparable in trend and direction to the two-step system GMM model results, with only minor changes in magnitude. The Kleibergen-Paap LM statistic is statistically significant, indicating that the equation is not under-identified. Furthermore, both the Cragg-Donald and Kleibergen-Paap F-statistics exceed the crucial values at the 10 % level that Stock and Yogo (2005) specified, indicating that the equation is not weakly identified. Thus, our findings are robust even in the presence of endogeneity.

Finally, this study employs the lagged regression to address the potential endogeneity. In this approach, the current value of the Z-score is regressed on the lagged values of the independent and control variables. The results in column 3 of Table 8 indicate that the lagged ESG variable has a statistically significant and positive impact on the current Z-score. This finding not only addresses endogeneity issues but also provides additional evidence that ESG performance is a predictor of default risk, i.e., higher ESG performance in the preceding year is also associated with a reduced likelihood of default in the current year.

### 4.5. Mechanism and channel analysis using ROA, COD, and CEF: serial mediation

To identify the channel and mechanism via which ESG reduces the probability of default, the study investigates the mediating role of ROA, COD, and corporate efficiency (CEF). The study proceeds with the simple parallel mediation using PROCESS model 4 and two serial mediation analyses using PROCESS model 6 as proposed by Hayes (2017). In the simple parallel mediation model, the analysis evaluates the role of each mediator (ROA, COD, and CEF) separately, one at a time, using the PROCESS model 4. In the serial mediation, two mediators are used simultaneously using the PROCESS model 6. In the first serial mediation model, ROA and COD are used, and in the second model, CEF and ROA are used in the relationship between ESG and default risk.

Table 11 presents the results of Eqs. (5)–(14) using the DKSE estimator with fixed effects after controlling for covariates. From column 1, it can be observed that ESG significantly positively impacts the Z-score. This means that an enhancement in ESG performance reduces the probability of corporate default, showing a direct positive effect of 0.0468. The supportive aspect is that ESG resilience in firms enhances trust among the stakeholders, resulting in financial stability and reducing the probability of default. These firms are less likely to find themselves in a position of financial distress, allowing them to meet their contractual obligations more efficiently.

#### 4.5.1. Simple mediation analysis
In the simple mediation analysis, ROA emerges as a significant mediator. When ROA is included (column 2), the direct effect of ESG on Z-score decreases to 0.0376 from 0.0468, implying a partial mediation with an indirect effect of 0.0092. This result highlights that ESG not only directly strengthens financial stability but also by indirectly improving profitability (ROA). Similarly, when COD is introduced as a mediator (column 3), ESG’s effect on Z-score drops to 0.0458, with COD having a negative and significant path to Z-score. It suggests that COD partially mediates between ESG and Z-scores with an indirect effect of 0.0009. The partial mediation effect of COD suggests that ESG not only directly lowers default risk but also indirectly enhances financial stability by reducing the cost of debt. Finally, examining CEF as a mediator (column 4) shows that both ESG and CEF are positive and statistically significant with a Z-score. This indicates an improved corporate efficiency as another pathway through which ESG mitigates corporate default risk.

#### 4.5.2. Serial mediation analysis
The serial mediation models further extend these insights. Studying the first serial mediation effect of ROA and COD reveals that ESG positively influences ROA (column 5), which in turn negatively affects COD (column 6). The impact of ESG on the Z-score via ROA and COD is positive after controlling for the covariates (column 7). Also, ESG’s impact on Z-score has decreased from 0.0468 in column 1 to 0.0370 in column 7 after controlling for ROA and COD. Further, the signs of the coefficients of mediators in column 7 are as expected. Thus, it suggests that ROA and COD serially mediate between ESG and Z-score with an indirect effect of 0.00012. In summary, ESG enhancement reduces the probability of corporate default by improving the Altman Z-score, mediated by ROA and COD, as shown in Fig. 2. In short, higher ESG performance enhances a firm’s ROA, and an improved ROA reduces the COD. This reduced COD minimizes the likelihood of default by enhancing the Z-score. These results align with the findings of Atif and Ali (2021) and Li et al. (2022). Thus, Hypothesis 2 is accepted.

Similarly, the second serial mediation model using CEF and ROA shows that ESG has a significant positive impact on CEF (column 8), which positively impacts ROA (column 9). Further, the impact of ESG on the Z-score via CEF and ROA is positive, and it decreased from 0.0468 to 0.0416 in column 10. Moreover, the signs of one of the mediators, ROA, in column 10 are as expected, confirming an indirect effect. Thus, it suggests that CEF and ROA serially mediate between ESG and Z-score with an indirect effect of 0.0125. In short, stronger ESG performance enhances corporate efficiency, improving ROA and ultimately lowering the likelihood of default by enhancing the Z-score. Thus, Hypothesis 3 is accepted.

### 4.6. Additional analysis

To gather further insights and generalize the findings, this study conducts additional analyses based on existing literature and tailors them to the Indian context. This additional analysis includes disaggregated analysis, index inclusion analysis, role of the COVID-19 crisis, sectoral analysis, heterogeneity analysis, and ex-post analysis.

First, prior research by Xie et al. (2018) suggests that each ESG pillar may influence the outcomes differently, and not all the pillars are equally relevant for predicting the financial risk (Biswas et al., 2025). Therefore, this study conducts a disaggregated analysis to study the impact of specific pillars on default risk. Second, for the first time in literature, this study investigates whether firms’ inclusion in a reputable ESG index like NIFTY100ESG correlates with lower default risk. To achieve this, the PSM technique is used to evaluate firms in the NIFTY100ESG index, which helps to isolate the treatment effect of strong ESG orientation. Third, prior studies like Wang and Yang (2023) and Atif and Ali (2021) have already examined the role of corporate lifecycles as moderators with ESG on impacting default risk. Further, Vivel-Búa et al. (2023) evaluated the ESG-default risk relationship only during the global financial crisis of 2008, leaving a gap for additional research on the COVID-19 crisis. Therefore, to build upon and extend prior research, this study employs the Difference-in-Difference (DID) framework and treats COVID-19 as an exogenous event to assess whether ESG performance acted as a buffer during systemic crisis periods. Fourth, the sector-wise analysis allows us to investigate how ESG-default risk dynamics differ across industries, including the finance sector, which has not yet been investigated in the previous research in the Indian context. This extends the study of Biswas et al. (2025), where they investigated only the banking industry in India and no other sectors. Fifth, the heterogeneity analysis across ownership types (e.g., promoter vs. institutional) and business group affiliation is based on Indian corporate governance by literature Kaur and Dey (2025), which highlights structural differences in governance and risk-taking behavior of promoter CEO. Finally, the ex-post analysis, which compares ESG scores of actual defaulted firms with matched non-defaulted firms using PSM, adds a predictive validation layer to the main results. While most studies assess the ex-ante association of ESG with default risk metrics (e.g., Z-score, distance to default), the ex-post approach enhances causal inference by showing how poor ESG performance is retrospectively associated with real default events.

#### 4.6.2. Role of NIFTY100ESG index on default risk using PSM
To determine if a company part of the NIFTY100ESG index has any effect on corporate default risk compared to companies not in the index, this study uses the PSM technique. In the first stage, companies from the sample are matched to companies from the NIFTY100ESG index. Then, those companies that are part of the index are assigned 1, and the rest are assigned 0. The probit model is then run on the treated index companies, and the predicted values are estimated. These predicted values are used as propensity scores. The second stage involves forming one-to-one pairs by matching the propensity scores of the treated and untreated groups. This stage ensures that the companies in the pair share similar identities based on characteristics. Fig. 3 below demonstrates that all treated firms successfully found a match from the untreated group. Further, the bias between the treated and untreated groups is tested before and after matching for robust matching. The results are provided in Table 13 below. Table 13 shows that ICR and FS are inconsistent before the match. However, after matching, it can be seen that the bias between the treated and control groups has been significantly reduced. These reduction in inconsistencies after the match indicates that the control variables’ properties are not biased.

Table 14 below displays the results of the average treatment effect on the treated (ATET) group before and after matching to assess whether there is a substantial difference in default risk between companies included in the NIFTY100ESG index and those not included in the index. Prior to matching, the disparities between treated and controlled groups are statistically insignificant, attributable to the bias introduced by confounders. However, upon matching, the ATET or the differences in default risk between the treatment and control groups are both positive and statistically significant. This suggests that a firm’s inclusion in the ESG index enhances financial stability, as indicated by the improved Altman Z-Score. The ATET of 1.6179 suggests that ESG-indexed firms possess an average Altman Z-score of 1.6179 units greater than similar non-ESG-indexed firms. Thus, Hypothesis 4 is accepted.

#### 4.6.5. Finance sector analysis

Therefore, the present study adopts the approach outlined by Moreno et al. (2021) and Chiaramonte et al. $$FIN\_ZScore = \frac{ROA + (\frac{EQ}{TA})}{\sigma ROA} \quad (16)$$

Where $FIN\_ZScore$ is the Z-score for finance firms, $ROA$ indicates return on assets, $\frac{EQ}{TA}$ denotes equity to total assets ratio, and $\sigma ROA$ indicates the standard deviation of ROA.

In this analysis, the study also controls for two additional finance sector-specific variables, the efficiency ratio (EFF) and the capital adequacy ratio (CAR), to more accurately capture the financial sector’s unique risk dynamics. The empirical results are summarized in Table 17. The results suggest that ESG performance has a statistically significant and positive effect on the Z-score, implying that higher ESG scores are associated with a reduced probability of default among finance firms. Strong ESG practices improve a bank’s reputation, stakeholder trust, customer loyalty, and thus better access to funding sources. Further, good governance practices help ensure regulatory compliance, prevent scams, scandals, adverse events, and mitigate shocks and defaults. This finding supports the results of Biswas et al. (2025), who report that ESG practices contribute to a more substantial reduction in bank default risk in India in the long run compared to the short run, with the governance pillar having the highest impact. Our results are also supported by Gopane (2025), who found a lower credit risk for ESG-compliant banks than ESG-indifferent counterparts.

#### 4.6.6. Heterogeneity analysis
Corporate ownership in Indian firms is characterized by concentrated shareholding, with promoter ownership playing a key role in most publicly traded companies (Kansil, 2021). Promoters are often the founding family members or business groups that retain significant authority through promoter CEOs & board of directors, influencing strategic and governance choices (Roy, 2015). On the other hand, non-family and standalone businesses are more scattered in terms of ownership and management expertise. Institutional ownership, which includes both domestic and foreign institutions, has been increasing in larger enterprises and is frequently connected with improved governance procedures. Furthermore, many Indian companies are members of large business groups, conglomerates of linked organizations that share resources and strategic direction (Ray & Chaudhuri, 2018). These business groups can provide financial assistance, but at the same time, can also influence governance due to intra-group dependencies (Yiu et al., 2005). Non-business group enterprises often function independently, with governance influenced mostly by market and regulatory norms. Given the promoter, institution, and business groups’ own way of dealing with corporate affairs, it becomes important to incorporate such context-specific heterogeneity factors when analyzing the relationship between ESG performance and default risk.

##### 4.6.6.1. Institutional vs. promoter holding
This study performs a heterogeneity analysis to examine how the relationship between ESG performance and default risk varies across firms with different promoter and institutional ownership levels. For this purpose, the sample is split into two equal groups using a 50 % threshold: firms with ownership above 50 % are classified as having high ownership, and those below 50 % as low. We then separately estimate the base regression model for each subgroup. The results are presented in Table 18.

Among firms with high promoter ownership, ESG performance has a positive and statistically significant effect on the Altman Z-score, suggesting improved financial stability. In contrast, the impact is negligible in firms with low promoter ownership. This is consistent with Rastogi et al. (2023) and Kumar and Singh (2013), who view that promoter-driven firms leverage ESG initiatives to firm value, protect reputation, improve goodwill, maintain stakeholder trust, and enhance long-term value, which directly contributes to wealth preservation and default risk reduction. In the Indian context, where promoter control is often concentrated, ESG initiatives are more likely to be aligned with long-term strategic interests, thereby strengthening financial resilience. Conversely, in firms with high institutional ownership, ESG performance is found to have a negative and significant impact on Z-score, while in firms with low institutional ownership, the effect is positive and significant. A possible explanation lies in the nature of institutional investors in India, where primarily mutual funds, hedge funds, and asset management companies (AMCs) operate in a highly competitive and expanding market. With growing retail and high net worth individuals (HNIs) participation, these AMC institutions often face pressure to deliver short-term returns to attract and retain investors (Bushee, 2004). However, ESG investments typically require a long-term horizon to yield tangible results (Kotsantonis et al., 2016). Therefore, in firms with low institutional ownership, the management may face fewer short-term pressures and is better positioned to pursue clear ESG strategies, leading to improved Z-scores. In contrast, firms with high and dispersed institutional holdings may prioritize short-term profitability over long-term ESG commitments, thereby undermining financial stability.

##### 4.6.6.2. Role of promoter CEOs in business groups and non-business groups
The promoter CEO, one of the distinctive characteristics of Indian firms, is a person from the promoter family acting as CEO in a firm, taking all the major decisions. The promoter CEO takes several decisions not only on corporate strategy but also on the ESG aspects, which impact the long-term existence of a firm (Sahoo et al., 2023). Thus, it becomes important to investigate how the promoter CEO moderates with ESG to influence a firm’s default risk. For this, the study first identifies and segregates the firms that belong to a business group. Then, the CEOs of the firms who are part of the promoter family are identified, and this study interacts the ESG with the promoter CEO to study the influence on Z-score. The results are presented in Table 19 below. The findings show that ESG performance has a significant positive impact on Z-score in both the business and non-business groups. However, the interaction term (PCEO×ESG) is insignificant in the business group, while it is positive and significant in the non-business group. The positive and significant interaction between ESG and Promoter CEO (0.139) in the non-business groups indicates that ESG performance yields greater improvements in financial stability when a promoter CEO leads the firm. This means the promoter CEO in a non-business group moderates the relationship. In the Indian context, the business group provides strong control and coordination to its affiliates (Ray & Chaudhuri, 2018). Further, the governance and disclosure-related strategies are centrally planned by the group at the top, leaving a small space for the CEO to alter the group’s legacy (Yiu et al., 2007). In contrast, in non-business groups, the promoter CEO has a skin in the game and directly benefits from firm survival and reputation in case of small boards (Sahoo et al., 2023). Thus, the insignificant ESG–promoter CEO interaction within business groups suggests that promoter-driven governance and reputation effects are already embedded in the group structure. The presence of a promoter CEO provides no additional governance benefit beyond what the business group itself ensures. By contrast, in non-business group firms, a promoter CEO directly aligns ESG initiatives with the owner’s long-term interest, strengthening ESG’s positive impact on financial stability.

#### 4.6.7. Ex-post analysis in the case of defaulted firms
Previous sections of this study have established that stronger ESG performances are associated with a significantly higher Z-score, signaling a reduced corporate default risk. However, existing literature has not conclusively determined whether ESG performance can predict default instances effectively. To address this gap, this research undertakes an ex-post analysis, examining whether firms that have defaulted in the past were characterized by poor ESG performance. By analyzing real cases of defaulted Indian companies, the study tests the predictive power of ESG in highlighting financially vulnerable firms. This approach not only strengthens causal inference but also enhances the practical value of ESG as a tool for risk management in emerging markets like India. Accordingly, this study hypothesizes that firms that defaulted exhibit significantly lower ESG scores than matched non-defaulted firms with similar financial and firm characteristics.

Based on the availability of ESG and financial data for defaulted firms, the study identifies 15 publicly listed Indian firms that experienced default events in recent years for empirical investigation. The dataset comprises 138 firm-year observations for defaulted firms, representing 18.40 % of the sample, as the treatment group, and 612 firm-year observations for non-defaulted firms as the control group. Table 20 shows that the mean Z-score for non-defaulted firms is 6.7534, well above the threshold of 1.8, while the mean for defaulted firms is 1.2092, falling below the threshold. A two-sample t-test comparing the means of Altman Z-scores of the two groups confirms that this difference is statistically significant, providing initial support for the hypothesis.

For the ex-post analysis, the Propensity Score Matching (PSM) technique is again used. PSM is a robust method for ex-post analysis, as it reduces selection bias by pairing defaulted firms with non-defaulted counterparts with similar observable characteristics (Baiardi et al., 2021). This enhances causal inference in observational studies and is widely applied in finance research. Initially, firms that defaulted were coded as 1 (treatment group), others as 0 (control group). A probit model is run on the treated group by controlling for Z-scores and other covariates. Then, the predicted values are estimated and used as propensity scores for matching purposes with the control group. Graphical tests (box plot and kernel density plot) in Fig. 5 indicate that the matching procedure was effective.

Table 21 presents the results of the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATET) before and after matching. Prior to matching, differences in ESG scores between groups were not statistically significant. However, post-matching, the ATET is negative and significant in both the t-test and Z-test, indicating that defaulted firms have significantly lower ESG scores than similar non-defaulting companies. Specifically, the ATET of − 6.3522 means that defaulted firms have ESG scores on average 6.3522 units lower than their matched non-default control peers. This shows that ESG can effectively predict actual corporate events if properly combined with other financial metrics like the Altman Z-score. This confirms and accepts our hypothesis that defaulted firms exhibit significantly lower ESG scores than matched non-defaulted firms with similar financial and firm characteristics.

### 4.7. Robustness check

To ensure the reliability and consistency of the baseline model, this study conducts a series of robustness checks by using alternate sources, proxies, and changing the estimation model. The results are presented in Table 22.

#### 4.7.1. Altering the independent variable
First, this study substitutes the ESG scores from FactSet with those provided by Refinitiv, a globally recognized and reputable ESG data provider, and re-estimates the base model. The results are presented in column 1 of Table 22. Re-estimating the base regression model with Refinitiv ESG scores as the core independent variable yielded highly consistent results in both direction and statistical significance.[^2] This confirms the observed baseline positive association between ESG performance and Z-score.

#### 4.7.2. Altering the dependent variable
Second, this study tested the results using an alternative measure of default risk. In place of the Altman Z-score, this study utilizes the Distance to Default (DTD), which is rooted in option-theoretic credit risk modelling and incorporates market-based variables such as equity volatility and firm value. The DTD is a market-based, forward-looking proxy and is often considered informative for assessing default risk (Beaver et al., 2005). The DTD data are collected from the Credit Research Initiative at the National University of Singapore. The result of the same is shown in column 2 of Table 22. The regression using DTD as the dependent variable also reveals a significant and positive relationship with ESG performance. This again suggests that the beneficial effect of ESG is evident across both accounting-based and market-based measures of default risk and not confined to the parameters of the Z-Score model.

#### 4.7.3. Alternate econometric approach
Third, this study employs additional econometric estimation techniques such as Panel-Corrected Standard Errors (PCSE) and Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS). Both PCSE and FGLS are designed to account for heteroscedasticity, serial correlation, and cross-sectional dependence frequently encountered in panel datasets. The results are shown in columns 3 and 4. The findings remained stable and consistent with those obtained from the DKSE approach, further affirming the reliability of the findings.

#### 4.7.4. Adopting a machine learning approach
Finally, this study applies the KRLS model proposed by Hainmueller and Hazlett (2013) to assess the robustness of the earlier findings. The KRLS is a machine learning algorithm that examines the relationship between the variables using pointwise derivatives rather than assuming linearity and additivity. The findings are provided in column 5. The KRLS model findings are similar to previous findings and show consistency. However, in most instances, the degree of the KRLS coefficients was reduced compared to the DKSE estimates, but the direction of the coefficients and the central idea of the stated findings remain identical.

### 4.8. Discussion of results

The study’s findings are consistent with earlier research and give additional empirical support to the role of ESG performance in boosting corporate financial resilience (Atif & Ali, 2021; Li et al., 2022). The negative relation of ESG with the default risk in India is supported by evidence from developed markets, where firms with stronger ESG practices exhibit greater financial stability due to improved corporate transparency, reduced information asymmetry, better operational efficiency, stakeholder trust, and reputational capital (DO & Vo, 2023). For example, the environmental pillar’s contribution to operational efficiencies and cost savings is consistent with results showing organizations that invest in sustainability programs have higher profitability and lower risk exposure (Shih et al., 2021). Similarly, the governance pillar’s significant impact in lowering debt costs is consistent with previous findings emphasizing the necessity of governance tools such as audit independence, transparency, and management in lowering lender risk perceptions (Zheng et al., 2024). The study’s findings indicating stronger financial resilience among ESG firms post-COVID-19 crisis of 2020 are in contrast to those of Vivel-Búa et al. (2023), who reported an insignificant impact of ESG performance post-2008 financial crisis. This divergence shows the evolution and strategic importance of ESG in the contemporary corporate world. It also reflects how it has progressed from a peripheral concern to a critical component of risk management and long-term value creation.

These findings are also compatible with the ideas of legitimacy theory, which holds that corporations must align their actions with social norms and stakeholder expectations in order to preserve legitimacy. By incorporating ESG practices, businesses demonstrate their commitment to tackling ESG issues. This increases their legitimacy in the eyes of stakeholders such as investors, creditors, and regulators. This agreement builds trust, lowers perceived risks, and makes it easier for a firm to obtain external funding at favorable terms, as shown by the mediating role of COD. Further, the study’s findings on ROA as a mediator show how ESG integration directly improves financial outcomes. This supports the idea that firms can increase their legitimacy not only through external perceptions but also by demonstrating improvements in operational efficiency and profitability.

The primary assertion is that ESG assessment functions as an inherent safety net. Companies that spend significantly in environmental sustainability, ethical social practices, and robust governance frameworks frequently witness reduced volatility (Mahanta, Sahu, & Behera, 2024). Reduced ESG risks, along with more stable and transparent operations, enhance a firm’s attractiveness to investors (Petridis et al., 2023). The “insurance effect” is especially prominent during crises, when the capacity to endure market shocks is crucial. Regulatory authorities in India, like SEBI, are intensifying their regulation via the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) requirements. Companies that ignore ESG principles are more likely to face compliance penalties, reputational loss, or potentially higher capital expenses in the future. On the other hand, organizations with established ESG frameworks may benefit from less stringent regulatory treatment, increasing their financial appeal. Correspondingly, companies that neglect ESG considerations are at an increased risk of compliance penalties, reputational harm, or potentially elevated capital costs in the future. Conversely, organizations with established ESG frameworks may be poised to gain from simpler regulatory treatment, enhancing their financial profile.

These trends highlight an incentive framework: the greater a company’s investment in quantifiable ESG enhancements, the higher its likelihood of being perceived as responsible, creditworthy, and sustainable. The integration of corporate responsibility with default risk indicators indicates that sustainability serves not merely as an ethical position but as a strategic benefit.

## 5. Conclusion and policy implications

This research is guided by five objectives in the background. First, to examine the relationship between ESG performance and its impact on mitigating the risk of company default within the context of Indian stock markets. Second, to investigate the mechanisms and channels by which ESG influences default risk, specifically by analyzing the serial mediating effects of ROA, COD, and CEF. Third, to investigate whether firms that are part of the ESG index demonstrate a significant difference in default risk when contrasted with those not included in the ESG index during the COVID-19 crisis. Fourth, to analyze the variations in the relationship between ESG factors and default risk across various sectors and ownership structures. Finally, to examine whether ESG performance can effectively predict actual default events using ex-post analysis.

Using data from non-financial companies based in India from 2016 to 2024, this is one of the first studies applying Hayes (2017) PROCESS model 6 to examine how ESG as a predictor links to corporate default risk, measured by the Altman Z-score through three mediators—ROA, COD, and corporate efficiency. Based on the analyses, it is found that ESG performance positively affects the Z-score, reducing corporate default risk. ROA, COD, and CEF mediate this relationship, and the serial mediation effect is significant. Moreover, using the PSM technique, this study revealed that companies included in the NIFTY100ESG index exhibit a significantly higher Z-score compared to those not part of the index. Also, during crisis periods such as COVID-19, the ESG index acts as a financial buffer in protecting firms. This study’s sectoral analysis revealed that ESG favourably impacts all economic sectors, including materials, utilities, communication, and industrials. In firms with high promoter holding and low institutional holding, the ESG has a favorable impact. Moreover, ESG performance yields greater improvements in financial stability when a promoter CEO leads a non-business group firm. Finally, the ex-post analysis using PSM shows that defaulted firms have a significant low ESG scores compared to defaulted firms exhibit significantly lower ESG scores than matched non-defaulted firms with similar financial and firm characteristics, thus indicating that ESG performance can effectively predict actual default events. This relationship shows ESG’s backing for corporate sustainability, which is aligned with the legitimacy theory and supports the notion of ‘flight-to-safety’ and ‘Doing well by doing good’ in the Indian context. These notions hold that firms prioritizing sustainability and ethical business practices deliver value to their stakeholders. These findings are also fairly similar across the individual ESG pillars.

Based on our analyses, this study proposes the following policy recommendations. For managerial executives, incorporating ESG considerations has transitioned from a peripheral task to a strategic necessity that can minimize the cost of finance, broaden capital accessibility, increase financial performance, and improve corporate efficiency. By integrating ethical practices into all aspects of their business, organizations can enhance their attractiveness while pursuing capital. This tendency is expected to intensify as authorities contemplate the potential for reduced capital requirements or increased lending capacity for banks financing ESG-focused initiatives.

Policymakers can create a broader cultural shift by pushing lenders and banks to incorporate ESG factors into their business models. Thus, lenders are encouraged to consider ESG ratings and performance alongside credit ratings when financing borrowers, as it will promote the adoption sustainable practices and reduce the financial risk for the borrowing firms. By encouraging sustainability with tangible financial rewards, authorities can hasten the adoption of cleaner technologies, promote equitable social behaviors, and raise governance standards across industries. As a result, these improvements strengthen the financial system’s overall stability, a priority that has gained traction in the aftermath of global economic turmoil. At this point, regulators and financial institutions are incorporating ESG elements into risk analysis, signaling a shift in how ESG defines stability. If this trend persists, we may witness a financial system that rewards corporations that prioritize environmental stewardship, social equality, and robust governance. Companies that fail to adjust to these changes may face the risk of lagging behind strategically.

This study has certainly some drawbacks. Many internal, external, qualitative, or quantitative elements, such as corporate innovation, know-how knowledge, cashflows, etc., may mediate and moderate the relationship between ESG and corporate default risk. Therefore, other possible mediating mechanisms remain open for future research, particularly in light of evolving ESG performance frameworks and disclosure standards. Further, cross-country comparison can also add a new dimension to future research.
