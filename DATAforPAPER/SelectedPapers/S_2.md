# Visualizing environmental, social, and governance disclosure in non-financial reports: does it matter for lenders? A machine-supported approach

### Abstract

**Purpose** – In recent years, companies have increasingly adopted visual tools to communicate their sustainability practices, responding to growing stakeholder demand for more transparent and accessible non-financial information. Within this context, the use of visual content in environmental, social, and governance (ESG) disclosure has also gained prominence in academic literature. Under the lens of signaling theory, the purpose of this study is to investigate the relationship between visual ESG disclosure and the cost of debt.

**Design/methodology/approach** – This study adopts a machine-supported approach, relying on Google’s Vision API to assess the extent to which a sample of 279 listed firms included in the MSCI Europe Index incorporate photographs and images related to ESG issues in their non-financial reports for the 2023 financial year. A regression model was used to assess the relationship between the level of visual ESG disclosure, proxied by the natural logarithm of the number of ESG photographs and images retrieved from sampled non-financial reports, and the cost of debt.

**Findings** – The results of this study reveal that the majority of ESG photographs and images in corporate non-financial reports are linked to environmental themes, followed by social topics and, to a much lesser extent, governance content. In addition, the findings of this study indicate that a greater use of visual ESG disclosure is associated with a reduction in the cost of debt.

**Originality/value** – This study enriches the ESG disclosure literature by shifting attention from the content to the form of disclosure, emphasizing the strategic role of visual elements in non-financial reporting and their influence on the cost of debt. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to offer empirical insight into the impact of using photographs and images for ESG disclosure on borrowing costs. This study also advances the application of signaling theory by demonstrating how visual communication can serve as a credible signal of corporate transparency in capital markets.

**Keywords:** Visual disclosure, ESG disclosure, Cost of debt, Signaling theory, Non-financial reports, Machine-supported approach

**Paper type:** Research paper

### 1. Introduction

Over the past few decades, societies have matured novel and urgent expectations for corporate conduct, compelling companies to move beyond essential legal and economic obligations and incorporate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) concerns into their strategies and business models (Boiral, 2013; Schaltegger et al., 2017; Ling, 2019; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020). Attuned to sustainability concerns, investors have become increasingly aware of the win–win connection between corporate efforts on ESG issues and long-term financial performance, while other stakeholders are demanding more comprehensive insights into the societal and environmental impacts of firms’ activities (Arayssi et al., 2020; Adams and Abhayawansa, 2022; Nicolò et al., 2024). Consequently, ESG issues have gained momentum within corporate reporting practices, supported by the emergence of voluntary disclosure frameworks – such as those established by the Global Reporting Initiative and the International Integrated Reporting Council – and by regulations mandating sustainability reporting, including Directive 95 / 2014/EU (EU, 2023) and the more recent Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive which will be effective from 2024 financial year onwards (EC, 2023; Hummel and Jobst, 2024; Nicolò et al., 2024).

Amid these waves, broad literature has investigated corporate ESG disclosure practices via annual or sustainability reports, websites, and social media, mainly focusing on textual narratives (Branco and Rodrigues, 2008; Tagesson et al., 2009; Michelon and Parbonetti, 2012; Amin et al., 2021; Nicolò, and Andrades-Peña, 2024). However, as stated by Guthrie et al. (2020, p. 1156), there is a progressive shift from “narrative disclosures to extensive use of visual tools that seem to be more attuned with delivering the message to all stakeholders.” This is confirmed by a growing scholarly interest in the corporate use of visual disclosures – primarily photographs and images – recognized as powerful tools that complement textual and numerical information in conveying a company’s identity and reality via financial and non-financial reports (Rämö, 2011; Invernizzi et al., 2021; Fenk, 2025). Specifically, scholars emphasize that corporate disclosure through visual tools is more likely to capture readers’ attention to specific accounting content – such as ESG issues – because the brain processes and understands images more quickly and easily than text and numbers (Lock and Araujo, 2020). Also, visual elements are notably eye-catching and leave a stronger imprint on memory than purely written narratives, particularly when they feature vivid, colorful scenes (Hooks et al., 2010; Nicolò et al., 2022; Fenk, 2025). Although they may not align as closely with the financial statements as graphs or written narratives, they establish a stronger connection with ESG issues through their compelling and more accessible representation of people, places, and society (Davison, 2015).

For these reasons, photographs and images are increasingly occupying a larger share of pages in annual and sustainability reports, resulting in a paradigm shift from purely textual to visual corporate storytelling (Davison, 2007; Davison, 2015; Kanbaty et al., 2024). This has been facilitated by the ongoing shift toward digitalization, coupled with the rising influence of artificial intelligence (AI) (Lombardi and Secundo, 2021; de Villiers et al., 2024; Fenk, 2025). Advanced digital and AI-driven tools enable companies to more easily produce, analyze, and disseminate visual content – such as photographs, images, and infographics – that persuades readers by presenting complex realities in a more digestible, understandable way (Ang et al., 2020; Zeng et al., 2022; Hausken, 2024).

Nevertheless, while the debate on visual disclosure in sustainability reports is burgeoning, empirical evidence about the pros and cons of using visual disclosure for conveying ESG information is still scant. Prior literature investigated the extent to which companies incorporate visual disclosures into sustainability, corporate social responsibility (CSR) or annual reports (Breitbarth et al., 2010; Rämö, 2011; Hrasky, 2012; Pesci et al., 2015; Chong et al., 2019; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020; Invernizzi et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2022; Kanbaty et al., 2024; Fenk, 2025), Integrated Report (IR) (Nicolò et al., 2022; Widyatama and Narsa, 2023), websites, and social media (Lock and Araujo, 2020; Iazzi et al., 2022). However, the scholarly attention was mainly devoted to detecting whether and how images and photos are used for rhetoric and impression management purposes (Hrasky, 2012; Davison, 2014; Pesci et al., 2015; Chong et al., 2019; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020; Zeng et al., 2022; Kanbaty et al., 2024) and, along this way, how such practices tend to vary among different industries according to the social pressures to which are exposed (Lock and Araujo, 2020; Iazzi et al., 2022; Nicolò et al., 2022; Kanbaty et al., 2024; Fenk, 2025). So there is still little knowledge about the extent to which visual ESG disclosure is likely to produce financial benefits, especially in terms of the cost of debt.

The cost of debt is an important indicator of how lending institutions perceive the creditworthiness of firms (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2020; Eliwa et al., 2021). So it is widely recognized that, along with financial disclosure, ESG information is incorporated in lending decisions to evaluate firms’ default and reputational risks (Eliwa et al., 2021; Raimo et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). For this reason, an increasing strand of literature has investigated the association between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2020; Hamrouni et al., 2020; Raimo et al., 2021; Maaloul et al., 2023), focusing on traditional disclosure scores, primarily gathered from recognized International Databases like Bloomberg. However, there is still much to learn about whether ESG disclosure in the form of images and photos may impact the cost of debt.

With this in mind, this study intends to fill this critical research gap by enhancing academic knowledge of the effects of visual ESG disclosure on the cost of debt. To this end, this study adopts an innovative, machine-supported approach to assess the extent to which a sample of 279 listed firms included in the MSCI Europe Index incorporate photographs and images related to ESG issues in their non-financial reports for the 2023 financial year. Recent advances in AI-driven software and computing capabilities enable researchers to analyze and derive insights from large-scale data sets in a more systematic and streamlined manner (Niehoff, 2022; Bhandari et al., 2022; Fenk, 2025). In particular, computer vision tools allow for advanced image-based information retrieval, facilitating the examination of substantially larger datasets while ensuring consistent standards in evaluating photographic material (Lock and Araujo, 2020; Fenk, 2025). Therefore, following prior studies (Lock and Araujo, 2020; Fenk, 2025), this study uses Google’s Vision API, a commercial pre-trained cloud-based vision service that relies on advanced machine learning models to analyze and interpret images (Google LLC, 2023). The Vision API automatically classifies key elements in an image and assigns descriptive “labels” (Google LLC, 2023). In this study, the label detection feature was used to retrieve visual content from the non-financial reports of the sampled companies and assign relevant labels. A total of 27,430 photographs and images were processed, and their labels were compared against a granular ESG dictionary developed by Ignatov and Rudolf (2023) to determine whether the visual content included ESG-related topics. Then, a regression model was estimated to assess the relationship between the level of visual ESG disclosure, proxied by the natural logarithm of the number of ESG photographs and images retrieved from sampled non-financial reports – and the cost of debt.

The remainder of this study is structured as follows. The next section presents a literature review on visual disclosure complemented by a brief discussion of prior research on the relationship between disclosure and the cost of debt. Section 3 is devoted to the theoretical background and development of hypotheses. Section 4 outlines the research methodology. Section 5 presents and discusses the findings, and the final section offers concluding remarks.

### 2. Literature review

The contemporary business landscape is increasingly influenced by visual communication, with tools – such as photographs and images – now playing a more prominent role across all types of corporate reporting (Davison, 2007; Quattrone, 2009; Chong et al., 2019), thereby contributing to a “visual turn” in accounting (Davison, 2015, p. 6). Annual financial and non-financial reports are now ever more enriched by images and pictures that shape – and often overshadow – accounting statements, transforming them into “presentation-driven” documents (Hopwood, 1996; Davison, 2014). This is because frequent advancements in accounting standards, combined with the surge in mandatory non-financial disclosure requirements, have rendered accounting narratives confusing and frustrating for non-expert users (Hrasky, 2012; Nicolò et al., 2022). Consequently, visual tools may offer “a forum to communicate information to stakeholders in a language they understand” (Hooks et al., 2010, p. 356). Photographs and images are able to generate visual representations that convey experiences and ideas beyond the scope of written language (Pink, 2006), also shaping stakeholders’ emotional responses (Preston et al., 1996; Rose, 2012; Davison, 2015). Therefore, also prompted by the recent surge in digitalization and AI technologies, companies are more and more adopting communication strategies that blend traditional narratives with engaging, illustrative visuals to frame their ESG storytelling (de Villiers et al., 2024; Kanbaty et al., 2024; Fenk, 2025).

Many studies advocate that when readers approach a page of a report, they typically tend to focus first on the largest photographs, then briefly examine the other images before moving on to the text (Garcia and Stark, 1991; Zillmann et al., 2001; Knobloch et al., 2003). The bigger and more colorful the photograph, the more attention it draws (Zillmann et al., 2001; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020). So a text accompanied by a photograph is more likely to be read and remembered by viewers (Zillmann et al., 2001; Knobloch et al., 2003; Hrasky, 2012; Davison, 2015).

Therefore, some scholars have investigated whether and how pictorial images may help companies unveil the hidden value of Intellectual Capital (IC) that current accounting regulations neglect (Hooks et al., 2010; Steenkamp and Hooks, 2011). Their findings highlight that pictures are a popular IC disclosure mechanism, especially regarding employees, brands (Steenkamp and Hooks, 2011), and corporate culture (Hooks et al., 2010).

Most studies have focused on visual disclosure in CSR or sustainability reporting, primarily using theoretical frameworks based on visual rhetoric and impression management. Overall, studies highlight how companies embrace the use of photographs and images within sustainability reports to create specific meanings and idealistic realities about CSR and sustainability issues (Breitbarth et al., 2010; Rämö, 2011; Boiral, 2013; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020; Kanbaty et al., 2024). Notably, visual disclosure within sustainability reports is used to reinforce the positive statements about sustainability performance and persuade stakeholders about corporate commitment toward ESG issues to gain legitimacy and public consensus (Boiral, 2013; Ang et al., 2020; Usmani et al., 2020; Cabrera-Narvaez and Quinche-Martin, 2021; Invernizzi et al., 2021; Kanbaty et al., 2024). This strategy also underpins the symbolic use of photographs with different sizes and colors that evoke particular positive feelings and emotions (Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020; Kanbaty et al., 2024) or the repetition of some illustrative visuals that reinforce the narrative explanations (Davison, 2014; Pesci et al., 2015). According to Rämö (2011, p. 382), some “recurrent virtuous symbolic visual manifestations in the reports can be seen as ideographs that reinforce the promising ideological construct of both phronetic business practice and sustainability.” Along with this view, Chong et al. (2019) observed that New Zealand-listed companies systematically rely on photographs in their annual reports for rhetorical impression management, portraying idealized depictions of responsible corporate citizenship to stakeholders. Similarly, Zeng et al. (2022) noted that Fortune 70 global corporations extensively use photographs to disclose human rights issues, aiming to construct images of happiness, safety, diversity, and mutual support. In addition, Kanbaty et al. (2024) observed that US companies incorporate medium- or large-sized photographs in their sustainability reports, typically placed horizontally at the top of the page to enhance salience. They also noted that the visual content – primarily depicting people, technology, and nature – is carefully chosen to evoke emotional responses.

Based on the legitimacy and impression management standpoint, a strand of scholars has also explored the factors that motivate companies to increase their use of photographs in sustainability reports. From this perspective, studies evidenced that less sustainability-driven firms are more likely to rely on powerful visual rhetoric, using persuasive photographs (Hrasky, 2012) as well as larger and more colorful images (Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020) to manage stakeholders’ perceptions and pursue symbolic legitimacy (Hrasky, 2012; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020). In contrast, Fenk (2025) observed that firms with higher environmental performance use more photographs to signal their superiority and differentiate themselves from their competitors. Additionally, companies operating in environmentally sensitive sectors (Fenk, 2025; Kanbaty et al., 2024), as well as those with high consumer proximity and exposure to controversial issues, are more likely to rely on visual disclosure in sustainability reports (Kanbaty et al., 2024).

To date, only one study has explored the effects of visual characteristics in CSR reports. Specifically, focusing on large companies worldwide in the health-care sector, Invernizzi et al. (2021) noted that the use of a moderate number of images facilitates investors’ information processing and decreases their perception of hypocrisy, leading to higher levels of organizational legitimacy.

Visual disclosure studies have also explored other corporate communication tools, such as IR (Nicolò et al., 2022; Widyatama and Narsa, 2023), websites, and social media (Lock and Araujo, 2020; Iazzi et al., 2022). More specifically, Nicolò et al. (2022) highlighted that larger, more profitable companies operating in environmentally sensitive sectors are more likely to use visual disclosure in IR to support stakeholders’ decision-making processes and meet their information needs, while the experimental study by Widyatama and Narsa (2023) found that when information is presented visually in IRs, it is more effective in aiding investment allocation decisions in Indonesia. Furthermore, Lock and Araujo (2020) found that companies are more likely to use social media (Twitter) rather than websites to communicate CSR information and that those in environmentally sensitive industries tend to disclose visual content focused on social issues rather than on environmental or economic ones, while Iazzi et al. (2022) observed that photographs are an effective tool for enhancing stakeholder engagement on CSR issues via Twitter.

Therefore, the literature review revealed that although scholarly attention to visual forms of disclosure is mounting in both traditional sustainability and CSR reports, as well as alternative communication channels such as websites and social media, the empirical evidence primarily focuses on detecting strategies of impression management and visual rhetoric. Some studies have started to analyze the possible antecedents of companies’ visual disclosure practices (Lock and Araujo, 2020; Garcia-Sanchez and Araujo-Bernardo, 2020; Kanbaty et al., 2024; Fenk, 2025); however, little is known about the consequences of visual disclosure, such as extent to which the use of visual representations within sustainability reports may provide financial benefits to companies, in term of better access to finance.

Previous research has shown that accounting information plays a crucial role in the credit decisions of lending institutions (Carmo et al., 2016; Ding et al., 2016; Spiceland et al., 2016). Lenders provide debt capital to borrowers in exchange for guaranteed repayments of both capital and interest. However, as long as borrowers adhere to debt conditions, lenders do not interfere with the operational decisions, leaving control in the hands of the shareholders and management (Aghion and Bolton, 1992; Spiceland et al., 2016). Therefore, accounting information helps lending institutions assess the extent to which financed firms are likely to fulfil their obligations (Ding et al., 2016; Spiceland et al., 2016). Accordingly, accounting literature advocated that high-quality financial reporting reduces the information asymmetry between firms and loan providers, thereby lowering the costs that these institutions incur when acquiring and processing information about firms’ financial performance in terms of future earnings and cash flows and default risk (Sengupta, 1998; Nikolaev and Van Lent, 2005; Ding et al., 2016; Muttakin et al., 2020). This, in turn, reduces the cost of debt by lowering the interest rates applied to borrowed capital (Carmo et al., 2016; Muttakin et al., 2020). Therefore, scholars provided empirical evidence that higher financial reporting quality (Francis et al., 2005; Aldamen and Duncan, 2013; Ding et al., 2016; Spiceland et al., 2016; Amrah and Hashim, 2020; Muttakin et al., 2020) as well as more readable financial disclosures (Bonsall and Miller, 2017; Hoffmann and Kleimeier, 2021) are conducive to reduced cost of debt.

However, as sustainability has increasingly become a key imperative underpinning modern corporate strategies and operations, lending institutions have begun incorporating information about companies’ ESG performance into their assessments of borrowers’ creditworthiness (Eliwa et al., 2021; Raimo et al., 2021). Accordingly, many studies have provided empirical evidence of a negative association between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt (Dunne and McBrayer, 2019; Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2020; Eliwa et al., 2021; Raimo et al., 2021; Maaloul et al., 2023; Malik and Kashiramka, 2024). This suggests that when ESG disclosures effectively offer lenders appropriate non-financial metrics, a firm’s comprehensive commitment to these practices can significantly reduce the information asymmetry between the firm and its lenders (Kordsachia, 2021; Malik and Kashiramka, 2024). This enhanced transparency builds trust and reduces uncertainty and risk perception, ultimately contributing to lower borrowing costs (Hamrouni et al., 2020; Eliwa et al., 2021; Malik and Kashiramka, 2024).

Nevertheless, these studies focused on traditional disclosure scores, primarily gathered from recognized international databases like Bloomberg and Refinitiv. This study aims to extend this strand of literature by providing an innovative analysis of the effects of visual disclosure in terms of photographs and images within sustainability reports and the cost of debt.

### 3. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

The signaling theory is one of the most widely used frameworks to explain why companies voluntarily disclose information beyond regulatory requirements (Ross, 1977; Salvi et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). Originally developed by Spence (1973), this theory is grounded in the concept of information asymmetry, which arises when one party possesses superior information – either in quality or quantity – compared to another (Guldiken et al., 2017; Giakoumelou et al., 2023). In such situations, the more informed party, known as the signaler, can reduce this asymmetry by sending a signal to the less-informed receiver (Salvi et al., 2021; Schiuma et al., 2024; Mariani et al., 2025). As defined by Porter (1980, p. 75), a signal is “any action by a competitor that provides a direct or indirect indication of its intentions, motives, goals, or internal situation” and constitutes the core element of signaling theory.

Within this theoretical lens, voluntary disclosure serves as a strategic signal that companies use to shape stakeholder perceptions, enhance their credibility and obtain financial benefits from investors, lenders, and the broader market (Salvi et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). Among the various forms of disclosure, visual ESG disclosure – the use of photographs, images, and other visual tools to communicate ESG information – has emerged as particularly effective (Hooks et al., 2010; Lock and Araujo, 2020; Nicolò et al., 2022; Fenk, 2025). Visual representations enhance clarity, emotional resonance, and memorability, allowing companies to convey complex ESG content in a way that is more intuitive, immediate, and accessible (Fenk, 2025). Compared to traditional textual disclosures, visual content reduces the cognitive effort required for information processing (Nicolò et al., 2022; Bussoli et al., 2025). In this sense, visual ESG disclosure serves to not only inform but also actively engage investors, lenders, and other stakeholders, producing signals that are both credible and cost-effective. Disseminated through non-financial reports, visual ESG disclosure plays a key role in reducing information asymmetries, particularly with financial stakeholders who increasingly evaluate not just the content but also the form in which sustainability information is presented (Raimo et al., 2021).

In this context, the signaling theory provides a robust framework for framing the potential inverse relationship between visual ESG disclosure and the cost of debt. Prior studies have highlighted various mechanisms through which transparent and timely disclosure can reduce borrowing costs (Raimo et al., 2021). When information asymmetries exist between companies and lenders, the latter tend to incorporate restrictive covenants into loan agreements, resulting in higher financing costs (Sengupta, 1998; Muttakin et al., 2020; Eliwa et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). In this context, visual ESG disclosure offers a valuable means of reducing uncertainty. By transmitting credible, accessible, and engaging signals regarding a company’s ESG orientation and performance, it helps mitigate perceived risk and improves the firm’s reputation in the eyes of lenders (Raimo et al., 2021). Moreover, a consistent use of visual ESG communication over time can strengthen a company’s overall risk profile. Lenders are increasingly viewing ESG disclosure as a proxy for transparency, accountability, and strategic foresight (Raimo et al., 2021). As a result, companies that adopt effective visual ESG disclosure practices are more likely to access capital under more favorable terms and face fewer financial constraints (Sengupta, 1998). Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

*H1. The level of visual ESG disclosure negatively affects the cost of debt.*

### 4. Research methodology

#### 4.1 Sample
The sample of this study consists of 279 companies included in the MSCI Europe Index. Covering approximately 85% of the free float-adjusted market capitalization of the European developed equity universe, the MSCI Europe Index is widely used in academic literature as a reliable benchmark for cross-country and cross-sectoral analyses (Hampl and Vágnerová Linnertová, 2024). This index was selected for the present study because it offers a representative and diversified sample of established companies operating in the European context, which are more likely to adopt advanced sustainability reporting practices and have broader access to capital markets. Furthermore, the companies listed in this index are subject to increasing stakeholder pressure and regulatory scrutiny, particularly with regard to ESG disclosure. From the initial constituents, companies that did not publish a standalone non-financial report in 2023 were excluded. Likewise, firms for which data on the dependent and control variables were not available were also removed. This selection process led to the identification of 279 companies from various European countries, as shown in Table 1.

#### 4.2 Dependent variable
The dependent variable of this study is the cost of debt (CoD), which represents the interest rate a company incurs on its borrowings and serves as an indicator of the total interest expense associated with debt financing (Shad et al., 2020; Petruzzella et al., 2025). Given its close association with a company’s risk profile, a higher cost of debt generally reflects increased financial risk and results in a broader credit spread (Maaloul, 2018). In line with previous research, data related to the cost of debt were collected from the Bloomberg database (Raimo et al., 2022; Salvi et al., 2022; L’Abate et al., 2024). According to Bloomberg (2013), the cost of debt is measured as a weighted average that takes into account the debt adjustment factor, government bond rates, and the relative weights of short- and long-term debt within the total debt structure. Specifically, the CoD was computed using the following formula:

$$CoD = \left[ \left( \frac{SD}{TD} \right) \times (CS \times AF) \right] + \left[ \left( \frac{LD}{TD} \right) \times (CL \times AF) \right] \times (1 - TR) \tag{1}$$

where:
CoD is the cost of debt, SD is the short-term debt, TD is the total debt, CS is the pretax cost of short-term debt, AF is the debt adjustment factor, LD is the long-term debt, CL is the pretax cost of long-term debt and TR is the effective tax rate.

#### 4.3 Independent variable
The independent variable of this study is the visual ESG disclosure score (V_ESG_DS), developed through a machine-supported methodology. This approach allows for the collection and processing of a large volume of data, including the extraction of specific visual information that would be highly time-consuming and difficult to obtain manually with consistency and precision. In line with prior literature (Nanne et al., 2020; Fenk, 2025), this study adopts a computer vision technique, using Google’s Vision API (Google LLC, 2023) within a cloud-computing infrastructure.

The methodology is structured into two main stages. In the first stage, photographs and images were extracted from the non-financial reports – such as sustainability reports and standalone ESG reports – of the companies included in the analysis. For this purpose, a Python script was developed using the “fitz” functionality (Kastman, 2017; Fenk, 2025), enabling the extraction of image files in .png format. This process yielded a total of 27,430 photographs and images. The second stage focused on identifying ESG photographs and images using a comprehensive visual ESG glossary (Ignatov and Rudolf, 2023). Based on this glossary, three dedicated dictionaries were developed, containing 55 labels for the environmental dimension, 88 labels for the social dimension, and 28 labels for the governance dimension – for a total of 171 ESG-related labels. These dictionaries were integrated into the Python script to automate the recognition of ESG content within the extracted images. Each label was assigned a confidence score, ranging from 0 (no confidence) to 1 (maximum confidence). This scoring system allows for the differentiation between highly reliable and less reliable label matches. The image analysis yielded an average confidence score of 0.90, indicating a high level of accuracy in identifying relevant ESG content. As a result of this process, 3,690 ESG photographs and images were detected across the non-financial reports. The independent variable, V_ESG_DS, was operationalized as the natural logarithm of the total number of ESG photographs and images identified in each company’s report.

#### 4.4 Control variables
To enhance the robustness of the econometric model, this study includes a set of control variables that may influence the cost of debt. Specifically, the following variables were considered: *Profitability, Financial_leverage, Size, Board_size, Board_independence, CSR_committee, Board_gender_diversity,* and *Board_meeting_attendance*. *Profitability* was measured as the ratio of net income to total assets, in line with previous studies (Gerwanski, 2020; Raimo et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). *Financial_leverage* was calculated as the ratio of total debt to total assets (Salvi et al., 2022; L’Abate et al., 2024). *Size* was proxied by the natural logarithm of total assets (Frias-Aceituno et al., 2013; Raimo et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). With regard to the governance variables, *Board_size* variable was measured as the number of members on the board of directors (Frias-Aceituno et al., 2013; Vitolla et al., 2020; L’Abate et al., 2024), while *Board_independence* was calculated as the percentage of non-executive directors serving on the board (Frias-Aceituno et al., 2013; Raimo et al., 2021; L’Abate et al., 2024). *CSR_committee* was measured as a dummy variable that takes the value of 1 if the company has a dedicated CSR or sustainability committee and 0 otherwise (Vitolla et al., 2020; Raimo et al., 2021). *Board_gender_diversity* was measured as the percentage of women serving on the board of directors (Frias-Aceituno et al., 2013; Vitolla et al., 2020; L’Abate et al., 2024), and *Board_meeting_attendance* was calculated as the average percentage of directors attending board meetings (Agnese et al., 2024). Data on the control variables were obtained from the Bloomberg database.

#### 4.5 Model specification
To test the effect of visual ESG disclosure on the cost of debt, this study used a regression model. The econometric approach adopted was represented by the following equation:

$$CoD_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 V\_ESG\_DS_i + \beta_2 Profitability_i + \beta_3 Financial\_leverage_i + \beta_4 Size_i + \beta_5 Board\_size_i + \beta_6 Board\_independence_i + \beta_7 CSR\_committee_i + \beta_8 Board\_gender\_diversity_i + \beta_9 Board\_meeting\_attendance_i + \epsilon_i$$

The results obtained, in line with the signaling theory, can be explained by the ability of visual ESG disclosure to reduce information asymmetries between companies and lenders. Visual elements enhance the communicative power of non-financial reports by making ESG information more accessible, intuitive, and engaging. This ease of interpretation lowers the cognitive burden typically associated with complex ESG data, thereby facilitating a clearer understanding of a company’s environmental and social commitments. In addition to simplifying complex content, visual disclosure can also trigger emotional reactions, such as trust and empathy through the strategic use of photographs and images. These emotional cues can significantly influence stakeholders’ judgments, fostering a deeper psychological connection between the company and its audience. For lenders, such emotional resonance may reinforce perceptions of the company’s credibility and ethical orientation, ultimately enhancing their trust in the firm’s risk profile. In this light, visual ESG disclosure functions as not only an informational tool but also a symbolic and affective signal of corporate transparency and sustainability orientation. By improving the clarity and perceived reliability of non-financial reporting, visual ESG disclosure helps lenders assess the company’s sustainability profile with greater confidence. This, in turn, reduces the perceived risk associated with financing, ultimately leading to more favorable lending conditions and a lower cost of debt. Moreover, beyond reducing information asymmetries, visual ESG disclosure can also contribute to improving the company’s perceived risk profile. Financial stakeholders, particularly lenders, are increasingly attentive to not only the content of sustainability information but also how transparently and effectively it is communicated. The consistent use of visual elements may be interpreted as a deliberate and proactive strategy to convey openness, reliability, and strategic foresight. By presenting ESG commitments and performance in a clear and engaging format, companies are more likely to be perceived as responsible and forward-looking, thereby mitigating concerns related to potential environmental or social risks. This perception of reduced risk enhances lenders’ trust in the company’s ability to manage long-term challenges and contributes to more favorable financing conditions and a lower cost of debt.

### 6. Conclusions

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between visual ESG disclosure and the cost of debt, drawing on the signaling theory to interpret the communicative role of visual elements within non-financial reporting. In particular, it sought to explore whether and how the visual representation of ESG information through photographs and images can influence lenders’ perceptions and translate into financial advantages for companies. The results of the regression analysis confirmed the hypothesis formulated, showing a negative and significant relationship between visual ESG disclosure and the cost of debt. This finding suggests that the use of visual tools in non-financial reports contributes to reducing borrowing costs by mitigating information asymmetries and lowering the perceived risk profile of companies in the eyes of lenders.

This study offers several theoretical contributions. First, it enriches the ESG disclosure literature by focusing on the form of disclosure, rather than its content alone – an aspect that remains underexplored. By highlighting the communicative and strategic role of visual elements in non-financial reporting, this study shifts attention toward the how of ESG communication, complementing existing research that primarily emphasizes the what. Second, this study contributes to the growing body of literature on the relationship between corporate disclosure and the cost of debt. While prior research has investigated the impact of financial and non-financial disclosure on firms’ access to capital, this study introduces a novel perspective by focusing specifically on the visual dimension of ESG reporting. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to offer empirical insight into the impact of using photographs and images for ESG disclosure on borrowing costs. In doing so, it advances knowledge on the role that disclosure formats play in shaping lenders’ perceptions and financing decisions. Third, this study extends the application of signaling theory within the ESG domain by demonstrating that visual ESG disclosure can serve as a credible and cost-effective signal of corporate transparency. While the signaling theory has traditionally been applied to examine the effects of financial and narrative disclosures, this research shows that visual communication – through its ability to simplify, engage, and even elicit emotional responses – can play a comparable signaling role. This study thus bridges the gap between visual communication and sustainable finance, offering an integrated theoretical lens through which to interpret the effects of ESG disclosure on capital market outcomes.

From a practical perspective, the findings of this study have important implications for companies, particularly for those seeking to improve their access to external financing. In a context where financial stakeholders are increasingly attentive to not only the content of ESG disclosure but also how such information is communicated, companies should carefully consider the communicative design of their non-financial reports. Specifically, they are encouraged to integrate visual tools throughout their sustainability communication strategies. These elements can enhance the clarity, engagement, and perceived credibility of ESG reporting, helping to foster stronger connections with key stakeholders, including lenders. In particular, photographs and images play a distinctive role, as they can evoke emotional responses, reinforce key messages, and provide concrete illustrations of a company’s values, initiatives, and performance. To maximize their signaling effect, companies should ensure that such visual elements are not limited to decorative or esthetic functions but are strategically aligned with the ESG content. For example, in the environmental dimension, companies might include images that document efforts related to energy efficiency, emissions reduction, biodiversity protection, circular economy practices or sustainable sourcing. In the social dimension, photographs may portray workplace diversity, employee well-being programs, stakeholder engagement activities or community development initiatives. In the governance area, visual tools could be used to illustrate organizational structures, board diversity, ethical training programs or risk management systems. Rather than concentrating visuals in isolated sections or front covers, companies should aim for visual consistency and integration across all ESG areas, ensuring that each theme is supported by relevant and meaningful imagery. Doing so can help reinforce transparency and build a coherent narrative of sustainability commitment, which in turn may reduce perceived risk among lenders and investors.

Despite its contributions, this study is not without limitations. First, the econometric analysis is based on a cross-sectional dataset, which does not allow for an assessment of temporal dynamics or trends in visual ESG disclosure and its effects over time. Second, the focus on firms included in the MSCI Europe Index – while ensuring comparability and high data quality – may limit the generalizability of the findings to smaller firms or companies operating in emerging markets. Third, the measurement of visual ESG disclosure, although grounded in prior literature and supported by a machine-assisted methodology, may benefit from further refinement and standardization, particularly in distinguishing between types of images and their communicative intent. Future research could address these limitations by using longitudinal designs to capture temporal effects and by analyzing alternative stock indices that also include small- and mid-cap firms, thereby broadening the scope and relevance of the findings. Finally, further studies may explore additional channels of visual ESG communication, such as corporate websites or social media platforms, to assess the strategic use of visual ESG content across non-traditional disclosure channels.
