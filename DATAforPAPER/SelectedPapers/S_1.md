### ABSTRACT

ESG has become a critical focus in the global hospitality industry. This study addresses a research gap by examining ESG dimensions’ financial effects. Employing a two-way fixed effect model with firm-level fundamental data and ESG data, we identify the effect of the ESG performance of hotel firms in the United States on their cost of debt. Our novel results show that in general the hotel firms with higher ESG scores are significantly associated with lower debt cost. Furthermore, the study has observed that the relationship between hotels’ ESG scores and their debt costs has become statistically significant since 2014, implying that ESG has been more valued by lenders in recent years. This study presents the first evidence of the cost advantage linked to the ESG performance of U.S. hotel firms in debt markets, providing further incentives beyond stock markets to adopt environmentally friendly and socially responsible practices.

## 1. Introduction

Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors have gained significant prominence in the corporate landscape, as firms face increasing pressure from stakeholders to adopt sustainable and responsible business practices (Eccles et al., 2014; Ioannou and Serafeim, 2015). The hospitality industry, in particular, has witnessed a growing emphasis on ESG performance due to its inherent environmental footprint (Legrand et al., 2013; Singal, 2014a), reliance on human capital (Back, 2024; Baum, 2019; Baum et al., 2016), and high operational and financial risks (Kim et al., 2021; Singal, 2015). As the hospitality industry navigates the challenges of resource scarcity, climate change, and evolving stakeholder expectations (Melissen et al., 2016; Moscardo, 2019), strategically integrating ESG into their business strategies and operations has become a critical imperative (Higgins-Desbiolles, 2020; Jones et al., 2016; Legendre et al., 2024).

This growing emphasis on ESG performance is not only crucial for companies but also increasingly important for investors. ESG investing has emerged as a prominent part of responsible investing in the U.S. (Caplan et al., 2013), with debt investors increasingly incorporating ESG issues into their investment decisions (Driessen, 2021). In 2021, over $1.6 trillion in global sustainable debt was issued (BloombergNEF, 2022). Lenders, such as banks, are motivated to consider ESG issues to mitigate litigation risks under lender liability laws and avoid reputational damage from associations with companies with ESG concerns (Cogan, 2008). Banks utilize loan covenants and relationship-based lending to incentivize businesses to adopt sustainable practices (Walz, 2022). In the U.S., banks are facing increased regulation and supervision related to managing climate-related risks (Cullen, 2023). As the socially responsible investment (SRI) trend expands (Nilsson, 2008; Tripathi and Kaur, 2020), bond investors are offering premium prices for green bonds, reflecting the value they place on environmental concerns (Zerbib, 2019). However, incorporating ESG considerations into investments brings both benefits and costs to investors (Pedersen et al., 2021). Despite the trend toward ethical investing, mixed findings on returns cast doubt on whether ESG investments generate financial returns (Boffo and Patalano, 2020). This ambiguity highlights the need for more focused research on ESG’s materiality within specific industries.

There is a strong industry effect on firms’ ESG practices due to variations in market characteristics across industries (Borghesi et al., 2014; Gillan et al., 2021). The hospitality industry has a significant environmental footprint due to its high energy consumption, water use, and waste generation (Kasim, 2007; Singal, 2014a) and faces social challenges such as labor practices, human rights, and community engagement (Appiah, 2019; Bharwani and Butt, 2012). Given its inherent vulnerability to various uncertainties (Demir and Gözgör, 2018), firms within the hospitality industry are increasingly prioritizing ESG performance as a strategic measure to cultivate positive relationships with stakeholders (Kumar, 2023). This is especially relevant in the hotel industry, where customer perception and stakeholder engagement are paramount. Furthermore, the high level of debt in hotels makes them more sensitive to factors impacting their access to capital, as these factors can significantly impact their overall profits and shareholder value (Altin et al., 2016). With the rising interest rates of the U.S. Federal Reserve, the increasing cost of debt poses a severe challenge for hotel developers and owners (Wroten, 2022). Understanding how ESG relates to the cost of capital, particularly debt capital, could be critical for hotel firms to secure financing (Rhou and Singal, 2020), making it crucial to investigate whether the ESG performance of hotel borrowers can help mitigate the burden of debt costs.

While previous hospitality studies primarily examined aggregated ESG measures and their impact on firm performance (e.g., Kang et al., 2010), investigating individual ESG dimensions is crucial as they provide specific insights into different aspects of a company’s environmental, social, and governance performance (Velte, 2017). Analyzing each dimension separately allows for a detailed understanding of how a company is addressing key sustainability issues and managing risks (Amel-Zadeh and Serafeim, 2018). This approach helps stakeholders gain a comprehensive view of a company’s sustainability efforts and identify areas for improvement (Khan et al., 2016). It also aids investors, regulators, and other stakeholders make informed decisions about the company’s long-term viability and ethical practices (Friede et al., 2015). Additionally, focusing on individual ESG dimensions allows for targeted interventions to enhance performance in specific areas, leading to more effective sustainability strategies and risk management practices (Grewal et al., 2017). However, the impact of individual ESG dimensions remains underexplored in the hospitality literature, with few studies investigating specific components of ESG, including environmental performance (Singal, 2014a) and workplace diversity (Singal, 2014b). ESG dimensions affect firm value differently (Theodoulidis et al., 2017). Kim and Lee (2020) and Hwang et al. (2024), for instance, examined the relationship between ESG dimensions and hospitality firms’ ROA and Tobin’s Q. More importantly, lenders may value environmental, social, and governance information differently, as demonstrated by the introduction of the Green Loan Principles (GLP) in 2018 and the subsequent development of the Social Loan Principles in 2021 to promote the financing of green and social projects.

In summary, despite the strategic importance of ESG in the hospitality industry and recent studies focusing on its impact on the equity market (Attia et al., 2023; Hwang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024), a significant knowledge gap remains in the literature regarding its unexplored impacts in the debt market, particularly debt pricing. This gap is crucial because, while large shareholders (blockholders) tend to be long-term oriented investors and thus value ESG performance as an intangible asset (Edmans, 2009), it is unclear if lenders value ESG similarly as they focus on debt repayment ability. Drawing on stakeholder theory (Freeman, 1984), this study fills this gap by linking hotel firms’ ESG performance with their cost of debt, revealing how the debt market incorporates ESG in pricing decisions. More specifically, this research differs from prior research by examining which ESG dimensions are indeed material to lenders. By incorporating risk mitigation theory (Goss and Roberts, 2011), this study empirically investigates how each ESG dimension is priced differently by the debt market based on its impact on a hotel’s risk profile. This approach provides hotels and other firms within the hospitality industry with specific practical implications and responds to calls for more granular and situational ESG research (Edmans, 2023). Accordingly, this study addresses the following research questions:

**RQ1:** To what extent does the total ESG performance of hotel firms affect their cost of debt?

**RQ2:** To what extent does each ESG dimension of hotel firms affect their cost of debt?

**RQ3:** How do the impacts of total ESG performance and individual dimensions on the cost of debt evolve over time?

Despite various theories on ESG performance’s impact on debt costs, empirical evidence remains mixed across contexts. Thus, our findings contribute context-specific evidence to the ongoing debate within the broader finance literature regarding ESG’s benefits in the debt market. Contrary to prior mixed findings involving firms from diverse industries (e.g., Flammer, 2021), our analysis reveals a negative relationship within a relatively homogenous industry, where firms’ operations are highly sensitive to their relationships with stakeholders, including customers and debt holders. This study adds to the hospitality literature by analyzing the impact of ESG factors on the cost of debt for hotel firms from a lender’s perspective, which extends beyond the current understanding of consumers, employees, equity investors and rating agencies. Our study highlights those hotels with better environmental and social performance benefit from lower borrowing costs, which goes beyond just having better credit ratings. As far as we know, this study is the initial attempt to quantify the influence of each ESG dimension on hospitality debt costs. We utilize the Sustainalytics database, which exhibits less social desirability bias than survey research (Podsakoff et al., 2003) and has minimal noise in U.S. companies’ ESG scores (Berg et al., 2022).

## 2. Literature review

### 2.1. Stakeholder-driven ESG

Firms are under increasing pressure to “do good” (Huang, 2021). The hospitality industry, particularly the hotel sector, faces increasing pressure to integrate ESG considerations into their business strategies (Singal, 2015). This pressure stems from various stakeholders, including customers, employees, local communities, and regulatory bodies, all of whom have growing expectations for sustainable and socially responsible practices (Baum et al., 2016; Chua and Han, 2022; Legrand et al., 2013; Qi and Chen, 2022).

ESG is a framework that integrates three key sustainability factors into a firm’s strategy and business approach (Gillan et al., 2021). According to Hanson (2013), environmental (E) factors examine how a firm manages its environmental impact and protects the environment. Social (S) factors refer to how the firm manages its interactions with stakeholders, including but not limited to, employees, suppliers, customers, and the local community. Governance (G) factors consider the firm’s management team, including shareholders’ interests and internal controls (Hanson, 2013).

Environmental practices in hotels encompass energy efficiency, water management, waste reduction, and sustainable sourcing (Singal, 2014a). These practices not only mitigate environmental impact but can also lead to operational cost reductions and enhanced reputation among environmentally conscious stakeholders (Lee and Park, 2009). Social practices include employee welfare, community engagement, responsible tourism, and fair labor practices (Baum, 2019). These can foster positive relationships with employees, customers, and local communities, potentially leading to improved employee satisfaction and customer loyalty (Kim et al., 2017). Governance practices, such as board independence, transparency, and ethics compliance, are crucial for effective corporate governance and aligning the interests of managers and stakeholders (Eccles et al., 2014).

In the context of ESG, stakeholder theory is particularly relevant for the hospitality industry due to several key factors. Firstly, hotels interact with a diverse stakeholder landscape, including guests, employees, local communities, and investors, each with distinct ESG-related concerns and expectations. This diversity necessitates a comprehensive approach to ESG practices. Secondly, by addressing ESG issues, hotels can create long-term value for all stakeholders, potentially leading to improved financial performance and sustainability (Jones et al., 2016). Thirdly, in an industry heavily reliant on reputation, stakeholder theory explains how ESG practices can enhance a hotel’s image among various stakeholder groups (Haddock-Fraser and Tourelle, 2010). Lastly, ESG practices aligned with stakeholder expectations can help hotels maintain their social license to operate in local communities (Mzembe and Meaton, 2014). These factors collectively underscore the importance of stakeholder theory in understanding and implementing ESG practices in the hospitality sector.

### 2.2. ESG risk mitigation

Risk mitigation theory provides another crucial lens through which to understand the importance of ESG practices in the hospitality industry, particularly in relation to debt financing. This theory suggests that robust ESG initiatives can help firms mitigate various risks, including environmental liabilities, regulatory penalties, reputational damages, and social conflicts (Goss and Roberts, 2011). By effectively managing these risks, firms with higher ESG performance may be perceived as lower-risk borrowers by lenders (Cheng et al., 2014). This is particularly relevant for hotel firms, which tend to have higher capital intensity, making them more dependent on debt financing than firms in other industries (Singal, 2015). However, the hotel industry’s intangible products, demand fluctuations, and discretionary spending in the hospitality business make it high-risk for lenders (Elgonemy, 2002). Due to risks, several U.S. banks reduced their loan exposure to hotels during COVID-19 (Moffat and Clark, 2022). With soaring interest rates, firms face difficulties in obtaining bank loans and the hospitality sector is severely affected, with 67.7 % reporting difficulties in Germany compared to 28 % in its manufacturing sector (Jtoysna, 2023).

Risk mitigation theory underscores the unique relevance of ESG practices for risk mitigation in this industry, stemming from several key characteristics of the hotel business. Firstly, the hotel industry is characterized by high capital intensity and dependence on debt financing (Singal, 2015). This makes risk mitigation particularly crucial for maintaining access to capital at favorable terms. Hotels face numerous operational risks related to environmental impact, labor practices, and governance issues, and ESG practices can help mitigate these risks (Legrand et al., 2013; Singal, 2014a).

The social dimension of ESG mitigates risks related to labor practices and community relations. Appiah (2019) found that community involvement, a key ESG practice, significantly predicts higher employee job satisfaction. Similarly, Lee and Park (2009) showed that socially responsible practices can enhance a hotel’s reputation and customer loyalty, ultimately leading to superior financial performance.

The governance dimension addresses risks related to corporate governance practices, transparency, and their influence on agency costs and information asymmetry. Goss and Roberts (2011) found that hotels with stronger corporate governance mechanisms, including board independence and ownership structure, exhibit better financial performance and lower agency costs, suggesting the importance of governance practices in the hospitality industry.

The combination of stakeholder theory and risk mitigation theory provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the potential relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt in the hospitality industry. Stakeholder theory explains why hotels engage in ESG practices and how these practices can create value, while risk mitigation theory explains how these practices can reduce various risks, potentially leading to more favorable lending terms. ### 2.3. Decomposing ESG impact

This study measures ESG performance in two steps. Initially, a total ESG score was used to measure firm ESG performance. The rationale is to first assess the ESG’s effect from a strategic perspective—whether integrating ESG into business strategy influences the cost of debt. This overall assessment is particularly critical for the hotel industry, as hotels often operate with limited financial resources and face increasing challenges in making strategic decisions about allocating funds among marketing, technology upgrades, room renovations, and ESG initiatives (PwC, 2023; Singh and Wang, 2021). Therefore, the initial inquiry is to determine ESG’s strategic importance.

Subsequently, this study decomposed the total ESG impact into individual dimensional impacts for both theoretical and practical reasons. Theoretically, stakeholders may have differing preferences when evaluating a firm’s ESG factors. For example, Malshe et al. (2023) found that consumers reward the environmental (E) dimension for its altruistic motives, but view governance (G) negatively due to perceived self-interest motives. However, institutional investors, such as pension funds, appreciate governance (G) but see environmental (E) efforts as costly. Practically, given limited resources, hotels must prioritize resources toward the most impactful ESG factors. It remains unclear how lenders view these dimensions differently in the hotel industry. Thus, this study aims to fill this gap by analyzing how firms’ ESG performance is connected to their cost of debt, as well as the multifaceted role ESG can play in lending decisions, which is beyond the stock market.

However, general finance literature has shown mixed results concerning the relationship between firms’ ESG scores and their cost of debt (e.g., Eliwa et al., 2021; Flammer, 2021; Revelli and Viviani, 2015; Sharfman and Fernando, 2008). For example, Apergis et al. (2022) found that total ESG, including all three dimensions, can reduce the cost of debt for firms with the S&P 500. Conversely, Arora and Sharma (2022) observed an insignificant relationship between the environmental dimension and the cost of debt for Indian firms. Similarly, Eliwa et al. (2021) found an insignificant relationship between the governance dimension and the cost of debt in EU countries. These inconclusive findings suggest whether debt markets’ response to high ESG performance and its individual dimensions, is context-dependent and likely differs across industries (Endrikat et al., 2014). Therefore, an industry-specific approach is increasingly necessary to account for the contextual differences (e.g., Feng and Wu, 2023). For example, the relationship between ESG and the cost of debt in the hospitality industry may be context-dependent due to factors such as strong chain brands (O’Neill and Mattila, 2010), high fixed costs (Singh and Upneja, 2007), high tangible assets (Degryse et al., 2012; Sheel, 1994), and the franchising business model (Koh et al., 2009).

Specifically, in the hospitality industry, while studies have primarily focused on ESG’s effects on equity market outcomes, such as stock returns and equity holder risks (see meta-analysis by Lee et al., 2023), its effects on debt market outcomes call for more research attention (Kim et al., 2017), especially in the context of rising interest rates globally. More specifically, existing hospitality research has investigated the association between ESG and various outcomes, including ROA, Tobin’s Q, stock returns, and equity risk measures (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Dogru and Sirakaya-Turk, 2016; Kim et al., 2017; Ozdemir et al., 2020; Su and Chen, 2020; Theodoulidis et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2022), but only a few studies have explored its effect on credit ratings (Singal, 2014a, 2014b). Despite the growing importance of ESG practices in the hospitality industry, there is limited knowledge about whether hospitality firms’ ESG profiles influence their costs of debt. Given the unique challenges the hotel industry faces in its impact on various stakeholders and its inherent high risk (Singal, 2015), ESG has become an intrinsic part of this industry. Additionally, hotel firms’ considerable investments in human resources and the strategic value of advertising are often underrepresented in financial reports (Qi et al., 2018). Thus, it is crucial for the hotel industry to understand how ESG and its dimensions, as non-financial measures, affect the cost of debt.

### 2.4. Theoretical framework and hypotheses

Risk mitigation theory suggests that firms with strong ESG performance are better equipped to manage and mitigate various risks, such as environmental risks, social risks, and governance risks (Sharfman and Fernando, 2008). For example, Goss and Roberts (2011) provide evidence that enhanced ESG performance can lower debt costs through reduced agency costs and mitigate lender risk perceptions and information asymmetry. Likewise, Cheng et al. (2014) bolster stakeholder theory by revealing that robust ESG engagement leads to improved access to financing through the stabilization of stakeholder relationships. Another perspective is that firms with inadequate ESG practices carry higher risks. Specifically, borrowers with unsatisfactory ESG practices are prone to financial or legal difficulties, decreasing their value (Shin, 2021). Extending this discussion to the hospitality industry, we expect the following:

**Hypothesis 1:** A firm’s total ESG performance negatively affects its cost of debt in the hospitality industry.

Financing institutions are increasingly integrating environmental considerations into their investment and lending decisions, making firms with inadequate environmental performance susceptible to higher debt costs (Shin, 2021). The hospitality sector, reliant on natural resource consumption and its environmental footprint, faces heightened scrutiny (Legrand et al., 2013; Singal, 2014b). Previous research found that a company’s carbon risk is related to a significant increase in the cost of debt (Wang et al., 2021). For example, Ding and Shahzad (2022) provided evidence that imposing environmental administrative penalties can significantly increase a firm’s debt costs in the following year. This effect is a result of the negative influence of penalties on a firm’s disclosure quality. Furthermore, a positive correlation has been observed between the cost of debt and carbon risk for companies that fail to address the Carbon Disclosure Project (CDP) (Jung et al., 2018). Lenders and investors may perceive firms that fail to disclose this information as carrying higher risks, resulting in higher debt costs. In light of this, to mitigate financial risks, financing institutions have increasingly taken a cautious approach when examining the environmental risks associated with loans to mitigate financial risks (Wang et al., 2021; Weber, 2012). We postulate that:

**Hypothesis 2a:** A firm’s environmental performance negatively affects its cost of debt in the hospitality industry.

Firms in the hospitality industry prioritize adopting ESG practices to maintain positive relationships and a good reputation with stakeholders, such as customers and communities (Haddock-Fraser and Tourelle, 2010; Mzembe and Meaton, 2014). Notably, good social performance in hotels and restaurants positively impacts company value (Kang et al., 2010) and can significantly reduce the systematic risk of restaurant and casino firms (Kim et al., 2017). However, Franco, Caroli, Cappa, and Chiappa (2020) found that the impact of social performance on financial returns is not linear but instead takes a U-shape. Specifically, the cost-effectiveness of CSR initiatives depends on the degree of stakeholder engagement (Tundys, 2021). However, given the recent implementation of the Community Reinvestment Act, which aims to encourage banks to address the credit demands of the communities they cater to, specifically those in low- and moderate-income areas, banks may factor in social considerations in response to this initiative when determining loan pricing (Shin, 2021). This could influence the cost-effectiveness of hospitality firms’ CSR practices. Thus, we propose that:

**Hypothesis 2b:** A firm’s social performance negatively affects its cost of debt in the hospitality industry.

Effective board monitoring ensures strong corporate governance, reducing information asymmetry and providing reliable information for investors (Buertey et al., 2020; Chan et al., 2014; Giannarakis, 2014; Moneva et al., 2020). Creditors may favor companies with good governance structures, including ownership and board independence (Lagasio and Cucari, 2019; Shin, 2021). Research conducted by Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. (2006) demonstrated that companies with poorer corporate governance tend to experience greater debt costs. Since effective corporate governance practices are ensured by board monitoring (Moneva et al., 2020), and providing more reliable information for investors (Buertey et al., 2020; Chan et al., 2014; Giannarakis, 2014). Therefore, creditors infer a better corporate governance structure within firms (Lagasio and Cucari, 2019; Shin, 2021). In the hospitality industry, hotels are keen to implement sustainable practices (Singh and Dutt, 2023) because financial performance could be improved by some components of corporate governance (Babajee et al., 2022; Jarboui et al., 2015). Hence, hospitality firms with stronger corporate governance are likely to be perceived as lower-risk borrowers, leading to cheaper debt financing. Hence, we posit the following hypothesis:

**Hypothesis 2c:** A firm’s governance performance negatively affects its cost of debt in the hospitality industry.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1. Data sources

The construction of our sample was intricate. We gathered fundamental data for public firms from the quarterly Compustat and monthly CRSP from WRDS database. Additionally, we included ESG data from Sustainalytics and company credit ratings from Compustat S&P Credit Ratings. While we had ESG data from Sustainalytics spanning 2010–2019, the company credit ratings from Compustat S&P Credit Ratings were only available for the years 2010–2016. Therefore, our final data coverage was limited to the period from 2010 to 2016, as we needed to calculate conditional expectation.

We selected the Sustainalytics database because its noise-to-signal ratio ranked second for U.S. companies, as assessed by Berg et al. (2022). The most crucial advantage of Sustainalytics ESG data is that it provides monthly data; therefore, we could merge ESG data exactly with Compustat quarterly data instead of yearly data. Other authoritative ESG datasets, such as Refinitiv ESG Data, are annual data, which would be combined with the company’s fundamental data, resulting in a small number of samples. We mainly used scores for environmental, social, governance, and total ESG from the legacy database. Notably, there are both general and industry-specific ESG indicators within the three ESG dimensions (Sustainalytics, 2015). Furthermore, the weightings are industry-specific, accounting for the unique materiality of ESG issues in different industries and their relevance to industry stakeholders. Finally, we obtained the companies’ ratings from Compustat S&P Credit Ratings covering the period from 2010 to 2016.

### 3.2. Sample construction

We followed Ioannides and Debbage (1998) to construct samples. Specifically, if a firm’s NAICS contains 72111, 72112, 72119, 72121, and 72131, the firm was defined as a hotel company. We required that the company have fundamental data, credit rating data, and ESG data at the same time to calculate conditional expectation. Following Falato and Liang (2016), we used fuzzy matching to match company names from Sustainalytics with data from the CRSP, including company names and dates based on the CRSP historical header file and kept only the matches with similarity larger than 90 %, and manually compared the remaining company names. Finally, we found that 29 hotel firms with fundamental data and credit rating data at the same time, and 15 firms with fundamental data, credit rating data, and ESG scores from the Sustainalytics legacy database at the same time.

### 3.3. Variables in regressions

The cost of debt, which was the dependent variable, was calculated by dividing interest expense by total liabilities (Dunne and McBrayer, 2019). We used the following firm-level control variables based on Bradley and Roberts (2015) and Chava et al. (2009). We controlled firm-level covariates, including total assets (atq), market-to-book ratio (mtb), leverage (level), tangibility (ppent), credit (credit), profitability (profit), and the modified Altman Z-score (zscore). Firm size was measured in terms of total assets. Following Deng et al. (2014), the market-to-book ratio (mtb) was calculated as the equity market value plus liability book value and the preferred stock divided by total assets (Deng et al., 2014). A firm’s leverage (level) was calculated by dividing the book value of its total debt (comprising both short- and long-term liabilities) by the book value of its total assets. Tangibility (ppent) was computed as net property, plant, and equipment over the book value of total assets. Firms’ credit ratings (credit) were obtained from Standard and Poor’s long-term domestic issuer credit ratings. We followed Kisgen (2009) and assign ordinal values to credit rating levels as follows: 1(AAA), 2(AA+), 3(AA), and so on, through 22(D). Profitability (profit) was the ratio of the net income to the book value of total assets. We adopted the approach outlined by Graham et al. (2008) and calculated the modified Altman (1968) Z-scores (Altman, 1968).

We used lagged independent variables to mitigate potential endogeneity problems, as one might argue that companies invest more money in ESG if they have low financing costs. To remove the impact of outliers, all variables were winsorized at 1 % and 99 %. Our debt-cost model is as follows:

$$Cost\ of\ debt_{i,t} = \beta_1 * ESG_{i,t-1}/E_{i,t-1}/S_{i,t-1}/G_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 * X_{i,t-1} + Year_{FE} + Firm_{FE} + e_{i,t}$$

where $Cost\ of\ debt_{i,t}$ is firm $i$'s cost of debt at time $t$. $ESG_{i,t-1}$ is one of the environment score, social score, governance score, or total ESG score at time $t-1$. $X_{i,t-1}$ included control variables that might influence debt pricing. We included firm and year-fixed effects in all panel regression models with robust standard errors.

### 3.4. Regression model

We worked with panel data, specifically focusing on companies within the same industry, which results in time-series and cross-sectional dependencies. However, the conventional OLS regression can only solve cross-sectional dependencies, which is not appropriate for our purpose. Instead, we employed a two-way fixed effect model, a regression model designed for panel data analysis in economics (Wooldridge, 2010). This model’s advantage lies in its ability to control two unobserved confounders: entity-specific characteristics and time-specific effects, which have been widely used in economics and social sciences (Imai and Kim, 2019; Tomz et al., 2007). In hospitality research, previous studies have adopted this model to examine CSR and ESG-related contexts (e.g., Murata and Hamori, 2021; Youn et al., 2015), proving effective in reducing biases due to unmeasured firm and time effects in hospitality (Paek et al., 2013). Therefore, we applied this model to investigate the ESG effect on the cost of debt, accounting for these unmeasured influences. More specifically, we utilized an advanced Two-way Fixed Effects panel regression model with robust standard errors (As the sample number is not that big, using cluster standard errors can be inefficient). This combination allowed us to account for potential heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation that may exist in panel data, and also mitigate some of the endogeneity concerns.

We observed that hospitality firms’ ESG variables, such as their total ESG performance and their environmental, social, and governance performance, exhibit a significantly negative correlation with their cost of debt. Although inconclusive, these findings suggest that companies with stronger ESG performance tend to experience reduced borrowing costs. Other univariate correlations show that smaller firms, less profitable firms, less promising firms, more leveraged firms, and firms with poor credit tend to have higher costs of debt. Using panel data, we divided firms into two groups according to their median ESG scores each year and performed t-tests, providing initial evidence for the link between hotel firms’ ESG performance and their associated debt costs; detailed t-test analysis is available in the supplementary materials.

We conducted univariate analysis to provide preliminary evidence that good ESG performance is associated with lower debt costs (see the results of descriptive analysis, t-tests, and correlations in the supplementary materials). But one could argue that higher quality firms tend to have both higher ESG scores and lower cost of debt. Thus, it may not be ESG performance itself that explains the lower cost of debt, but rather the inherent characteristics of firms with high ESG scores. To address this concern, we examined the impact of ESG scores on conditional expectation of the cost of capital. Specifically, we aimed to determine whether, all else being equal, an increase in ESG score is associated with a lower cost of debt.

Using advanced Two-way Fixed Effects panel regression, we documented the influence of ESG scores on conditional expectation for the cost of debt (Table 3). The findings indicate a significant inverse association between the total ESG score of hotels and their cost of debt (b = -0.0055, p < 0.01), supporting Hypothesis 1. It quantifies how a one-unit change in the independent variable (log of total ESG score) is associated with a change in the expected or average value of the dependent variable (cost of debt) while holding all other independent variables constant. Specifically, the average cost of debt is quantified at 1 %, or equivalently 100 basis points (bps) every quarter. Furthermore, the mean value of the Total ESG score is approximately 54. With the regression coefficient for the logarithmic value of the ESG score being -0.0055, it can be inferred that an industry-wide enhancement in the Total ESG score from 54 to 64 would lead to a decrement in the cost of debt by 9.34 basis points (bps). This implies that an augmentation of 10 points in the total ESG score is associated with a reduction in the cost of debt by approximately 9.34 %. This finding bears significant economic implications, underscoring the pivotal role of ESG performance in financial cost management and strategic corporate governance.

Furthermore, we adopted a two-step approach, first assessing the impact of the overall ESG score on the cost of debt, and then analyzing the environmental (E), social (S), and governance (G) dimensions separately. This approach helps identify the specific ESG aspects that drive the observed overall impacts. It also offers practical benefits for the accommodation sector by highlighting which ESG dimensions lenders prioritize. Analyzing the dimensions simultaneously could lead to analytical challenges due to their relatively high correlation, potentially leading to multicollinearity, making it difficult to isolate the effects of each dimension. Given that overall ESG has been shown to significantly impact the cost of debt, we tested the effects of the environmental, social, and governance scores separately. The results indicate that the firms’ environmental performance has a statistically negative association with the cost of debt (b = -0.0036, p < 0.01), supporting Hypothesis 2a. Similarly, social performance of the firms is statistically negatively associated with the cost of debt (b = -0.0032, p < 0.05), supporting Hypothesis 2b. However, firm governance performance is not statistically associated with the cost of debt, failing to support Hypothesis 2c.

We conclude that hotels’ total ESG performance has a significantly negative association with their debt costs, primarily due to the impact of their environmental and social performance. As expected, the negative association between firm size (atq) and the market-to-book ratio (mtb) with the cost of debt is observed, suggesting that larger hotel firms and/or those with more growth opportunities receive lower borrowing costs. In addition, leverage (level), as expected, is positively associated with debt costs, implying that hotel firms with greater leverage face higher borrowing costs due to increased default risk.

### 3.6. Endogeneity

The Two-way Fixed Effects model is efficient in addressing endogeneity concerns arising from time-invariant unobserved factors that might be correlated with the independent variables. This method alleviates the endogeneity problem in panel regression by controlling for constant factors specific to each individual over time, thus reducing potential bias caused by endogeneity. To be more specific, individual fixed-effects estimation effectively addresses time-invariant factors across all individuals by removing individual-specific effects from the model.

While one may argue that conditional expectations require every relevant characteristic should be under control variables, however, there are still some variables that cannot be observed, such as the quality of the firm, CEO intelligence, etc. As long as this is time-invariant, individual fixed effects can effectively solve this endogeneity concern. Similarly, the year-fixed effect method helps to alleviate the endogeneity problem by controlling the constant factors of all individuals in a given year. To tackle potential endogeneity related to reverse causality, we used a one-period lag as our independent variable, as one may argue that companies invest more money in ESG if they have low financing costs. Although we recognize that other factors might still introduce endogeneity concerns, we acknowledge the necessity of incorporating additional techniques such as in future analyses beyond the current setup.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

The purpose of this study is to examine the effects of hotel firms’ total ESG performance, as well as its individual dimensions, on their cost of debt. Utilizing two-way fixed effect regressions, our findings suggest that hotel firms’ total ESG performance is associated with a reduced cost of debt, thereby addressing the first research question, which focuses on total ESG’s impact on the cost of debt. This finding may be explained by the unique characteristics of the hospitality industry. For example, the high tangibility of assets in the hotel industry (e.g., property, plant, and equipment) may reduce the importance of governance factors in lending decisions, as these assets can serve as collateral and mitigate the risk of default (Sheel, 1994). Additionally, the franchise business model common in the hotel industry may also reduce the relevance of firm-level governance, as the reputation and governance of the franchisor may be more important than that of the individual franchisee (Koh et al., 2009). Furthermore, addressing the third research question, which lies in the evolution of ESG impacts over time, this study observed a growing relevance of total ESG performance, mainly driven by the environmental dimension.

This study underscores the strategic motivations that companies possess when evaluating the expenses involved in transitioning to a sustainable business model vis-a-vis the capital benefits offered by financial markets. The study provides preliminary evidence that hotel firms with better ESG practices are granted reduced debt financing costs. As far as we know, this research represents the initial empirical inquiry conducted within the hospitality sector that explores the influence of ESG on the cost of debt in the lending market, an area that has received relatively little attention compared to its effects on customer attitudes (Qi and Chen, 2022), firm profitability (Theodoulidis et al., 2017), stock return dynamics (Chen et al., 2022), and firm market value (Singal, 2014b). Thus, this study extends the existing knowledge of multiple stakeholders’ stances on hospitality firms’ ESG (e.g., Chua and Han, 2022) by examining the views of lenders.

Our findings further suggest that hotel firms’ environmental (E) and social (S) performance are significantly associated with a reduced cost of debt, aligning with prior findings (Singal, 2014a, 2014b). Building on these studies, our study takes a step further toward the final pricing outcome of lenders’ decisions. It is the first attempt to quantify the value of each ESG dimension in debt financing costs specifically in the hotel industry. Moreover, our research highlights the growing significance of ESG performance in determining debt costs since 2014. The trend may be explained by the growing focus of regulators and policymakers on the role of ESG factors in financial markets (Driessen, 2021).

Interestingly, the governance dimension of the ESG framework was not significantly associated with borrowing costs. This finding aligns with CSR’s focus on environmental and social aspects (Gillan et al., 2021). Compared to the Environmental (E) and Social (S) dimensions, which are more externally focused, Governance (G) is measured by indicators more internally focused, such as practices concerning business ethics, corporate governance, and public policies aimed at protecting shareholders (Sustainalytics, 2015). Given the hotel industry’s growing emphasis on environmental sustainability and social responsibility, the E and S dimensions are more pronounced for stakeholders, whereas governance is less visible to them, including lenders. Theories such as asset-based lending (Degryse et al., 2012) highlight that hotel assets, such as property and equipment, can bolster lending opportunities even in the presence of high information asymmetry. This reduced need for information consideration by lenders may diminish the significance of governance factors in debt cost evaluation. Moreover, brand value signaling theory (O’Neill and Mattila, 2010), suggests that strong hotel brands can lower information costs for lenders, potentially emphasizing brand power over governance in debt pricing decisions. Additionally, agency cost explanations, as noted by sources like Singh and Upneja (2007), underscore how high fixed costs in hotels can limit managerial misbehavior and decision-making, thus mitigating governance risks. Considering the specificity of the hotel sector, it is essential to explore the hotel finance literature to ascertain if other studies on capital structure and cost of debt corroborate similar findings.

### 4.1. Theoretical implications

The findings of this study contribute to the existing literature by adopting a novel theoretical approach that combines stakeholder theory and risk mitigation theory to investigate the impact of ESG performance on the cost of debt for hotel firms. While existing empirical research in hospitality literature focuses on ESG impacts from the perspective of consumers and firms (Chen et al., 2022; Memarzadeh and Anand, 2020; Singal, 2014b; Su and Chen, 2020), this study adopts the lender perspective to investigate the impact of ESG on a firm’s debt financing, which has been largely neglected in the extant literature.

First, our findings support the application of stakeholder theory in the context of debt financing and align with risk mitigation theory. The results indicate that ESG may serve as a proxy for risk and may be used by lenders to identify creditworthy hospitality companies. This aligns with Chen et al.’s (2022) empirical study on the impact of ESG on stock returns, which revealed that hotels with high ESG ratings can be seen as safe-haven assets, yielding higher returns than portfolios with weaker ESG ratings. By demonstrating that ESG performance influences debt costs, we show that lenders, as key stakeholders, value firms’ efforts to address the needs of various stakeholders through ESG practices. This extends the traditional application of stakeholder theory beyond customers and employees to include financial institutions.

Second, this research decomposes the total ESG scores into dimensions to explore their individual relationships with debt costs in the hospitality industry. The results show that lenders place greater emphasis on environmental and social factors than governance factors. This finding provides significant new insights into how stakeholder theory and risk mitigation theory operate in practice, suggesting that in the hospitality industry, environmental and social practices may be perceived as more effective in addressing stakeholder concerns and mitigating operational and reputational risks. Informed by the finance literature, an alternative explanation for environmentally sensitive lending decisions is that a company’s environmental profile correlates with part of the firm’s risk that has been omitted (Chava, 2014). The governance score result contradicts our hypothesis based on prior studies, such as Shin (2021) and Lagasio and Cucari (2019). From a risk mitigation theory perspective, the lower emphasis on governance scores by lenders could indicate that they view environmental and social practices as more effective risk mitigation tools in the hospitality context. This might be due to the industry’s high visibility and direct impact on communities and the environment, making these factors more critical for risk assessment.

Third, by estimating the coefficients and depicting trends over time, this study presents evidence that ESG plays an increasing role in hospitality debt pricing. This trend suggests an evolution in how lenders, as stakeholders, value ESG practices, and how they perceive these practices as risk mitigation strategies. By identifying 2014 as a pivotal year, we provide a temporal context for the growing importance of ESG in risk assessment and stakeholder management in the hospitality industry. Our research presents empirical data supporting the increasing role of ESG in debt pricing and provides insights into the magnitude of its impact on loan pricing decisions in the hospitality industry. By analyzing this trend, this study highlights the growing significance of ESG in the loan pricing process and sheds light on the importance of sustainable business practices for firms seeking access to capital in the context of the hospitality sector.

### 4.2. Practical implications

This study has several practical implications for hospitality investors. The research indicates that adopting ESG-sensitive lending policies has the potential to influence external debt costs for hotel borrowers. Lenders may choose to abstain from lending to firms based on their ESG concerns because of ethical considerations or to mitigate the risk of lender liability and reputational damage. However, other lenders may incorporate the potential risk into their pricing by charging businesses with ESG concerns a higher price for their debt. Hence, affected firms with limited access to alternative sources of financing may face higher interest rates on their debt loans. The findings suggest that hotel firms’ total ESG performance can translate into cheaper debt. Therefore, hotel firms could allocate more budgets to sustainable activities to reduce debt financing costs. Additionally, they can leverage their ESG profiles by actively disclosing ESG information and communicating it to lenders when seeking debt financing.

More specifically, hotel firms can reduce debt financing costs by developing feasible environmental and social practices and leveraging their E and S information. Green projects such as pollution control, sustainable water infrastructure, renewable energy, wastewater management, and climate change adaptation can help develop E. To develop S, firms could participate in affordable foundational infrastructure initiatives such as clean drinking water, food security, and sustainable food systems. Unfortunately, firms with G profiles cannot be rewarded with reduced costs in the debt market; therefore, they may seek alternative financing options, such as equity financing, since debt may not be a cheaper option than equity.

Our study reveals that the impact of ESG performance on the cost of debt has become more pronounced in recent years. This finding is consistent with the growing demand for ESG data by investors and lenders, as documented in recent studies (Amel-Zadeh and Serafeim, 2018). As investors and lenders increasingly incorporate ESG factors into their decision-making processes, firms with strong ESG performance are likely to benefit from better access to capital and lower financing costs. This trend is particularly relevant for the hospitality industry, where the importance of ESG reporting has been highlighted by industry reports (PwC, 2023) and academic studies (Ioannou and Serafeim, 2015; Kang et al., 2010).

### 4.3. Limitations and future research

First, while we used a longitudinal dataset across years and firms, our data were only updated to 2016 based on availability. Future research can explore more updated data to examine how ESG’s effects on the cost of debt evolve, such as during economic recessions or expansions. Second, the impact of ESG varies by industry due to differing social and environmental externalities (Hoepner et al., 2016). Considering that the hospitality industry is typically a heavy asset industry, another critical factor is that allocation assurance alleviates the default risk to some extent, which could reduce the cost of debt. Future research should explore these variances across and within industries. Third, we focused on ESG’s influence on the overall cost of debt, not separating bank loan and bond costs. Future research can assess these markets’ responses to ESG, guiding firms’ choice between bank loans and bonds. Finally, future CSR studies could use a differences-in-differences (DID) model to further mitigate endogeneity, as suggested by Lee et al. (2023).
