**ABSTRACT**

Climate change is causing increasing uncertainty for countries’ economic growth and carbon emission control has become a global consensus. The People’s Bank of China introduced a green finance policy (GFP) in the third quarter of 2017, which incorporates green finance into the Macro-Prudential Assessment (MPA) framework it has developed to assess commercial banks. Using the difference-in-differences (DID) methodology and data from 2440 Chinese A-share listed enterprises, we find that companies with higher ESG ratings experienced a significant decrease in their costs of debt (Cod) after the promulgation of this policy. Moreover, among the specific dimensions of ESG scores, Environmental scores have a more significant impact on corporates’ Cod compared with those of Social and Governance. Further analysis also shows that the Cod of firms that are big-sized, state-owned, or from non-heavily-polluting industries decrease more significantly after the proposal of this GFP. These findings are relevant for subsequent revisions of the GFP and inspiring for industrial transformation and green finance system in both China and the world.

## 1. Introduction

Climate change leads to an abnormal increase in the average global temperature, which has negative impacts on the economic growth across countries (Kahn et al., 2021). These facts not only render governments lay more emphasis on carbon emission control and sustainable development, but also shift the investing concept of financial institutions and individual investors, in which case green finance and environmental, social and governance (ESG) are under quick development. This also mirrors a huge business opportunity since the global green finance market must invest 1.6 trillion to 3.8 trillion dollars annually based on the calculation of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), assuming that the carbon neutralization can be realized at 2050.

As one of the biggest carbon emission country, China has set a target of realizing ‘Carbon Peak’ and ‘Carbon Neutralization’ in 2030 and 2060, respectively. Thus, the Chinese government has launched several policies to develop the green finance. As early as 2007, the People’s Bank of China (PBC) and China Banking Regulatory Commission (CBRC) published ‘Opinions on Implementing Environmental Protection Policies and Regulations to Prevent Credit Risks’, which is the first green finance policy (GFP) in China. Besides, there are also some policies concerning green bonds, green credit, and ESG disclosure. Specifically, ‘China’s Monetary Policy Report’ for the fourth quarter of 2017 mentioned that the PBC has added green finance to the Macro-Prudential Assessment (MPA)’s ‘Implementation of Credit Policy’ since the third quarter of 2017, for the purpose of evaluating commercial bank’s service level (mainly green debt and green bonds) of green finance. This is the first GFP that directly require commercial banks to consider firms’ green performance in China and get much attention.

To test the efficiency of this GFP and figure out the influencing factor in determining the intensity of the GFP’s impact on a company, this paper views the GFP proposed by the PBC in 2017 as a quasi-natural experiment and uses the generalized difference-in-difference (DID) methodology to evaluate its influence on the Chinese listed companies’ costs of debt (Cod). We next find that the scores of Environmental (E) has more impacts on companies’ Cod than Social (S) and Governance (G). Our results also indicate that the Cod of companies with high ESG ratings decreased significantly after the proposal of the policy, and companies which are big-size, state-owned or come from the non-polluting industry are affected more significantly by this policy. In empirical tests, we employ data from Chinese A-share listed companies over the 2015–2020 period, which includes 2440 listed companies after excluding enterprises from the financial industry and those marked as ST or *ST by the stock exchange. We also exclude enterprises listed later than 2015 to ensure that all samples have a same data period. These data were sourced from public databases including Wind, CSMAR, and Huazheng ESG.

To ensure the statistical stability and economic significance of our findings, we conduct a series of robustness tests, including parallel trend test, Propensity Score Method (PSM), and the placebo test, which are indispensable when using the DID methodology. We also employ a dynamic ESG rating that can range from 1 to 9 and change with time to replace the binary variable *Treat* in the basic model to mitigate the potential endogenous problem due to the selecting bias of the ESG rating threshold. Moreover, the gauge of Cod$^1$ and the data source of ESG ratings$^2$ are replaced to prevent the data itself from influencing the empirical results. Finally, we further control the city × year fixed effects and industry × year effects to capture the unobservable heterogeneities that jointly changing with time, industry, and city. Results of all these robustness tests confirm our findings are both statistically significant and economically enlightened.

The decrease in the Cod of high ESG ratings companies may be attributed to two perspectives, which are ‘Signaling Theory’ and reputation improvement. For one thing, high ESG rating is a signal of firms’ positive externalities in contrast with other competitors and low bankruptcy risks. Companies have no incentive to consider about ESG ratings if their business are under extreme poor conduction, which means that companies with high ESG ratings tend to have lower bankruptcy risks (Atif & Ali, 2021). However, it is worth noting that high ESG rating cannot be interpreted as a signal of a firm’s high financial performance because evidence show that there is no certain relationship between them (Aouadi & Marsat, 2018). For another, high ESG rating will polish the firm’s image through the reputational approach. Financial institutions and other investors lay much more emphasis on the corporate’s non-financial information nowadays, so high ESG rating can make the firm be viewed as a responsible social participant and better its public reputation. In conclusion, with positive externalities, lower bankruptcy risks, and better public reputation, commercial banks and other investors are reasonable to offer a lower Cod for companies with high ESG ratings.

The result also shows an increasing trend in the effect of lowering Cod, which reveals that it takes some time for this policy to transmit to the market’s actual operation. Fig. 1 directly shows the difference of Cod between high and low ESG rating companies during 2015–2020, which reveals that the difference has been widening since 2017 and the gap is even larger after 2018.

![Fig. 1. Difference of Cod between high and low ESG rating companies (%).](https://placeholder-url.com/fig1.png)

It is worth noting that the firm’s ESG rating functions as the grouping basis in our DID model on the ground that the PBC’s green finance policy did not have predetermined targeted enterprises or financial institutions, and ESG rating can reflect the strength of a company’s exposure to the policy. Hence, after the promulgation of this policy, with commercial banks’ attention on the MPA, they will lay more emphasis on companies’ ESG ratings, making it possible for companies with higher ESG ratings to be affected more greatly by the GFP. This method is called ‘Generalized DID’, which is commonly used by some influential literatures when a policy affects all the research subjects by using an indicator to characterize the extent to which the firm is affected by the policy (Bai & Jia, 2016; Campello & Larrain, 2016; Vig, 2013). In our benchmark regression, to make it easier to understand, we put companies with ESG ratings higher than the average level into the treat group, but this may raise endogenous concerns as selecting bias may exist in choosing the average level as the ‘threshold’, so we allow every ESG rating to be an independent group in the robustness tests and the results are consistent with the benchmark regression.

Green credit has long been a sector that gains great attention from policymakers in China, but relevant policies before the PBC’s green finance policy promulgated in 2017 were not effectively executed by financial institutions due to the lack of detailed assessment and evaluation criteria, thus we choose this GFP as the exogenous shock. To be more specific, there did exist some earlier green credit policies such as ‘Green Credit Guidelines’ (Guideline) enacted in 2012, but it was very difficult for financial institutions to judge and prove which firm is ‘greener’ due to the missing ESG rating data that can cover most Chinese listed companies before 2015,$^3$ when other data that can reflect the green performance of firms are hardly accessible as well. Besides, supervisions mentioned in these previous policies are more of a policy spirit that stays on paper without substantive and concrete means of implementation. For example, although the Guideline mentions that financial institutions and regulators should evaluate and report the work of green credit, it still relies more on the institutions’ self-motivation and did not specify the assessment tools, thus lack the coercive force, while the GFP in 2017 clearly mentioned that the green finance is incorporated into the ‘credit policy implementation’ of the MPA. Moreover, if these previous green credit policies really have effective influences on enterprises, the Cod of high ESG rating companies should decrease earlier than 2017, but parallel trend test prove that there are no significant differences between high ESG rating companies and others before the promulgation of this GFP, which also indicates previous green credit policies cannot be viewed as exogenous shocks in our research. So, it is safe to conclude that the GFP proposed in 2017 is the first green policy that directly affects the credit and bond pricing decision and is exogenous for both financial institutions and Chinese A-share listed companies.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized into the following three aspects. First, this paper evaluates the GFP’s influence on all A-share listed companies early and employs ESG rating as the grouping basis for the first time. Other scholars have made a lot of achievements on some other green policies’ impacts on firms, for example, some researches argue that green credit policy will lead to the decrease of financing scale and increase of debt financing cost for heavy-polluting enterprises (Li, Cui, & Zheng, 2022) and an article proves that corporate environmental policy can boost a firm’s reputation and risk-adjusted profitability who engages in green product innovation (Gangi et al., 2020). Most of these studies only focus on the heavy-polluting enterprises and ignore the function of ESG rating, but we step further by considering that the ESG rating can reflect the intensity of the green finance policy’s impact on a company.

Second, we add to the literature in ESG and green finance. For instance, the former research shows that the ESG disclosure level is positively associated with the firm value (Feng & Wu, 2021; Li, Gong, Zhang, & Koh, 2018), and another literature also finds that ESG activities reveal a nonnegative association with corporate efficiency and return on assets (Xie et al., 2019). This paper proposes a new mechanism: Cod of companies which have a relatively high ESG rating will drop significantly as a result of the green finance policy’s impact. This finding may expand the existing theories and is applicable to capital markets around the world.

Third, our heterogeneity analysis of firms based on three different aspects illustrates that this GFP is not perfect and offer multidimensional suggestions for the later green finance policies, which are useful for both regulators and investors. We discuss the heterogeneity within the GFP’s impact on Chinese listed companies from firm’s size, ownership, and industry. Our results indicate that the existing green finance policy does not have significant impacts on enterprises that are medium and small-size, in the polluting industry or private-owned. This phenomenon is common in the traditional credit market and is not receiving enough attention currently. However, these ‘discriminations’ damage the market efficiency in both short-term and long-term, thus need to be corrected. If later policies can focus more on these ‘discriminated’ companies, then the subjects involved in the green development of China’s industry will be more diversified, in which case improve the quality of low-carbon industry transformation and the efficiency of GFPs in China.

The remainder of this article is organized in the following way. Section 2 puts forward the theoretical hypotheses. Section 4 reports and discusses the results of benchmark regression and robustness tests. Heterogeneity analysis is conducted in the Section 5. Section 6 concludes the research and offers policy implications.

## 2. Theoretical hypothesis

Previous literature have found that the correlation between firms’ Cod and their ESG performances is of great significance. The firms with relatively high ESG scores usually have a lower short-term and long-term Cod than those with relatively low ESG scores (Ratajczak & Mikołajewicz, 2021), while the firms’ ESG disclosure quality also has an impact on this phenomenon (Eliwa et al., 2021). We believe that this cost decrease is also efficient for China’s entities since the central bank of China had included green finance in the Macro-Prudential Assessment (MPA). The assessment of the green finance implementation will impose pressure on the commercial banks to issue green loans, making them take more proactive lending strategies and relax the requirement for the issuers. This is because a commercial bank with better performance in the MPA can have a lower deposit-reserve ratio prescribed by the central bank. With lower cost, longer maturity and larger loan balance, the green loans are more efficient in reducing the target firms’ Cod than conventional loans.

Green loans are proved to be a better choice for qualified enterprises in China to decrease their Cod, and we have reasons to believe that the firms with higher ESG performance are more available to obtain the green loans. ESG scoring criteria are very similar to those for green loans. For example, the ‘Green Credit Guidelines’ imposed by the CBRC in 2012 have made requirements for commercial banks, stipulating the standards of emissions limit, pollution treatment and ecological protection that a qualified enterprise should meet when they obtain the green loans. These assessment indicators have already been included in the ESG evaluation, influencing the firms’ final ESG scores. So, a higher ESG score (especially the environmental score) a firm can achieve, the easier they can obtain green loans.

The information asymmetry is also a critical factor when commercial banks issue green loans. Lending institutions will incorporate firms’ ESG information in their lending decision to evaluate two types of risks: default risk and reputation risk (Weber et al., 2014). There does exist information asymmetry in the accessing process due to the firms’ discourse and credibility. However, the ESG evaluation can solve this problem since the high discourse quality and credibility have already been secured when the firms are given high ESG scores by ESG rating agencies. The reduction of the information asymmetry contributes to the decrease of the difficulties when these firms are trying to obtain green loans.

In the bond market, these enterprises with high ESG scores also gain advantages. It has been confirmed that the high performance of ESG/CSR is associated with lower risk premium, higher bonds rating and lower corporate bond yield spreads (Oikonomou et al., 2014). Firms that perform well in the ESG evaluation can usually have lower Cod when issuing green bonds since investors have more confidence and interest in these high-quality enterprises.

Given our research focus on China, it is important to elaborate on why the policies of the central bank directly influence the decisions of financial institutions. As part of the module “Credit Policy Implementation,” the central bank has incorporated an evaluation of financial institutions’ adoption of “green finance.” The results of this policy will have a significant impact on financial institutions, with both potential rewards and penalties. For example, the central bank’s monetary structural tools, such as policy low-interest loans and innovative business pilots, will prioritize support for the top-ranking commercial banks. However, if the performance of these banks is poor, the central bank may even suspend some of their business operations and exercise its authority. In 2017, for instance, the Bank of Nanjing and the Bank of Ningbo were penalized. Therefore, the green financial policy of the central bank can have a significant impact on the credit decision-making behavior of financial institutions. This is particularly true when financial institutions consider reaching green financial assessment indicators and use the enterprise ESG rating as an important basis for credit decision-making. Financial institutions may also provide “preferential treatment” to enterprises with higher ESG ratings. Ultimately, this leads to a reduction in the cost of debt (Cod). Considering all we have mentioned above, we first hypothesize that:

**H1.** Green finance policy can reduce the Cod of ESG high-rated enterprises, and the higher the ESG rating, the more obvious the reduction effect.

When we further explore this phenomenon in each specific enterprise, we believe that this reduction of a firm’s Cod is also associated with its scale. At present, the high Cod faced by small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) is a challenge across the world, especially in transitioning economies and developing countries (Brown et al., 2012; Cheng et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2020), which is also known as the “Macmillan gap”. Through preliminary analysis of samples, we found that the correlation between firms’ ESG scores and their scale is not significant. This means that scale discrimination mainly occurs in the financing process rather than the rating process.

The financial influence caused by the scale discrimination can be divided into both the demand side and supply side of the currencies. On the supply side, the information asymmetry caused by the low transparency in financial condition, company operation and cash flow disadvantages SMEs when obtaining loans or issuing bonds (Stiglitz & Weiss, 1981), while the lack of bank accredited guarantees and high uncertainty for SMEs also exacerbated this situation (Yu & Fu, 2021). It can be convinced that there does exist scale discrimination in the credit market, making the lending institution and bond investors prefer large enterprises.

On the demand side, SMEs are more likely to seek financial support from trade credit when relatively inexpensive bank loans are unavailable (Petersen & Rajan, 1994; Petersen & Rajan, 1997). Studies show that trade credit accounts for 25% of the average firm’s total debt liabilities in their sample of >3500 firms across 34 countries from 1990 to 2011 (Levine et al., 2018), taking a huge proportion of the debt balance of the SMEs. Besides, when the managers of private-owned enterprises and SMEs believe that they can’t obtain a bank loan with an approximate interest rate and amount, they will slow down their expansion, adopt conservative financing strategies, reduce financing demand, or turn to internal financing.

Under the scale discrimination, the SMEs with high ESG scores still hard to benefit from the green credit due to the restriction from the lending institution, investors, and the firms themselves. The lack of loan balance, dependence on trade credit, and conservative financing strategy greatly reduced the role of green finance policy. That’s the reason we have to consider the firms’ scales as an important factor and thus raise our second hypothesis:

**H2a.** The impacts of the GFP on the Cod of large enterprises are more significant.

Another factor we must focus on is the firms’ ownership. Supported by governmental organizations and quasi-governmental organizations, state-owned enterprises can also take advantage when obtaining loans just as the enterprises with large scale do. This priority can be concluded as ownership discrimination.

Studies have suggested that high state ownership decreases the cost of debt, as the government’s presence could be viewed by debt-holders as an assurance of repayment and protection against bankruptcy (Borisova & Megginson, 2011). If a company is government-owned, then bondholders will feel secure about getting their money back due to the implicit government guarantee. Politically connected firms are more likely to be the beneficiaries of a government bailout than non-connected firms (Faccio et al., 2006). Information asymmetry between a 100% owner and a prospective buyer of a company could be mitigated by the owner retaining some stake in the company, signaling a belief in its quality and future (Leland & Pyle, 1977).

Accordingly, the correlation between ownership and enterprises’ ESG scores is not sufficient in our preliminary analysis of samples. Since the state-own enterprises take advantage as the large-scale enterprises do when obtaining loans or issuing debts. The mechanisms for the scale discrimination we mentioned are also sufficient when we discuss about the firms’ ownership. Based on this, we third hypothesize that:

**H2b.** The impacts of the GFP on the Cod of state-owned enterprises are more significant.

According to the definition of ESG indicators, three evaluating segments are involved in the rating process, namely the Environmental Protection(E), Social Responsibility(S), and Governance (G). To what extent these three indicators can be identified by the lending institution and investors, affecting their lending, or investing decision, is worth further studies. Traditionally, the achievements of environmental protection are easier to be recognized by individuals and institutions, because the exact benefits of the environmental protection policies can be observed in the enterprises’ Financial and operational situation. These benefits can increase the enterprises’ development potential, reduce their regulation restriction, and make them more stable when facing external shocks. These traits just meet the investor’s criteria for high-quality enterprises, making non-polluting firms more competitive when obtaining loans and issuing bonds. This means at the same level of ESG total score, firms higher in the “E” scores are preferred by the financial institutions and investors over those higher in the “S” and “G” scores.

We have assumed that a firm with a higher total ESG score can have a more obvious reduction effect on its Cod under the green policy. However, it is necessary for us to do further research when the pollution firms and non-pollution firms are given the same total scores. We believe that a pollution enterprise (lower score in Environmental Protection, but higher scores in Social Responsibility and Governance) has a worse Cod reduction effect than a non-pollution enterprise (high score in “E”, but lower scores in “S” and “G”), even they are given the same total ESG scores. Based on this, our fourth hypothesis is that:

**H2c.** The impacts of the GFP on the Cod of enterprises from non-heavily-polluting industries are more significant. The overall theoretical structure is shown in Fig. 2.

## 3. Empirical design

### 3.1. Data

To investigate the influence of China’s GFP on the Cod of enterprises, this paper employs data from A-share listed companies over the 2015–2020 period, which includes 2440 listed companies and 13,041 observations. We exclude enterprises from the financial industry and those marked as ST or ST* because these companies’ financial data tend to be abnormal and interfere with the empirical results. Besides, for the purpose of clearly observing the impact of the GFP, we also exclude enterprises listed later than 2015 to ensure that all samples have the same data period.

The main sources of data are public databases, including Huazheng ESG database, Wind, and CSMAR. To be more specific, ESG ratings come from the Huazheng ESG database, financial data used as control variables come from Wind, and Cod come from CSMAR. We use Huazheng ESG database in the benchmark regression because of its advantages in long history and high coverage for A-Share companies in China over other databases, so it is widely used when researching Chinese listed companies. There are some other agencies carry out ESG rating business in China, such as SynTao Green Finance, Bloomberg, and Wind. However, they all have shortcomings in contrast with Huazheng. For example, SynTao Green Finance only covers about 300 biggest firms, Wind did not have ESG ratings data before 2017, and Bloomberg can only cover fewer than half of the companies that Huazheng can (especially some small and medium-sized listed enterprises), thus make Huazheng ESG database the best option when we extract ESG ratings data. To relieve the possible concern on the variations between different agencies’ internal ratings standards, we also employ ESG ratings from Bloomberg database to replace the data used in our benchmark regression.

To mitigate the influence of outliers on the regression, all continuous variables in this paper are winsorized at the 1% and 99% level.

### 3.2. Model and variables

‘China’s Monetary Policy Report’ for the fourth quarter of 2017 mentioned that the PBC has added green finance to the Macro-Prudential Assessment (MPA)’s ‘Implementation of Credit Policy’ since the third quarter of 2017. The target of this policy is to evaluate financial institution’s service level of green finance (Chen, 2020) and this GFP is an exogenous shock for both enterprises and commercial banks. A company’s ESG rating is a comprehensive index and highly related to the company’s overall business model, which changes seldom within a limited period for most of the companies. Therefore, we use the ESG rating of the company in 2017 (the year that the GFP proposed) as the basis for comparing its ESG performance with the average level. We view this GFP proposed by the PBC as a quasi-experiment and evaluate its efficiency through a difference-in-differences (DID) methodology. The model we estimate is set as follows:

$$Cod_{i,t} = \beta Post_t \times Treat_i + \gamma X_{Group} + \mu_t + e_i + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{1}$$

In this model, $i$ and $t$ stand for the firm and time series respectively. Our samples cover a period from 2015 to 2020, among which the annual samples are fixed on 31 December (the date of the data reported in the annual report). Cod represents the listed company’s ratio of corporate interest expense to the sum of short-term debt and long-term debt. The bigger the number of Cod means the higher the average cost of the firm’s external debt financing. Treat is a dummy variable that decides whether a listed company is affected by the GFP. In our model, if a firm’s ESG rating is above average, then Treat = 1; otherwise, Treat = 0. The rationale behind this method of grouping is that companies with above-average ESG ratings are more profoundly impacted by policy because it is the financial institution that enforces the policy, and the ratings serve as a subjective basis for determining “green” practices. Specifically, we classify the firms’ ESG rating into 9 levels (from 1 to 9) and result shows that the mean of the ESG ratings of the listed companies is 6.4 in 2017. Thus, if a firm’s ESG rating is equal or higher than 7, then Treat = 1, and if not, Treat = 0. Based on the ratings from Huazheng ESG database, the corresponding relation between the ESG rating and number are illustrated in the Table 1.

Post is the dummy variable decides whether the enterprises can be affected by the GFP. Considering that the GFP was proposed at the end of 2017, so if the date of the data is in 2018 and later, Post = 1, otherwise Post = 0. The variable $Treat \times Post$ means the net processing effect of difference-in-differences methodology. We want to further demonstrate the selection of the policy shock time. On the one hand, the Green Finance Policy (GFP) proposed in 2017 is the first policy in China that quantitatively assesses financial institutions in terms of their environmental impact. This policy serves as the foundation for subsequent policies introduced in 2018 and Chen, 2020. The subsequent policies add and disclose the specific indexes and weights of the quantitative assessment (2018) and some fine-tuning Chen (2020), which essentially continue and refine the 2017 policy. On the other hand, we also consider shocks in fictional earlier time periods during robustness tests.

If the GFP mentioned before is effective, the regression coefficient $\beta$ of $Post \times Treat$ will be significantly negative, which implies that the reduction of Cod in the firms with better ESG performance is more significant than the ones with worse ESG performance. $\mu_t$ represents time fixed effects and $e_i$ represents individual (company) fixed effects.

We also control several factors that are universally thought to have influences on companies’ Cod, which are abbreviated as $X_{Group}$ in our model. To lower the probability of defaults, leverage and liquidity are the two major perspectives that investors concern when they lend money to a firm, thus factors such as liability-to-asset ratio (Valta, 2012) and cash-to-asset ratio (Pittman & Fortin, 2004), which may impact a company’s Cod. Specifically, Tobin Q also plays an important role in the financial institutions’ debt pricing decisions (Lin et al., 2011). Besides, existing literature indicates that revenue and the attribute of a company are some other factors that are closely connected with Chinese companies’ Cod, including ROE, ownership, age, size, and asset turnover rate (Borisova et al., 2015; Borisova & Megginson, 2011; Ni & Yin, 2018). These control variables cover profitability, solvency, liquidity, and capital structure of an enterprise, which are the main influence factors of corporate’s cost of debt, and mitigate the problem of a biased coefficient estimation of the $\beta$. Descriptive statistics are presented in the Table 2 and Appendix A describes all the variable symbols and how they are constructed in this paper.

## 4. Empirical results

### 4.1. Benchmark regression

We present our empirical results of model (1) in this section to evaluate the efficiency of the GFP proposed by China’s central bank, in which we use the difference-in-differences (DID) model with firm-level data. Table 3 reports the results of the benchmark regression and our emphasis is on the coefficient of the key explanatory variable $Post \times Treat$ in the model. Column (1) of Table 3 shows that the PBC’s policy which brought green finance into ‘The Implementation of Credit Policy’ of the MPA framework reduces Cod of enterprises with higher ESG ratings. The coefficient is -0.6550 and significant at a 1% level. In column (2), we introduce the control variables that are thought to have significant impacts on firms’ costs of debt, and the coefficient is -0.6405, which is also significant at 1% level. The economic significance of this coefficient is that, after the promulgation of the GFP, companies with higher ESG ratings are compensated for their positive externalities and better public reputation, in which their Cod has dropped 0.6405% more than other companies. This result provides support for our **Hypothesis H1**.

Moreover, coefficients of control variables also have economic significance and are mostly as predicted. For one thing, the coefficients of the variable ROE and Leverage are significantly negative at 1% level, which is readily comprehensible and indicate a negative correlation between a company’s Cod and its profitability and leverage. For another, the coefficients of the variable Age and TATR are significantly positive, which reveal a positive correlation between the Cod and these two variables. They can be explained by the facts that high-tech and non-heavily-polluting companies tend to be younger in China, and financial institutions are reluctant to allocate cheap credit resources for companies that have a very high assets turnover rate due to their low growth potential in the long-term. Finally, these coefficients of control variables are consistent with previous literature related to the enterprises’ Cod (Dang et al., 2022; Shailer & Wang, 2015).

Apart from the empirical level, the essence of ESG rating in this research serves as a basis for grouping in the GFP. This suggests that it is acceptable for certain factors to influence the rating. Additionally, Deng et al. (2023) confirms that there is no significant reverse causality between ESG ratings and the financial performance or productivity of firms.

#### 4.2.4. Other robustness tests

**4.2.4.1. Dynamic ESG ratings.** In the benchmark regression, we set companies with ESG ratings higher than the average level in 2017 as the samples in our treat group. This may cause a concern about the selecting bias in choosing the average value as the ‘threshold’. To handle this concern, we allow ESG rating to change with time and every rating is viewed as a group, making it possible for us to mitigating the endogenous concern and test whether the higher the ESG rating, the lower the Cod after the promulgation of this GFP at the same time. Moreover, rendering the ESG rating change with time for every individual firm is more in line with the practical situation because a firm can have different ESG ratings in different years. Results reported in the column (1) of Table 7 show that the coefficient of the interaction term is -0.1063 and significant at 1% level, which is consistent with the benchmark regression. It also confirms the selection of ESG rating threshold does not generate serious endogenous problems. The economic significance of this coefficient is that, after the promulgation of the GFP, one unit increase in a company’s ESG rating will lead to 0.1063% decrease in its Cod on average.

**4.2.4.2. Alternative ESG rating database.** We have argued about the rationality of using Huazheng ESG database as the source of our ESG rating data, but possible differences in rating criteria between agencies could lead to a potential endogenous concern that using rating data from different agencies could cause different conclusions. ## 5. Further study

### 5.1. Does E matter more than S and G?$^4$

In Section 2, we have discussed about why “E” scores may be more important for companies than “S” or “G” from a theoretical perspective. In these three dimensions, environmental protection (E) tends to create the strongest positive externalities for the society, thus it will also be compensated by the market in the price of debt financing and lower the Cod. To confirm this mechanism in an empirical approach, we carry on a regression test, in which we employ the detailed ESG scores data from Bloomberg database. These detailed ESG scores are divided into three dimensions which are Environmental (E), Social (S), and Governance (G), thus allow us to research on which dimension’s score has more impacts on the firm’s Cod by estimating the following three DID models.$^5$

$$Cod_{i,t} = \beta Post_t \times Environmental_{i,t} + \gamma X_{Group} + \mu_t + e_i + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{3}$$
$$Cod_{i,t} = \beta Post_t \times Social_{i,t} + \gamma X_{Group} + \mu_t + e_i + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{4}$$
$$Cod_{i,t} = \beta Post_t \times Governance_{i,t} + \gamma X_{Group} + \mu_t + e_i + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{5}$$

Take model (3) for example, consistent with our method in ‘Dynamic ESG rating’, we set each environmental score as a treat group and use the DID methodology in the regression. If the increase of Environmental (E) score means a higher influence intensity by the GFP, the coefficient of the interaction term $Post \times Environmental$ should be significantly negative. For models (4) and (5), the logic is approximately the same as for model (3). Results are reported in the column (1) to (3) in Table 8. It is clear that the coefficient of interaction term in column (1) is -0.025 and significant at 5% level, while coefficients in both column (2) and (3) are not significant, which indicates that the scores of companies’ Environmental (E) has more impacts than Social (S) and Governance (G) on firm’s Cod after the promulgation of this GFP.

### 5.2. GFP’s heterogeneities on size, ownership, and industry

‘Size discrimination’ and ‘Ownership discrimination’ are often seen in Chinese credit market, in which companies with bigger asset size or state-owned tend to be benefited more by the policy, thus have access to more support from PBC’s financial instruments and bank lending than others (Luo & Ying, 2014; Xu et al., 2022). Besides, the polluting nature of the industry that the firm belongs to is also a major concern for financial institutions in recent years because of several restrictive policies on traditional ‘two-high’ enterprises and guidance on developing green finance, in which case financial institutions become increasingly cautious on allocating capital for heavily-polluting industries, thus render the companies from non-heavily-polluting industries gain advantages over others, and we call this phenomenon ‘Industry discrimination’. These discriminations greatly damage the market efficiency, and make some small, private, and make heavily-polluting companies fall into financial dilemmas, which undermines the plurality of economic participants and urgently need to be solved. Based on these facts and analyses, we wonder whether the above-mentioned “discrimination” still exists in the process of the impacts of this GFP on the enterprises’ Cod, and provide realistic empirical results and insights for policy authorities, enterprises, and financial institutions.

#### 5.2.1. Polluting nature of firm’s industry

To test our **Hypothesis H2a**, we divide the industries of firms into heavily-polluting industries and other industries based on the ‘Environmental Information Disclosure Guidelines for Listed Companies’ proposed by the China Securities Regulatory Commission and conduct group regression. Columns (1) to (2) of Table 9 show that the coefficient of $Post \times Treat$ in non-heavily-polluting industries’ group is -0.9550 and significant at 1% level, while that in heavily-polluting industries’ group is not significant. Interpreting from an economic perspective, with ESG ratings higher than the average level, companies in non-heavily-polluting industries have an average 0.9550% decrease in Cod after the promulgation of the GFP, but firms in heavily-polluting industries do not, which means the GFP has more impacts on the firms from non-heavily-pollution industries. These results prove that our **Hypothesis H2a** holds, which means the ‘Industry discrimination’ does exist in the execution of this GFP.

#### 5.2.2. Asset size

The results presented in columns (3) to (4) of Table 9 provide support for our **Hypothesis H2b**, and prove that the ‘Size discrimination’ still exists in this GFP. Using the logarithm of total assets as a proxy for firms’ size, we divide our samples’ size into two groups based on the mean of firms’ size. Enterprises with size higher than or equal to the average value are classified as big-size firms, while those below the mean value are grouped as other firms. Columns 3 to 4 of Table 9 show that the coefficient of the interaction term in the big-size group is -0.9796 and significant at a 1% level, while the coefficient of coefficient in the other group is not significant. These results indicate that the GFP has more influences on Cod of large-scale firms and verify that the ‘size discrimination’ in traditional credit market is still applicable to the GFP.

#### 5.2.3. Ownership

Statistics in columns (5) to (6) of Table 9 support our **Hypothesis H2c**, in which the Cod of state-owned enterprises (SOE) drop more significantly after the promulgation of PBC’s green finance policy, while other firms do not have a significant change. To be more specific, the coefficient of the interaction term in the SOE group is -1.0582 and significant at 5% level, while the coefficient of other companies is not significant. Hence, it is reasonable to conclude that there still exists ‘ownership discrimination’ when financial institutions execute the GFP. These findings provide evidence for the existence of ‘Ownership discrimination’ in this GFP.

## 6. Conclusions and policy implications

By using the DID methodology, we find evidence of the green finance policy proposed by the PBC in 2017 affecting enterprises’ Cod significantly. Companies with ESG ratings higher than the average is associated with significantly lower Cod after the promulgation of the GFP. This means that the central bank’s GFP affects more on high-ESG-rated firms. Specifically, a unit increase in the company’s ESG rating leads to 0.1063% decrease in its Cod. We believe that the high ESG rating is a signal of firms’ positive externalities in contrast with other competitors and low bankruptcy risks, and it also improves firms’ public reputations, which jointly render the GFP decrease the Cod of high ESG rating companies. We also conduct a series of robustness tests on the parallel trend hypothesis, PSM-DID methodology, more fixed effects, and some other tests to ensure the economic significance of our benchmark regression.

These findings suggest that the Green Finance Policy (GFP) is generally effective in reducing the Cost of Debt (Cod) for companies with good ESG performance. Overall, the People’s Bank of China (PBC) proposed a green finance policy in 2017. The policy requires financial institutions to consider the ESG rating of companies when making credit decisions. Companies with better ESG ratings not only comply with the policy requirements, but also face lower reputation risks, administrative penalties, and stock price collapse risks. Thus, companies with higher ESG ratings receive “preferential treatment” from financial institutions and are required to pay lower premiums for risk, which gives these companies access to lower Cod.

However, results of heterogeneities analysis reveal that there still exists some ‘discriminations’ in the execution of this GFP, including ‘size discrimination’, ‘ownership discrimination’, and ‘industry discrimination’, in which the Cod of companies that are big-sized, state-owned, or from the non-heavily-polluting industries drop more significantly than other companies. As one may expect, these discriminations are damaging the market efficiency and need to be solved. Moreover, evidence indicate that scores of Environmental (E) play a more important role than Social (S) and Governance (G) in the process of the impacts of this GFP on the enterprises’ Cod, which means the ability of Chinese financial institutions to use ESG scores is still low at this stage.

Our findings on the GFP’s influence on high ESG rating companies reveal an important fact that the ESG rating may reflects the intensity of a company’s influence by the following GFPs, and it is highly possible that the ESG rating will become one of the major determinants in financial institutions’ debt pricing, thus affects the corporates’ financial constraints.

We also provide some realistic suggestions for policy authorities, enterprises, and financial institutions based on our empirical results. Firstly, the PBC should enhance the execution of the GFP by increasing the weight of green finance in MPA to encourage firms engage in the improvements of their ESG performance, which can support the green transformation plans made by the Chinese government. Secondly, considering the heterogeneities we have discussed, future policies are supposed to lay more emphasis on guiding financial institutions to compensate for the companies with high ESG ratings but are small-sized, private-owned or from the heavily-polluting industries in order to mitigate discriminations in the credit market. Specifically, it is of great importance for financial institutions to allocate more capital or lower the Cod for traditional heavily-polluting industries because the Chinese government is making great efforts to realize the ‘transformation finance’. Transformation finance is the concept of financial market’s support for guiding traditional heavily-polluting industries to participate in the technology improvements and pollution control, which is meaningful due to the heavily-polluting industries’ larger marginal benefits for both the society and environment than the non-heavily-polluting industries.$^6$ Besides, keeping pace with the authorities also gives financial institutions more access to liquidity and policy tools support from the PBC. Finally, companies should concentrate on improving their environmental performance (scores) such as planting trees, controlling pollution, and water protection to lower their Cod more effectively because of investors’ highest emphasis on scores of E in ESG recently.
