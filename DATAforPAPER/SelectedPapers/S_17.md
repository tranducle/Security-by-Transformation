### ABSTRACT

Our study aims to examine the effect of sustainability practices, measured by sustainability performance and disclosure, on the cost of debt of ASEAN firms (in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand). There is a significant research gap where there is a scarcity of literature examining the impact of sustainability practices on the cost of debt, especially in Southeast Asia, and much of the existing literature still has conflicting results. We employed panel data analysis using generalized least square random effects regression, based on a sample of 1,443 observations from 2004 to 2019. The analysis reveals mixed results that an ASEAN firm’s sustainability practices lowers its cost of debt. Albeit debt holders incorporate sustainability practices information (measured by disclosure) of ASEAN firms in their credit evaluation, debtholders do not find sustainability practices (measured by performance) to be significant. This is possibly due to some crucial information that are apparent in sustainability disclosure but not in sustainability performance scores. Moreover, the interaction between sustainability performance and sustainability disclosure shows complimentary relationship between the two. Lastly, by incorporating country sustainability characteristics, industry sensitivity, and corporate governance as moderating variables, we find the possibility of a prevailing substitute relationship between firm-level and country-level sustainability practices and immense expectations imposed by stakeholders to firms with environmentally harmful practices.

## 1. Introduction

Sustainability$^1$ practice is on a global rise, becoming a critical business issue and a strategic concern driven by market forces. The global economic growth seen in recent decades is continuously compounded by inertia stemming from inadequate governance and unsustainable business practices, which results in ecological and societal impacts, such as environmental degradation, scarcity of resources, the rise of greenhouse gas emissions, social inequality, and disparities that jeopardize people’s quality of life (WBCSD, 2010). ASEAN (Association of South East Asian Nations)—a regional intergovernmental organization comprises of ten countries in Southeast Asia: Brunei Darussalam, Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam (ASEAN, 2020)—is no different. Being one of the biggest emerging economies, the economic outlook of ASEAN is prosperous when judged by the rapid economic growth but less so when pursued at the expense of environmental capital and social capital that ultimately hinder future development (ASEAN, 2019). Exacerbated by climate change in recent years, the adverse effects of unsustainable practices and consumptions prompt the region’s increasing vulnerability to extreme weather events that further precipitate economic and social havoc (ADB, 2017; ASEAN, 2019). As a response to stakeholders’ pressures, several sustainability initiatives are established to shed light on sustainable practices within the region, and there is a rise of companies’ concerns over sustainability issues (ASEAN CSR Network, 2018). In summary, sustainability practices will continue to gain momentum in ASEAN.

Simultaneously, the corporate debt market continues to gain significance in global and ASEAN economies. A report by OECD (2020) documents an extended growth in corporate bond borrowing since 2008, amounting to an average annual corporate bond issuance of USD1.8 trillion globally—double the annual average of 2000–2007. In the ASEAN region, the size of the local currency bond market also continues to grow, with 2.3% quarterly and 9.9% annual growth recorded in 2019 (ADB, 2019). The outstanding size of local currency bonds among member countries of ASEAN even totaled to USD1.5 billion in the same year (ADB, 2019).

The aforementioned rise of sustainability issues has motivated a considerable amount of research on the rationale behind sustainability practice and its financial consequences. Yet, despite the ample size of the corporate debt market and its global significance (OECD, 2020), a scarcity of studies examining the impact of sustainability practices on the cost of debt is still apparent (Hoepner et al., 2016), primarily studies conducted in ASEAN market. Not only is this particular topic scarce, the idea that debtholders reward companies for their sustainability practices in terms of lowering their cost of debt is still a controversial issue in academia. Several scholars (Crifo et al., 2017; Eliwa et al., 2019; Fonseka et al., 2019; Ge and Liu, 2015) argues that such practices are negatively related with the cost of debt, yet other scholars (Erragragui, 2018; Hoepner et al., 2016) find the relationship to be non-negative or even insignificant. It is why, as further demonstrated in our study’s perusal of related research, the empirical literature on sustainability practices and the cost of debt is still needed.

Drawing on four frameworks (stakeholder theory, legitimacy theory, institutional theory, and agency theory), our research empirically examines the impact of firms’ sustainability practices on their cost of debt, using a sample of listed firms in ASEAN countries. Our study aim to extend the existing literature in several ways. Firstly, we focus on the association between sustainability practices and the cost of debt—which still provide conflicting results. Prior literature focuses mostly on either sustainability performance or sustainability disclosure, and there is still a limited number of prior studies that examine both sustainability performance and sustainability disclosure, as measures of sustainability practices, to the cost of debt (Eliwa et al., 2019). Sustainability performance reflects a firm’s commitment to sustainability strategy and what it does in terms of sustainability initiatives. Although various initiatives are in place, it is imperative for the concerned firm to communicate such initiatives through sustainability disclosures, notably in the form of a sustainability report. We believe that utilizing both performance and disclosure as measures of sustainability practices and examining the impact of the interaction term between the two measures to the cost of debt, would provide further insights into debtholders’ perspective on ASEAN firms’ sustainability practices.

Second, our study provides insights into the impact of sustainability practices on the cost of debt of firms, specifically in ASEAN countries, which has yet been examined by previous studies. Prior studies on this topic either focus on a single country—e.g., Erragragui (2018), Ge and Liu (2015)—or a group of developed countries—e.g., Bhuiyan & Nguyen (2019), Eliwa et al. (2019). Prior literature (Eliwa et al., 2019; Fonseka et al., 2019; Ye and Zhang, 2011) highlight that further investigation of the relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt is needed in emerging economies context, due to their diverse cultural and institutional settings, as well as their less prominent stakeholders’ pressures. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first to examine the relationship in an emerging and socially diverse ASEAN region—even though the data availability limit the sample of our study to consists of only five out of ten ASEAN countries (Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand).

Finally, we also incorporate the moderating variables in the country, industry, and firm levels to acknowledge the different characteristics among countries, industries, and firms. Albeit prior studies have incorporated these moderating variables singly, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the moderating effects of these three levels of characteristics to the association of sustainability practices and the cost of debt contemporaneously. It has been recognized that sustainability practices of firms in a more sustainable country are more credible than firms in a less sustainable country due to the efficient monitoring and effective enforcement of sustainability-related regulations imposed in the country (Dhaliwal et al., 2014; Eliwa et al., 2019; Ramanna, 2013; van der Laan Smith et al., 2005). Hence, we expect that, in ASEAN countries that are more sustainable, the negative effect of sustainability practices on the cost of debt is more prominent. Forbye, as suggested by prior literature (Reverte, 2012; Yoon et al., 2018), firms that operate in environmentally sensitive industries are required to meet with higher sustainability standards. As a result, firms in sensitive industries tend to have better corporate performance, legitimacy-seeking behavior, and reduced information asymmetry which strengthen the negative impact of their sustainability practices and the cost of debt (Kilian and Hennigs, 2014). Lastly, it has been recognized that good corporate governance mitigates the agency problem of asymmetric information due to the effective monitoring it espoused (Buertey et al., 2020). It is then arguable that improved corporate governance, which results in enhanced corporate monitoring and mitigation of information asymmetry, may prompt a firm to perform and disclose initiatives that better represent actual changes within the firm (Chan et al., 2014; Giannarakis, 2014). Henceforth, we expect corporate governance to strengthen the negative relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt.

Other than the contributions towards the body of knowledge, our study has several contributions to corporations and policy makers. Our research presents further knowledge that would help corporations understand the extent of their sustainability practices to the financial aspects of corporations, specifically the cost of debt. Moreover, our empirical findings document a broad picture of the sustainability practices of firms in ASEAN countries that could be taken as suggestions for further development sustainability-related policies in ASEAN.

## 2. Theoretical review and hypothesis development

### 2.1. Sustainability practice and the cost of debt

It has been recognized that debtholders are among the firms’ most instrumental stakeholders (Kanda, 1992). Stakeholder theory suggests that in order to obtain stakeholders’ support, firms should directly and explicitly uphold the interests of their stakeholders by adopting relevant practices (Deegan, 2014; Theodoulidis et al., 2017). Thus, by addressing debtholders’ sustainability concerns, firms would be regarded as more creditworthy and rewarded with a lower cost of debt. Meanwhile, legitimacy theory postulates that firms are bound by the social contract in which they agree to perform various socially desired actions in return for approval and ultimate guarantee of their continued existence (Deegan, 2002). The institutional theory also implies that firms are influenced by institutional settings or organizational fields in which they operate, thereby firms are driven to adopt sustainability practices accordingly (Fernando and Lawrence, 2014). Considering the sustainability concerns of debtholders would then increase the legitimacy and creditworthiness of the concerned firm from the debtholders’ perspective (Deegan, 2002), resulting in the firm being rewarded with a reduced cost of debt. Lastly, agency theory (Jensen and Meckling, 1976) posits that sustainability practices, as a form of a firm’s communication, would reduce the information asymmetry that exists between firms and their debtholders (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2019; Martínez-Ferrero et al., 2016). Debtholders face certain types of risks$^2$ regarding their lending decision to a firm (Thompson and Cowton, 2004) and evaluating a firm’s practices might mitigate these risks (Ge and Liu, 2015). In this context, sustainability practices provide insights into the firm’s sustainability initiatives and contributions regarded as crucial in evaluating the creditworthiness of the firm (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2019; Martínez-Ferrero and García-Sánchez, 2017), which can alleviate information asymmetry between a firm and its debtholders. The concerned firm would then be regarded as more creditworthy and rewarded with a lower cost of debt by debtholders (Derrien et al., 2016). Henceforth, in line with these four theories, it is expected that firms that address the sustainability concerns of its stakeholders by implementing sustainability practices would acquire a lower cost of debt compared to firms that do not implement such practices.

Several literature (Crifo et al., 2017; Eliwa et al., 2019; Fonseka et al., 2019; Ge and Liu, 2015) provide empirical evidence of a negative relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt, whilst another literature (Erragragui, 2018; Hoepner et al., 2016; Ye and Zhang, 2011) find otherwise. As the empirical findings on sustainability practice and the cost of debt are still inconclusive, several scholars (Eliwa et al., 2019; Fonseka et al., 2019; Ye and Zhang, 2011) urge further examination of this relationship. Moreover, the relationship has yet to be analyzed in the emerging markets (Fonseka et al., 2019), specifically the ASEAN region.

**H1.** *Sustainability practices of a firm negatively impacted their cost of debt.*

### 2.2. The moderating effect of sustainability practices measured by disclosure on the relationship between sustainability practices measured by performance and the cost of debt

Sustainability performance and disclosure both represents different aspects of sustainability practices. Sustainability performance refers to the actual sustainability-related activities conducted by a firm whilst sustainability disclosure refers to a firm’s communication of such activities to stakeholders. Thence, we examine the subsequent interaction between sustainability performance and sustainability disclosure as both measures of sustainability practices, along with its impact on the cost of debt, to gain insights on debtholders’ perspective of the borrowing firms’ sustainability practices—i.e., whether or not information about a borrowing firm’s sustainability practices as a whole is valuable to debtholders.

In ASEAN countries, significant portions of sustainability disclosures—in the form of issuing sustainability reports—remain voluntary. Agency theory suggests, in this context, that voluntary sustainability disclosure—as a way for firms to communicate sustainability practices to their shareholders and stakeholders (Papoutsi and Sodhi, 2020)—can theoretically be seen as a means to respond to firms’ financial stakeholders (La Rosa et al., 2018) and seems to mitigate the agency problems of asymmetric information (Diamond and Verrecchia, 1991; La Rosa et al., 2018). Thus, firms that disclose their sustainability activities would be seen as more opaque in the eyes of the firms’ stakeholders. Several scholars (Ali et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2014) support this notion by postulating that sustainability disclosure reduces firm risks—including credit risks—and validates a firm’s credibility. Yet, in contrast, Eliwa et al. (2019) document a substitute relationship between ESG performance and ESG disclosure in European Union countries, thereby the interaction term between the two are positively associated with the cost of debt.

Nevertheless, we rely on the theoretical framework and the notion of prior studies (Al-Tuwaijri et al., 2004; Clarkson et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2016) to instead expects a complementary relationship between sustainability performance and disclosure. This would prompt sustainability disclosure to strengthen the negative relationship between sustainability performance and the cost of debt due to the supposition that sustainability disclosures improve stakeholders’ and debtholders’ trust on firms’ performance (including sustainability performance), mitigate information asymmetry between firms and their debtholders, and thus reduce cost of debt.

**H2.** *Firms’ sustainability practices measured by sustainability disclosure strengthens the negative impact of firms’ sustainability practices measured by sustainability performance on their cost of debt.*

### 2.3. The moderating effect of country characteristic: Country sustainability

Referring to institutional theory, each country, as an institutional setting on which firms based their operations, has unique sustainability-related isomorphisms and legitimacy requirements, which eventually affect the nature of firms’ sustainability practices. Scholars—e.g., Baldini et al. (2018), Chapple and Moon (2005)—document empirical evidence that the varying country sustainability characteristics, including socio-cultural diversity and regulations, strongly influence firms’ behavior. In alignment, stakeholder theory posits that, in a more stakeholder-oriented (i.e., a more sustainable) country, firms are more likely to have better sustainability performances and produce higher quality$^3$ sustainability disclosures due to efficient monitoring and effective enforcement of sustainability-related regulations from complementary institutions in the country (Ramanna et al., 2013; van der Laan Smith et al., 2005).

Prior literature has also documented the moderating effect of country sustainability characteristics on the association of sustainability practices and the cost of debt. Based on a sample of 42 countries, Dhaliwal et al. (2014) find significant evidence that the inverse relationship between CSR disclosure and the cost of capital is more evident in more stakeholder-oriented countries due to the fact that CSR disclosures in those countries contain more information needed by stakeholders to monitor and evaluate firms than those in less stakeholder-oriented countries. Likewise, Eliwa et al. (2019) further report the impact of ESG practices on the cost of debt to be greater in more sustainable countries. They find the lower cost of debt is determined by the sustainability characteristics of firms’ origin country, which is consistent with institutional theory.

Howbeit, country sustainability characteristics have not been analyzed as a moderating variable to the relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt in the ASEAN region. ASEAN countries are incredibly diverse, not only in economy and culture but also in the legal framework and public awareness related to sustainability issues (ASEAN CSR Network, 2018).

**H3.** *The negative impact of sustainability practices on the cost of debt is more pronounced for firms operating in more sustainable countries.*

### 2.4. The moderating effect of industry characteristic: Industry sensitivity

The institutional theory postulates that a firm’s sustainability practices are affected by the organizational field or institutional settings (i.e., industry) on which it operates. A body of empirical literature—e.g., Cowen et al. (1987), Miralles-Quiros et al. (2018), Reverte (2012), Yoon et al. (2018)—shows industry as one of the most common variables to explain the content and extent of sustainability performance and disclosure. As suggested by Yoon et al. (2018), firms belonging to sensitive industries are required to meet with higher standards due to the environmentally harmful activities they might endorse. Concurrently, these firms are imposed to provide better corporate performance than firms belonging to non-sensitive industries (Cai et al., 2012; Garcia et al., 2017). In prior studies (e.g., Kilian and Hennigs, 2014), corporations from the mining, oil, and chemical industries generally emphasize more information—especially regarding environmental, health and safety issues—to their stakeholders, as they are closely monitored by their stakeholders who demand these firms to atone for their unsustainable ways. As a result, firms in sensitive industries tend to have better corporate performance, legitimacy-seeking behavior, and reduced information asymmetry, which strengthens the negative impact of their sustainability practices and the cost of debt.

Reverte (2012) finds a more pronounced negative relationship between CSR disclosure quality and the cost of equity capital for those firms operating in environmentally sensitive industries than those of average industries. Miralles-Quiros et al. (2018) also document more substantial positive valuation effects of CSR in environmentally sensitive industries in Brazil.

**H4.** *The negative impact of sustainability practices on the cost of debt is more pronounced for firms operating in environmentally sensitive industries.*

### 2.5. The moderating effect of firm characteristic: Corporate governance

An agency conflict in the form of information asymmetry exists between lenders and borrowing firms, which could ignite opportunistic managerial pursuit. In this context, corporate governance is seen as a system that governs the managers’ behavior and delineates their discretionary opportunistic latitude (Charreaux and Desbrieres, 2001). As effective corporate governance leads to better monitoring of management, several scholars—e.g. Buertey et al., 2020—postulate that it mitigates the agency problem of asymmetric information between firms and their stakeholders, including creditors. Furthermore, in line with stakeholder theory, Sahut et al. (2019) suggest that firms with good corporate governance respond better to the needs of their stakeholders, counting debtholders, compared to firms with poor corporate governance. It is then arguable that improved corporate governance, which results in enhanced corporate monitoring and mitigation of information asymmetry, may prompt a firm to perform and disclose initiatives that better represent actual changes within the firm (Chan et al., 2014; Giannarakis, 2014). We, therefore, expect corporate governance to strengthen the relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt. The effect of corporate governance on such a relationship has yet been extensively examined in ASEAN countries.

**H5.** *The negative impact of sustainability practices on the cost of debt is more pronounced for firms with better corporate governance.*

## 3. Research method

### 3.1. Variables measurement

#### 3.1.1. Dependent variable
Following Eliwa et al. (2019), Luo et al. (2019) and Ye and Zhang (2011), we employ an accounting-based measure of the cost of debt, calculated as the ratio of a firm’s interest expense divided by its average net debt. This proxy is appropriate since sustainability-related measures relate more with accounting-based measures compared to market-based measures due to the fact that accounting-based measures reflect internal decision-making capabilities and managerial performance of the firm rather than external market responses to the firm’s actions (Orlitzky et al., 2003). Data on both interest expense and net debt of a firm are retrieved from Datastream.

#### 3.1.2. Independent variable
The independent variable is sustainability practice, to which two measures, performance, and disclosure, are employed. In line with previous scholars (e.g., Drempetic et al., 2019; Duque-Grisales et al., 2019), we use Refinitiv ESG score as a measure of sustainability performance (*SPerform*), which is considered as a comprehensive evaluation of the company’s sustainability impact due to its research transparency and in-depth insights (Drempetic et al., 2019). As the values range from 0 to 100$^4$, we divided the ESG performance score with 100 and transforms the values of *SPerform* to range from 0 to 1. Meanwhile, for sustainability disclosure, we follow Dhaliwal et al. (2014, 2012) and utilizes an indicator variable (*SReport*) where 1 if a firm discloses its sustainability activities by issuing a standalone sustainability report during the year, and 0 otherwise. Dhaliwal et al. (2014) argue that an indicator variable could avoid bias in data coding, allows a large-sample study, and enables conclusions to be drawn with high generalizability. The data for *SReport* is retrieved from Datastream.

#### 3.1.3. Moderating variables
We also incorporate country sustainability characteristics (*CSC*), industry sensitivity (*SI*), and corporate governance (*CrossList*) as moderating variables. The underlying construct in the measure of a country’s sustainability is the integration of Schwartz and Carroll’s (2003) three core domains of CSR—economic, legal, and ethical responsibilities—and the three-pillar conception of sustainability—environment, social, and economic (see: Purvis et al., 2019). A combination of both concepts resulted in the use of several time-varying proxies that represent each aspect of sustainability in country-level: (1) environmental aspect—Environmental Performance Index (EPI) developed by Yale University; (2) socio-economic aspect—Human Development Index (HDI) developed by UNDP; (3) ethical aspect—ethical behavior of firms (*CorpEthics*) retrieved from the Global Competitiveness Index developed by the World Economic Forum; and (4) legal aspect—sustainability practices regulations indicator based on ASEAN CSR Network (2018). The principal factor of the aforementioned four proxies, *CSC*, is used in the main tests. Furthermore, we utilize an indicator variable (*SI*) to measure industry sensitivity, where 1 if a firm is included in environmentally sensitive industries and 0 otherwise. In line with Miralles-Quiros et al. (2018) and Yoon et al. (2018), we identify industries within the energy, material, and utilities sectors as environmentally sensitive industries, using the Global Industry Classification Standard (hereafter, GICS). Lastly, we use cross-listing as a measure of a firm’s corporate governance. Following Del Bosco and Misani (2016), *CrossList*, is measured by the number of foreign stock exchanges on which the concerned firm cross-lists its shares each year. The data for *CrossList* are taken from Datastream.

#### 3.1.4. Control variables
Our research models include several control variables, which are firm size (*Size*), financial leverage (*Lev*), return on assets (*RoA*), cash flow interest coverage (*CashCov*), financial distress or bankruptcy risk (*ZScore*), and country risk (*CRisk*). *Size* is the natural logarithm of total assets, *Lev* is the ratio of a firm’s total debt to total assets, and *RoA* as the sum of a firm’s net profit or loss and after-tax interest expense on debt deflated by total assets. *CashCov* is measured as the sum of a firm’s operating cash flow and after-tax interest expense on debt divided by total interest expense on debt. *ZScore* are measured based on the Altman Z-Score (Altman, 1968). Finally, we incorporate *CRisk* as a control variable that reflects the extra risk apparent in a specific country or market. We follow the method of Damodaran (2019b, 2019a) and calculates *CRisk* as the sovereign rating default spread of the country adjusted for additional volatility in the equity market, based on sovereign debt rating from Moody’s. A negative relationship between *CoD* and control variables of *Size*, *RoA*, *CashCov*, and *ZScore* is expected to be found as firms with larger *Size*, higher *RoA*, greater *CashCov*, and higher *ZScore* are expected to have a lower default risk and have a more significant cushion that the firm has to meet its interest on debt obligations (Bhuiyan and Nguyen, 2019; Fonseka et al., 2019; Ge and Liu, 2015). Conversely, firms with a lower level of *Lev* are expected to have lower default risks, implying a positive relationship between *Lev* and *CoD* (El Ghoul et al., 2011; Erragragui, 2018; Fonseka et al., 2019). Lastly, a positive relationship is also expected between *CRisk* and *CoD*, as higher sovereign debt rating results in a relatively lower default spread, country risk premium, and borrowing costs in the country.

### 3.2. Research model

Our study examine the following regression model to test the first hypothesis:

$$CoD_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 SPerform_{i,t} + \beta_2 SReport_{i,t} + \beta_3 Size_{i,t} + \beta_4 Lev_{i,t} + \beta_5 RoA_{i,t} + \beta_6 CashCov_{i,t} + \beta_7 ZScore_{i,t} + \beta_8 CRisk_{i,t} + \beta_9 CountryFixedEffects_i + \beta_{10} IndustryFixedEffects_i + \beta_{11} YearFixedEffects_t + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{1}$$

where:
*   $CoD_{i,t}$: the cost of debt
*   $SPerform_{i,t}$: the sustainability performance score
*   $SReport_{i,t}$: sustainability disclosure where 1 if a firm discloses its sustainability activities by issuing a standalone sustainability report and 0 otherwise
*   $Size_{i,t}$: the natural logarithm of total assets
*   $Lev_{i,t}$: the ratio of total debt to total equity
*   $RoA_{i,t}$: the return of assets (the sum of net profit or loss and after-tax interest expense on debt deflated by total assets)
*   $CashCov_{i,t}$: the cash flow interest coverage (the sum of a firm’s operating cash flow and after-tax interest expense on debt deflated by total interest expense on debt)
*   $ZScore_{i,t}$: the Altman Z-Score
*   $CRisk_{i,t}$: the country risk premium (sovereign rating default spread of the firm’s origin country adjusted for additional volatility in the market)

In order to test the second hypothesis, we use the following research model:

$$CoD_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 SPerform_{i,t} + \beta_2 SReport_{i,t} + \beta_3 SPerform_{i,t} * SReport_{i,t} + \beta_4 Size_{i,t} + \beta_5 Lev_{i,t} + \beta_6 RoA_{i,t} + \beta_7 CashCov_{i,t} + \beta_8 ZScore_{i,t} + \beta_9 CRisk_{i,t} + \beta_{10} CountryFixedEffects_i + \beta_{11} IndustryFixedEffects_i + \beta_{12} YearFixedEffects_t + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{2}$$

To test the third, fourth, and fifth hypothesis simultaneously, we employ the following model:

$$CoD_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 SPerform_{i,t} + \beta_2 SReport_{i,t} + \beta_3 CSC_{i,t} + \beta_4 SPerform_{i,t} * CSC_{i,t} + \beta_5 SReport_{i,t} * CSC_{i,t} + \beta_6 SI_{i,t} + \beta_7 SPerform_{i,t} * SI_{i,t} + \beta_8 SReport_{i,t} * SI_{i,t} + \beta_9 CrossList_{i,t} + \beta_{10} SPerform_{i,t} * CrossList_{i,t} + \beta_{11} SReport_{i,t} * CrossList_{i,t} + \beta_{12} Size_{i,t} + \beta_{13} Lev_{i,t} + \beta_{14} RoA_{i,t} + \beta_{15} CashCov_{i,t} + \beta_{16} ZScore_{i,t} + \beta_{17} CRisk_{i,t} + \beta_{18} CountryFixedEffects_i + \beta_{19} IndustryFixedEffects_i + \beta_{20} YearFixedEffects_t + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{3}$$

where:
*   $CSC_{i,t}$: the principal component of EPI, HDI, CorpEthics, and CSRLaw, which represents a country’s sustainability characteristics in the environmental, social, ethical, and legal aspects.
*   $SI_{i,t}$: 1 if a firm is included in environmentally sensitive industries and 0 otherwise
*   $CrossList_{i,t}$: corporate governance (the number of foreign stock exchanges on which the firm cross-lists its shares in each year)

We employed a panel data analysis using generalized least squares (GLS) Random Effects regression based on several considerations. First, the result of the classical assumption tests shows the Random Effects GLS regression model to be the most appropriate. Second, our dependent variable has a continuous distribution that is suitable with the Random Effects GLS model. Third, comparing the results of Random Effects GLS regression, Random Effects regression with clustering, and Fixed Effects regression with clustering, the coefficient of main independent variables—sustainability performance and disclosure—and the interaction terms are statistically significant in the Random Effects GLS regression. Lastly, based on the prior comparison, the standard errors in the Random Effects GLS regression are smaller than those in other models.

### 3.3. Sample selection and data

We used several criteria for sample selection. First, our initial sample consists of all firms listed on the ASEAN Exchanges, which includes Bursa Malaysia (Malaysia), Ho Chi Minh Stock Exchange (Vietnam), Indonesia Stock Exchange (Indonesia), The Philippine Stock Exchange (the Philippines), Singapore Exchange (Singapore), and The Stock Exchange of Thailand (Thailand), over 2004–2019. Second, only non-financial firms are included, as the financial sector is strictly regulated. Third, firms with no financial or ESG information on the Datastream were excluded. Thus, due to the unavailability of their ESG information on Datastream, firms listed on the Ho Chi Minh Stock Exchange (Vietnam) are not included.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Sample and data

The sampling process resulted in an unbalanced panel of a final sample consisting of 1,443 firm-year observations relating to 172 firms, covering the period from 2004 to 2019. Table 1 documents the sample selection procedure.

Table 2 reports the number of observations per country and sector. Panel A in Table 2 shows that the ASEAN-5 countries are well represented in the sample, with Singapore comprising 34.86% of the sample, followed by Malaysia with 25.57%, Indonesia with 14.76%, Thailand with 14.07%, and the Philippines with 10.74%. Panel B in Table 2 shows that all non-financial sectors based on the GICS are well represented in the sample. ## 6. Results and discussion

The Random Effects GLS regression is used to examine the main models. The results for the main models are reported in Table 5.

### 6.1. Sustainability practices and the cost of debt

Referring to column (1) in Table 5, sustainability practice measured by disclosure is negatively associated with the cost of debt, as evidenced by the *SReport* coefficient of -0.0229 is statistically significant at 1% level. Consistent with prior literature (Eliwa 2019; Ge and Liu, 2015; Crifo et al., 2017), the results indicate that firms with better sustainability disclosure (i.e., issue sustainability reports) would gain a lower cost of debt, suggesting that lenders integrate information about the sustainability practices—measured by sustainability disclosure—of the borrowing firms in their risk profile evaluation and lending decision. However, the results for *SPerform* are not significant on the cost of debt, which indicates that debtholders do not consider information about the firm’s sustainability practices measured by sustainability performance in their evaluation of the firm’s creditworthiness.

A possible explanation would be that there are some information in a firm’s sustainability report that is not apparent in the firm’s sustainability performance score, even though those information provide insights into the foundation and the substantiveness of the concerned firm’s sustainability practices that might be valuable in determining a firm’s cost of debt (Al-Tuwaijri et al., 2004; Eliwa et al., 2019; Erragragui, 2018). Sustainability performance refers to sustainability-related activities conducted by the firm, while sustainability disclosure refers to the way the firm communicates its sustainability-related activities to the stakeholders (Eliwa et al., 2019). Although the sustainability performance scores and the metrics on which it based are widely-used and robust, this measure might not include valuable sustainability information that is thoroughly explained in sustainability report of ASEAN firms in the sample, such as a firm’s motives for sustainability practices, a firm’s program distribution of sustainability practices, and a firm’s roadmap for sustainability practices. Henceforth, an ASEAN firm’s issuance of the sustainability report is negatively and significantly affecting its cost of debt, while its sustainability performance is not—providing mixed results for the first hypothesis.

Furthermore, based on Table 5 column (1), we also find the regression coefficients for the control variables to be insignificant on the cost of debt, with the exception of cash flow interest coverage (*CashCov*) and country risk (*CRisk*). Debtholders of ASEAN firms consider a firm’s ability to pay back its interest on debt and the risks within its origin country when evaluating its creditworthiness, as evidenced by the negative *CashCov* coefficient and positive *CRisk* coefficient (significant at 1%). Albeit the results of firm size (*Size*), leverage (*Lev*), return on assets (*RoA*), and Z-score (*ZScore*) are not as expected, these results provide further insights that in ASEAN countries, cash flow interest coverage and country risk are two of the main considerations in determining a firm’s cost of debt.

### 6.2. The moderating effect of sustainability practices measured by disclosure on the relationship between sustainability practices measured by performance and the cost of debt

The results of Table 5 column (2) indicate sustainability practices measured by disclosure to strengthen the negative relationship between sustainability practices measured by performance and the cost of debt in ASEAN countries, thereby supporting H2. Although initially the regression result for Model 2 in Table 5 documents a positive *SPerform* coefficient on the cost of debt, the *SPerform\*SReport* coefficient is negative and significant. This indicates that the negative association between ASEAN firms’ sustainability practices measured by performance and their cost of debt is more apparent when the firms issue sustainability reports, thereby supporting the supposition of a complementary relationship between two measures of sustainability practices (sustainability performance and sustainability disclosure) in H2 and the results of Table 4.

A positive relationship between *SPerform* and *CoD* when *SReport* is not present implies that a firm that performs sustainability activities without disclosing these activities does not benefit from a lower cost of debt and disclosing sustainability activities by issuing sustainability reports is crucial in determining a firm’s creditworthiness. Albeit this does not align with the findings of Eliwa et al. (2019), the result above aligns with the legitimacy theory—which highlights that a firm’s legitimacy might still be threatened, even if the firm performs sustainability activities, if it fails to make disclosures that convincingly demonstrate compliance (Newson and Deegan, 2002; Lanis and Richardson, 2012)—and the results of Al-Tuwaijri et al. (2004), Clarkson et al. (2008), and Gao et al. (2016). Furthermore, whilst the results of H1 documents sustainability practices measured by performance do not significantly affect the cost of debt, the result of H2 suggests that, in ASEAN countries, disclosing sustainability activities improves the substantiveness of the borrowing firms’ performance, including sustainability performance, and their credibility in debtholders’ perspective. As a result, the concerned firms’ sustainability performance information is deemed more valuable in the eyes of debtholders, and a lower cost of debt is obtained by the firm.

### 6.3. The moderating effect of country sustainability characteristics

In regards to the third hypothesis, the results in Table 5 column (3) show that the negative impact of a firm’s sustainability practices on its cost of debt is not more pronounced in more sustainable ASEAN countries. Country sustainability characteristics negatively affect the cost of debt, which is consistent with a prior study from Hoepner et al. (2016) and our hypothesis. Nevertheless, the interaction term between country sustainability characteristics and firms’ sustainability practices (performance and disclosure) emanates different results. The *SPerform\*CSC* and the *SReport\*CSC* are not statistically significant. Therefore, H3 is not supported.

The aforementioned results are different from prior literature (Dhaliwal et al., 2014; Eliwa et al., 2019) in developed countries. Thence, it is arguable that country sustainability characteristics have differing impacts on the association between sustainability practices and the cost of debt based on the nature of those countries themselves—mainly, developed versus developing countries. In the context of ASEAN firms in the sample, this supposition is unsurprising albeit in contrast with our third hypothesis, as the results of descriptive statistics (Table 3) and correlation matrix (Table 4) indicate a negative relationship between firms’ sustainability practices—performance and disclosure—and the sustainability characteristics of their origin countries. Referring to institutional theory, a firm’s organizational practices are affected by not only organizational-level factors but also factors from institutional settings (e.g., country) on which it operates (Oliver, 1991)—one of these being legal framework (Porta et al., 2008). Subsequently, these institutional factors drive firms within the settings to very likely resemble one another—a process described as isomorphism. The overall ineffective implementation of sustainability-related regulation in ASEAN may lead to minuscule coercive isomorphism that imposes firms to adopt sustainability practices. Furthermore, Koch et al. (2013) imply that country-level and firm-level governance are substitutes for each other in a country whereby the prevailing legal framework is inefficient. Whilst Koch et al. (2013) focus on corporate governance rather than sustainability practices, we believe the same principle can be implemented. Henceforth, in a region—namely, ASEAN—whereby sustainability-related regulations are ineffective, firms’ sustainability practices are negatively related—i.e., act as substitutes of—country sustainability. This relationship would then result in country-level and firm-level sustainability in ASEAN to negate each others’ effect on the cost of debt, thereby the interaction term between the two is not significant to the cost of debt.

### 6.4. The moderating effect of industry sensitivity

As seen in column (3) of Table 5, the indicator variable *SI* is not significantly related to the cost of debt. Albeit it does not align with our expectation, this result is unsurprising, as Table 5 also reports *SI* to not significantly correlated to *CoD*. Likewise, the interaction term between the sustainability performance of firms in environmentally sensitive industries or *SPerform\*SI* and the cost of debt, as well as the interaction term of sustainability disclosure of firms in environmentally sensitive industries or *SReport\*SI* and the cost of debt, are insignificant. These results indicate no significant difference between sustainability practice and the cost of debt of a company belonging to environmentally sensitive industries versus non-environmentally sensitive industries operating in ASEAN countries, thus rejecting H4.

The findings in Table 5 column (3) regarding industry sensitivity as a moderating variable do not align with extant empirical findings, specifically Reverte (2012) and Garcia et al. (2017), in which firms in sensitive industries arguably have better sustainability performance and sustainability disclosure than firms in non-sensitive industries. These firms also acquire lower cost of debt than firms in non-sensitive industries, yet the differences are minuscule. It is possible that, albeit firms in sensitive industries have better sustainability practices implementation than firms in non-sensitive industries, these practices are still inadequate to fulfill the sustainability expectations of debtholders. The negative effects of unsustainable practices—including those caused by environmentally sensitive industries—in the ASEAN region continue to increase, giving rise to the environmental vulnerability that endangers the socio-economic stability (ADB, 2017; ASEAN, 2019). As a response to the environmental damage caused by these firms, stakeholders—including debtholders—impose even higher sustainability expectations on ASEAN firms that belong to environmentally sensitive industries (Yoon et al., 2018). Henceforth, debtholders in ASEAN countries do not highly value the sustainability practices of environmentally sensitive industries compared to overall industries.

### 6.5. The moderating effect of corporate governance

Overall, referring to column (3) in Table 5, we find mixed results where the coefficient of *SPerform\*CrossList* coefficient is insignificant, but a significant negative association is documented by *SReport\*CrossList*, which aligns with our hypothesis that corporate governance strengthen the negative effect of sustainability disclosure on the cost of debt.

The underlying argument as to why corporate governance moderates sustainability disclosure and the cost of debt but not sustainability performance and the cost of debt might be related to the argument for H1. Sustainability performance score might not include information in sustainability reports—such as a firm’s motives, program distribution, and roadmap for sustainability practices—that provide insights into the foundation and the substantiveness of the firm’s sustainability practices and crucial in determining a firm’s cost of debt (Al-Tuwaijri et al., 2004; Eliwa et al., 2019; Erragragui, 2018). Therefore, in the results of H1, *SReport* is significant to *CoD*, but *SPerform* is not. It is then arguable that improved corporate governance, which results in enhanced monitoring and better mitigation of information asymmetry, would prompt a firm to issue sustainability report that better represent actual sustainability changes within the firm (i.e., improve the base and substantiveness of its practices) and thus, strengthening the relationship between a firm’s sustainability practices measured by disclosure and the cost of debt. In contrast, even with the improved corporate governance, a firm’s sustainability performance score still cannot fully embody all of the crucial information that debtholders need to determine a firm’s cost of debt, thereby corporate governance does not strengthen the impact of sustainability practices measured by performance on the cost of debt.

## 7. Sensitivity analysis

We perform several sensitivity tests with the purpose to analyze the robustness of the main findings, which consists of: (1) test to identify the existence of non-linear relationship; (2) alternative sustainability performance measure; (3) alternative country sustainability characteristics measure; (4) exclusion of firms operating in Singapore.

We examine whether or not there is a non-linear relationship between ASEAN firms’ sustainability practices—measured by performance (*SPerform*) and disclosure (*SReport*)—and the cost of debt. Albeit most of the prior literature—e.g., Eliwa et al. (2019), Erragragui (2018), Fonseka et al. (2019), Ge and Liu (2015)—find a linear relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt, a study by Ye and Zhang (2011) based on a sample of Chinese firms finds a non-linear relationship instead. There is a possibility that a relationship alike exists in ASEAN—an emerging market similar to China. Therefore, we follow the method of testing developed by Lind and Mehlum (2010) to identify the existence of a non-linear relationship on all three models. The results reported no presence of a non-linear relationship between sustainability practices and the cost of debt (untabulated).

Furthermore, albeit the corporate governance measure used as a moderating variable differs from the measure used by Refinitiv for ESG score or sustainability performance, it is possible that collinearity exists between these governance-related measures. In accordance, we run a sensitivity test to examine further whether or not the main findings related to the hypotheses are robust by incorporating only the environmental and social scores from Refinitiv ESG in sustainability performance (*SPerform*). This study finds no significant difference between the results of H1, H2, H3, H4, and H5 using the *SPerform* measure in the main findings and using this alternative *SPerform* measure (untabulated).

Moreover, the moderating effect of country sustainability characteristics on the relationship of sustainability practices and the cost of debt will be further examined through two alternative measures of *CSC*. First, the economic aspect of sustainability is considered as one independent proxy; thereby, the *CSC* moderating variable is a principal component of five proxies (EPI, HDI, GNI per capita, Ethical Behaviour of Firms from GCI, and sustainability regulations indicator of each country per year). To reflect the economic aspect, this study utilizes an index based on the country’s gross national income (GNI) per capita retrieved from UNDP, which indicates the average national income of a country in a year. Second, this study utilizes an alternative country sustainability measure based on the ESG framework. In accordance with the ESG framework, the proxies, on which *CSC* are based, consist of the EPI (to reflect the environmental aspect), the HDI (to reflect the social aspect), and the Worldwide Governance Indicator (to reflect the governance aspect). The time-varying data of Worldwide Governance Indicator (hereafter, WGI) are retrieved from the World Bank. However, we find no significant difference between the results of both alternative measures of *CSC* and the primary results (untabulated).

Lastly, it is arguable that a development gap exists between ASEAN countries, not only between ASEAN-5 and CMLV countries but also between ASEAN-5 countries themselves. In this regard, Singapore has a competitive edge over other ASEAN-5 countries, thereby prompting the IMF and the World Bank to even consider Singapore as a developed country—in contrast to other ASEAN-5 countries which are still considered as developing nations. The sample of this study shows a high representation of firms from Singapore. Therefore, to ensure the robustness of the primary results, this study regresses the main models after excluding the Singaporean firms from the sample, and the findings remain the same, albeit untabulated. Overall, the results of these sensitivity tests are not qualitatively different from the results of this study’s primary analysis—indicative of the robustness of the main results.

## 8. Conclusion

In recent years, there is a growing trend that recognizes the importance of sustainability practices and the significance of the corporate debt market. We present contributions to the existing body of literature by empirically examining the impact of firms’ sustainability practices on their cost of debt of 5 ASEAN countries from 2004 to 2019. The results are robust, based on a battery of sensitivity tests.

The principal findings of our study reveal mixed results that sustainability practices have statistically negative effects on debt financing in ASEAN countries. Debtholders incorporate information related to the borrowing firm’s sustainability practices from a sustainability report issued by the firm into their risk assessments and lending decisions, thereby an ASEAN firm that issues more sustainability reports are rewarded with a lower cost of debt. Howbeit, it is arguable that there are some valuable information in debtholders’ perspective that exist within a firm’s sustainability disclosure that are not represented by the metrics of sustainability performance. Henceforth, debtholders of ASEAN firms do not highly regard information of the firm’s sustainability practices measured by sustainability performance in determining the firm’s cost of debt. Nevertheless, we document a complementary relationship between the two measures of ASEAN firms’ sustainability practices (i.e., performance and disclosure) in which, sustainability practices measured by disclosure strengthen the negative relationship between sustainability practices measured by performance and the cost of debt. It is arguable that ASEAN firms that disclose their sustainability-related activities by issuing sustainability reports are able to improve their corporate performance, the substantiveness of their sustainability performance, and their credibility in the eyes of debtholders, which results in a lower cost of debt obtained by the firms. As this finding contrasts those of Eliwa et al. (2019), based on a sample of firms in European Union countries, it indicates the diverging debtholders’ perspectives on the sustainability practices of firms in developed countries (e.g., European Union countries) and developing countries (e.g., ASEAN countries).

Furthermore, in contrast to prior studies conducted in developed countries, the relationship between a firm’s sustainability practices and its cost of debt is not more pronounced in more sustainable ASEAN countries, possibly due to the inefficient sustainability-related legal framework found in ASEAN countries that causes firms’ sustainability practices to act as substitutes of its origin country’s sustainability characteristics. Moreover, we find no conclusive evidence that industry sensitivity influences the cost of debt. These results might be associated with the immense sustainability-related expectations that stakeholders and debtholders imposed on firms in environmentally sensitive industries as a response to their environmentally harmful practices. Lastly, we find mixed results that corporate governance moderates the relationship between an ASEAN firm’s sustainability practices and its cost of debt. Good corporate governance and the effective monitoring it imposed prompt firms to disclose and issue sustainability reports that are more reflective of real sustainability changes within the firms, thereby strengthening the negative relationship of sustainability practice, measured by disclosure, and the cost of debt. Conversely, corporate governance does not strengthen the negative relationship between sustainability practices, measured by performance, and the cost of debt, possibly due to some information (e.g., a firm’s motives, program distribution, and roadmap for sustainability practices) deemed crucial in determining a firm’s creditworthiness, that are represented in sustainability report but not in sustainability performance score, even with better corporate governance. In conclusion, these findings further indicate that ASEAN, as an emerging economy, emanates different results related to sustainability practices and the cost of debt compared to prior literature in developed countries.

Based on these results, there are several implications for academia, mainly by being the first, to the best of our knowledge, to provide empirical results to the topic of sustainability practices and the cost of debt in ASEAN. Our findings extend the prior literature and represent opportunities for future research related to sustainability practices and the cost of debt. Furthermore, as the relationship documented between the two measures of sustainability practices is positive, it is further implied that, by disclosing sustainability activities and developing a more substantive approach of sustainability performance, ASEAN firms would benefit from a lower cost of debt. Managers should be encouraged to increase their attention to sustainability related activities as these are investments that may reduce information asymmetries, leading to lower costs of debt. Lastly, for policymakers in ASEAN countries, the results of our study shed light on the importance of enacting an efficient legal framework and effective monitoring that could drive its people towards adopting more sustainable practices. It might provide insights on key areas of considerations for future sustainability related regulations in the ASEAN region as well. In addition, policymakers could consider further mandatory CSR requirements for ASEAN firms operating in environmentally sensitive industries to atone for their harmful activities.

In spite of the contributions of our study, there are several limitations that represent opportunities for future research in this area. First, we only employed secondary data obtained from specialized databases, mainly Datastream, and whilst these databases are widely accepted in accounting literature, collecting primary data—such as interviews with corporate executives—may provide diverging results. Second, related to sustainability performance scores, the process by which Refinitiv assesses firms’ ESG score leads to the creation of a single rating and corresponding score. Albeit this is highly useful for empiricists, the use of alternative measures that distinguish between positive and negative sustainability performance may shed additional light into which parts of sustainability performance that are relevant for debtholders. Third, the use of an indicator variable to reduce bias in coding sustainability disclosure potentially causes a significant loss of information about sustainability disclosure quality and future research could incorporate content analysis to gain further understanding of the quality and importance of sustainability reporting as a proxy of sustainability disclosure. Fourth, the use of an accounting-based measure of the cost of debt might not consider denomination differences between local and foreign currency—even though such differences affect the interest rate assigned to the debt and thereby, the cost of debt itself—and could be noisy if a firm changes its level of debt near year-end. Future research can employ alternative measures—e.g., credit ratings, yield spread—or develop enhanced measures of the cost of debt to overcome this limitation. Fifth, we measure country sustainability characteristics through the use of a principal component based on four proxies. Although we utilize two additional alternative proxies in sensitivity analysis, it is possible that we do not include other factors related to country sustainability characteristics that simultaneously determine the relationship of a firm’s sustainability performance and the cost of debt. Thence, future research could explore other factors (e.g., public awareness of sustainability, degree of investor protection, legal framework, social cohesion) that could make the measure of country sustainability characteristics more robust. In addition, it would be interesting for future research to examine other measures of corporate governance (e.g., board independence, ownership concentration, audit committee effectiveness) and incorporate other industries that are deemed as environmentally sensitive endemic in the main region of the research—e.g., agriculture, paper and forest products—to investigate their impact on the association between sustainability practices and the cost of debt. It is also suggested that future research could expand the research on the impact of sustainability practices—measured by performance and disclosure—to the cost of debt to a global scale (i.e., beyond ASEAN firms). Lastly, it is enthralling to examine the utilization of other reports, such as an integrated report, and its impact on the cost of debt—a suggestion for future research.
