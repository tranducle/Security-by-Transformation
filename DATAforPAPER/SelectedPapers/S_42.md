# ESG Performance and the Cost of Debt of Chinese Firms: Do Board Idiosyncrasies Matter?

## Abstract
The ESG paradigm has exerted increasing pressure on Chinese firms to adopt environment-friendly and socially responsible policies as part of their governance framework. Despite its importance, limited attention has been given to the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt (COD) and whether board traits influence this relationship. Therefore, this study addresses this gap by analyzing the moderating influence of board size and independence on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt using a two-way interaction approach. We also investigate whether board diversity (i.e., age, gender, education, and culture) complements board size and independence in strengthening the negative relationship between ESG performance and COD using the three-way interaction approach. The study employed two-stage least squares and two-step GMM estimation techniques using a sample of Chinese non-financial firms. The two-way interaction results suggest that large and independent boards have a moderating effect and influence the negative relationship between ESG and COD. Furthermore, the three-way interaction results suggest that board diversity complements large and independent boards by strengthening the negative impact of ESG on COD. This effect of board diversity is present when the board of directors possesses age, gender, educational, and cultural diversity. Interestingly, our analysis also indicates that ESG performance is beneficial for both environmentally sensitive and non-sensitive firms but has more profound benefits for environmentally sensitive firms.

**Keywords** ESG performance · Cost of debt · Board size · Board independence · Board diversity · Environmental sensitivity

## Introduction

The growing importance of ESG performance has attracted the attention of academics, policymakers, and other stakeholders. The ESG paradigm has exerted increasing pressure on firms to adopt environment-friendly and socially responsible policies as part of their governance framework. The adoption of sound ESG practices and their full disclosure is becoming essential for firms to sustain their competitive advantage and deliver healthy financial performance. Corporate stakeholders such as investors, employees, suppliers, customers, and shareholders are also expecting firms to not only focus on the profit maximization objective but also give adequate consideration to social and environmental issues. As a result, most global firms are reporting their ESG practices which are being used by leading credit rating agencies for assigning ESG scores. The concept of ESG has also evolved over time. Initially, Environmental, Health, and Safety (EHS) initiatives were introduced which progressed into corporate sustainability and corporate social responsibility campaigns. Recently, the adoption of ESG practices has gained momentum and focuses on improving the overall governance environment in a firm.

Firms are using ESG performance as a tool for communicating their commitment towards the environment, society, and governance (Raimo et al., 2021). Investors also consider ESG performance in their investment decisions which is increasing pressure on firms to achieve high ESG scores (Shakil, 2021). High ESG performance requires adequate non-financial disclosures that reduce the information asymmetry, perceived risk and ultimately the cost of debt (Eliwa et al., 2021). However, some studies suggest that managers safeguard their professional reputation by portraying sound ESG performance but continue to pursue their private interests (Prior et al., 2008). Therefore, we argue that the misalignment of interests can be mitigated through enhanced governance and the board is mainly responsible for enforcing monitoring mechanisms. Further, we argue that firms with effective and diverse boards would be able to, benefit from sound ESG performance. For example, firms committed to ESG performance are considered more sustainable and less risky, therefore, lenders would be willing to provide financing at a lower rate. Several recent studies suggest that ESG is negatively associated with COD and other studies found that board characteristics moderate the association between ESG and financial performance (Raimo et al., 2021; Velte, 2020).

In view of the importance of ESG, several studies have been conducted to understand its benefits and determinants. For instance, Li et al. (2018) analyzed the moderating role of Chief Executive Officer (CEO) power on the relationship between ESG and firm value. Further, Nekhili et al. (2021) analyzed the moderating role of the employee board on the relationship between ESG and financial performance. In addition, Rastogi et al. (2023) analyzed the moderating role of ownership concentration on the relationship between ESG and firm value. While many studies have focused on the impact of ESG on firm performance, value, and risk, but very few studies have investigated the impact of ESG performance on COD (Raimo et al., 2021; Gigante & Manglaviti, 2022; Apergis, Poufinas & Antonopoulous, 2022). In view of this gap, this study intends to further extend the existing literature on ESG performance and COD by investigating whether board size and independence influence the relationship. Further, we analyze whether board diversity complements board size and independence in strengthening the negative relationship between ESG and COD using the three-way interaction approach which is rarely used in the literature. Unlike previous studies, we used a sample of Chinese non-financial firms comprising 35,040 firm-year observations for the period 2009 to 2021. The study has focused on China as it’s the world’s fastest-growing economy which is striving to achieve peak carbon emissions by 2030 and carbon neutrality before 2060. China strictly abides by the principle that “Nature is invaluable”. Therefore, we believe that an in-depth analysis of Chinese firms’ ESG performance is tremendously important both from an academic and practical perspective. Our study is different from previous studies in several ways. First, we investigate whether board size and independence moderate the association between ESG performance and COD using the two-way interaction approach. Second, we analyze whether board diversity complements board size and independence in strengthening the negative influence of ESG on COD, using the three-way interaction approach. Third, our study provides evidence using a comprehensive sample of Chinese non-financial listed firms.

The study uses the two-stage least squares (2SLS) and two-step GMM for empirical estimation of the main models. The reported results are robust to endogeneity concerns and are consistent across alternative variable measurements. Our study contributes to the existing literature in several ways. First, we provide evidence that board size and independence moderate (weaken) the negative relationship between ESG performance and COD. Second, we find that board diversity complements board size and independence in strengthening the negative relationship between ESG and COD. The three-way interaction results imply that board effectiveness does not depend only on its size and independence but also on its diversity. Third, our results indicate that sound ESG performance benefits both environmentally sensitive and non-sensitive firms in terms of lower COD. However, environmentally sensitive firms with sound ESG performance experience a significantly lower COD than their non-sensitive counterparts. Fourth, by analyzing ESG score thresholds we report that firms at the higher ESG threshold have significantly lower COD as compared to firms in the other threshold. Fifth, the results suggest that ESG performance has profoundly reduced COD during the Chinese government’s 13th five-year plan as opposed to the previous five-year period. The favorable outcome is a reflection of China’s commitment towards sustainable and transparent ESG practices.

The remaining paper is structured as follows. The “Literature Review, Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development” section provides the literature review, theoretical background and the hypotheses development. The “Methodology” section presents the methodology while the “Discussion of Results” section provides the empirical results and their discussion. The “Further Analyses” section presents further analyses. Finally, the “Conclusion” section concludes the study and presents the implications, limitations, and directions for further research.

## Literature Review, Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

### Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) Performance and the Cost of Debt

COD refers to the rate of interest that a firm pays to its debtholders. Firms use several strategies to gain the confidence of investors and other stakeholders while striving for lower COD to improve firm performance. The previous literature suggests that COD is an important factor that affects the overall performance of a company (Yazdanfar & Öhman, 2015). The COD of a firm is affected by many factors such as risk-taking, ownership structure, CEO characteristics, voluntary disclosures, and the existing level of debt financing (Mattingly, 2017; Owusu et al., 2022; Raimo et al., 2021). Further, the demands of stakeholders including shareholders and investors have changed considerably, who expect firms to not only focus on the profit maximization objective but also give adequate consideration to social and environmental issues. Therefore, the adoption of sound ESG practices and their full disclosure is becoming essential for firms to sustain their competitive advantage, lower COD, and deliver healthy financial performance. Further, the relationship between ESG performance and COD may be explained by the agency and signaling theories (Raimo et al., 2021). The agency theory implies that lenders (principals) provide financing to the managers (agents) expecting a return that compensates them for the risk taken (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Managers tend to have an information advantage over other stakeholders and this information asymmetry problem may be reduced through greater information disclosures (Bryl & Fijałkowska, 2020). Further, greater ESG disclosure leads to better ESG performance. Firms with high ESG performance are considered more sustainable with lower agency costs and COD (Raimo et al., 2021). It is argued that enhanced ESG disclosure reduces the information asymmetry and associated agency costs. Moreover, the signaling theory implies that managers disclose ESG information to differentiate their firms from unsustainable and non-credit-worthy firms (Abdi & Omri, 2020). ESG information and disclosures provide positive signals to stakeholders about the firm’s long-term commitment to ESG. The signaling theory implies that voluntary disclosures such as ESG disclosure mitigate the concerns of lenders and helps in reducing the COD (Abdi & Omri, 2020; Spence, 1973). ESG performance also reflects a firm’s commitment to efficient and sustainable business practices which are carefully evaluated by lenders (Caragnano et al., 2020). Prior research suggests that lenders are likely to avoid lending to firms damaging the environment and society due to the adverse reputational consequences of such decisions (Caragnano et al., 2020). Several recent studies have reported that sound ESG performance reduces COD (Raimo et al., 2021). These studies argue that firms with sound ESG performance are considered sustainable, less risky and have a lower cost of debt. Hence, we developed the following hypothesis:

**H1:** There is a negative association between ESG performance and the cost of debt, ceteris paribus.

### Board Idiosyncrasies, ESG Performance, and the Cost of Debt

The board plays a crucial role in the modern business environment, especially in organizations where the ownership and management functions are separated. The board usually consists of inside and outside directors and the total number of directors determines the board size. Board size refers to the total number of directors on the board while board independence refers to the proportion of independent directors on the board (Naciti, 2019; Poletti-Hughes & Briano-Turrent, 2019). Board size and the proportion of outside directors (i.e., independent directors) influence the board’s effectiveness towards corporate governance and its ability to make environment-friendly decisions. The agency theory suggests that effective boards mitigate agency conflicts and improve a firm’s financial and non-financial performance (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). The stakeholder theory also suggests that effective boards tend to perform in favor of stakeholders and pursue their objectives (Perrault, 2015; Zalata et al., 2019). However, the previous literature provides mixed evidence on the effectiveness of large and independent boards. For instance, Husted and Sousa-Filho (2018) reported a positive relationship between board size (BS) and ESG performance while Orazalin (2019) and Giannarakis et al. (2014) found a negative relationship. Further, Kiliç et al. (2015) suggested a positive relationship between board independence (BI) and ESG performance while, Ararat et al. (2010) reported a negative association between them. Further, Fernández-Gago et al. (2018) argued that BI is likely to stimulate socially responsible behavior among board members that may enhance sustainable and environment-friendly decision-making. Cucari et al. (2018) also suggested that firms with independent boards are likely to invest more in sustainable activities. Ortas et al. (2017) argue that boards with more independent directors highly encourage investment in ESG practices to satisfy the concerns of stakeholders. On the contrary, Khan et al. (2021) and Pucheta-Martınez and Gallego-Alvarez (2019) suggest that BI does not play a crucial role in encouraging firms to disclose ESG information.

It is clear from the above discussion that BS and BI have both advantages and disadvantages. The proponents of large and independent boards argue that such boards usually perform a better monitoring function and bring rich experience which reduces the wasteful utilization of assets and mitigates agency problems (Orazalin, 2019). Further, BI is crucial for mitigating agency conflicts as inside directors would pursue their private interests (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Contrarily, it is argued that large and independent boards suffer from free rider problems and coordination costs. It has also been argued that when BS and BI surpass certain thresholds, the board becomes inefficient, and the associated costs exceed the benefits (Lipton & Lorch, 1992). Further, large and independent boards may delay decisions because they suffer from communication issues and egoistic behavior, especially outside directors who do not usually have comprehensive information due to low involvement in the daily operations of the firm (Ararat, Orbay & Yurtoglu, 2010). In light of these contradictory viewpoints, we believe that there is a need to investigate whether BS and BI are favorable for ESG performance. Further, BS and BI influence the relationship between several variables. However, to the best of our knowledge, the literature does not provide evidence of whether BS and BI moderate the association between ESG performance and COD. Hence, we intend to fill this gap by investigating whether BI influences the relationship between ESG performance and COD. Thus, the following hypotheses were developed.

**H2:** Board size moderates the association between ESG performance and the cost of debt, ceteris paribus.

**H3:** Board independence moderates the association between ESG performance and the cost of debt, ceteris paribus.

As discussed above, the existing literature provides mixed evidence on how BS and BI affect ESG performance. This implies that some other characteristics of the board may influence the moderating impact of BS and BI on the relationship between ESG and COD. We argue that BS and BI may not bring enough monitoring efficiency to the board. The recent literature suggests that diverse boards with age, gender, educational, and cultural diversity perform better monitoring and provide superior strategic decision-making (Beji et al., 2021; Issa & Zaid, 2021). These studies argue that diverse boards bring rich and extensive industry knowledge, skills, experience, and cultural insights that enhance the board’s effectiveness and prevents groupthink. Several theories have explained the importance of board diversity for a firm. For instance, the resource-based theory (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978) considers the board of directors as an important resource that provides counseling, advice, and monitoring (Hillman & Dalziel, 2003). It implies that diverse boards help protect the interests of stakeholders and strengthen firms’ ability to secure important resources such as information, finance competencies, and knowledge. The cognitive theory argues that diversity on the board is crucial and advantageous as it serves as the basis of strategic value-addition, and efficient decision-making and brings new insight to the boardroom (Beji et al., 2021; Harjoto et al., 2019). The agency theory postulates that performance can be improved with diverse boards when directors of different ages, genders, educational backgrounds, and nationalities work collectively and raise their voices against decisions that adversely affect minority shareholders and other stakeholders (Carter et al., 2003). Similarly, recent studies also suggest that diversity enhances the board’s overall strategic decision-making potential (Hafsi & Turgut, 2013; Beji et al., 2021). These studies argue that diverse boards make environment and socially friendly decisions that enhance a firm’s overall ESG performance. Further, Bear et al. (2010) and Shaukat et al. (2016) argue that diverse boards tend to influence a firm’s CSR strategy ultimately enhancing its reputation. Ferrero-Ferrero et al. (2015) and Post et al. (2011) found a positive relationship between board age diversity and firms’ social performance. Contrarily, several studies found an insignificant effect of board diversity (i.e., gender, age, and demographic) on ESG, CSR, and social performance (Hafsi & Turgut, 2013; Giannarakis, 2014; Manita et al., 2018). Based on the above discussion, we argue that the advantages of enhanced board diversity outweigh the disadvantages of large and independent boards. Therefore, large and independent boards may outperform in executing their fiduciary duties in the presence of enhanced diversity. We also argue that firms are better able to communicate their creditworthiness and sustainability to lenders in the presence of a diverse board. Further, board diversity reduces managerial opportunism therefore, lenders would perceive firms with diverse boards as less risky. Moreover, board diversity prevents groupthink in the board room by stimulating critical thinking on key decisions, better monitoring, and enhancing overall board effectiveness. In addition, board diversity in terms of age, gender, culture, and education brings rich industry knowledge, experience, and energy to the board required for making influential strategic decisions that enhance stakeholders’ confidence. However, to the best of our knowledge, prior studies have not provided evidence that board diversity complements BS and BI to strengthen the negative relationship between ESG and COD. Therefore, we intend to fill this gap and develop the following hypotheses:

**H4:** Large boards with more diversity strengthen the negative influence of ESG on the cost of debt, ceteris paribus.

**H5:** Independent boards with more diversity strengthen the negative influence of ESG on the cost of debt, ceteris paribus.

## Methodology

### Data and Sampling

This study has utilized the data of non-financial firms listed on the Chinese stock exchanges for the period 2009 to 2021. Consistent with past studies, we exclude financial firms as the financial sector adheres to different regulatory and disclosure requirements (Eliwa et al., 2021; Raimo et al., 2021). The final dataset comprises 35,040 firm-year observations from 81 industries and is unbalanced in nature. The data was extracted using the Sino-Securities Index Information (SSII) and CSMAR databases which are considered reliable databases for both financial and non-financial data, consistent with Li et al. (2022), Raimo et al. (2021) and Xia et al. (2023).

### Measurement of Dependent, Independent, and Moderating Variables

The study used the cost of debt as a dependent variable which was measured as a proportion of interest expense to the liabilities at the year-end, consistent with Caragnano et al. (2020) and Maaloul (2018). Further, ESG performance was measured through ESG scores extracted from Sino-Securities Index Information (SSII) which is considered a highly reliable financial database (Bin-Feng et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022). The review of the prior literature suggests that many rating agencies publish ESG scores/ratings of firms after a thorough evaluation in three domains, i.e. environmental, social, and governance. Li et al. (2022) reported that globally about 600 rating agencies are providing ESG scores/ratings of firms and among them 20 are Chinese. These rating agencies differ in terms of evaluation matrices, duration, and coverage. In the context of China, SSII is considered a highly reliable rating agency that covers the widest range of companies over a long time duration. Therefore, this study uses ESG scores of SSII consistent with Li et al. (2022), Zheng et al. (2023), and Bin-Feng et al. (2024). SSII assigns scores to firms ranging from 1 to 100 after evaluating firm-specific information from 16 different themes covering more than 40 key issues related to ESG. BS was measured through a logarithm of the total members on the board (Poletti-Hughes & Briano-Turrent, 2019). BI was measured as the proportion of independent directors to the total board size (Naciti, 2019). Further, the board is considered to be diverse when it consists of board members with diverse education, gender, affiliation, age and nationality. Therefore, we measured board diversity comprehensively using four dimensions of diversity, i.e. age, nationality, education, and gender. Board Age Diversity (BAD) was measured by dividing the average age of directors by the standard deviation of directors’ age. Moreover, we measured Board Gender Diversity (BGD) as the proportion of female directors to the total board size consistent with Kalcheva and Lins (2007). Board Educational Diversity (BED) was measured by the BLAU index consistent with Kabir et al. (2023) and used the board members’ graduating universities and specializations for calculating it. The higher the BLAU index the higher the educational diversity on the board. In addition, we measured cultural diversity using the Overseas Ratio (OVR). OVR is measured as the proportion of foreign directors and directors with foreign experience to the total members on the board, consistent with Westphal and Zajac (1995).

### Measurement of Control Variables

Several control variables such as firm size (Fsize), firm age (Fage), growth, leverage, and listing age (Listage) were used consistent with the existing literature. We measured Fsize as the natural logarithm of total assets and leverage as the proportion of total liabilities to total assets (Abdullah, Hashmi & Iqbal, 2022). Furthermore, Fage was measured as a natural logarithm of the total number of years from the commencement of business and Listage as the natural logarithm of the number of years since the firm is listed on the respective stock exchange (Lundvall & Battese, 2000). Lastly, we measured growth by dividing the operating income of the current year by the operating income of the previous year minus 1.

### Statistical Analysis and Model Specifications

The review of the literature suggests that empirical analysis usually suffers from problems such as endogeneity, multicollinearity, and heteroscedasticity that may adversely affect the statistical results. Therefore, this study has employed the two-stage least squares (2SLS) for empirical estimation. 2SLS employs the instrumental variables approach to address the endogeneity problem. It is argued that firms with high ESG performance tend to have lower COD. However, it may be argued that firms with lower COD tend to invest in ESG practices as these firms may avail debt financing at a lower cost which is invested to enhance ESG performance. Therefore, to address this potential endogeneity issue, we have employed 2SLS for empirical estimation. Consistent with the previous literature, we used the industry average of ESG scores and lagged variables as instruments (Khan et al., 2021; Semadeni et al., 2014). Further, in order to cross-validate the 2SLS results we employed the two-step GMM estimation which is also extensively used in the previous literature. The use of two-step GMM further strengthens our empirical analysis. Before statistical estimation, we also performed several diagnostic tests to detect multicollinearity, structural breaks, non-normality, heteroscedasticity, and autocorrelation and ascertain if the required assumptions have been satisfied.

The study estimated 2 interaction models (i.e., Models 1–2) that analyze whether BS and BI moderate the relationship between ESG performance and COD following the approach of Brambor et al. (2006) and Dawson (2014). To empirically validate H1-H3, the following models were developed:

$$COD = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG + \beta_2 BS + \beta_3(ESG \times BS) + \beta_4 Fsize + \beta_5 Leverage + \beta_6 Growth + \beta_7 Fage + \beta_8 Listage + \sum \beta_i Yeareffects + \sum \beta_j Industryeffects + u \dots \text{ (1)}$$

$$COD = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG + \beta_2 BI + \beta_3(ESG \times BI) + \beta_4 Fsize + \beta_5 Leverage + \beta_6 Growth + \beta_7 Fage + \beta_8 Listage + \sum \beta_i Yeareffects + \sum \beta_j Industryeffects + u \dots \text{ (2)}$$

Furthermore, to empirically validate H4 and H5, we developed models 3–10. Following the approach of Dawson (2014), Goodwin-Stewart and Kent (2006), and Liu and McConnell (2013), we employ the three-way interaction approach to investigate whether board diversity strengthens the negative influence of BS and BI on the relationship between ESG and COD. Models 3 and 4 are presented as a sample of the three-way interaction models.

$$COD = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG + \beta_2 BS + \beta_3 BAD + \beta_4(ESG \times BS) + \beta_5(ESG \times BAD) + \beta_6(BAD \times BS) + \beta_7(ESG \times BS \times BAD) + \beta_8 Fsize + \beta_9 Leverage + \beta_{10} Growth + \beta_{11} Fage + \beta_{12} Listage + \sum \beta_i Yeareffects + \sum \beta_j Industryeffects + u \dots \text{ (3)}$$

$$COD = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ESG + \beta_2 BI + \beta_3 BAD + \beta_4(ESG \times BI) + \beta_5(ESG \times BAD) + \beta_6(BAD \times BI) + \beta_7(ESG \times BI \times BAD) + \beta_8 Fsize + \beta_9 Leverage + \beta_{10} Growth + \beta_{11} Fage + \beta_{12} Listage + \sum \beta_i Yeareffects + \sum \beta_j Industryeffects + u \dots \text{ (4)}$$

As discussed earlier, we have measured board diversity comprehensively using four dimensions, i.e., age, gender, education, and nationality. Therefore, BAD will be replaced with (i) BGD in models 5 and 6, (ii) BED in models 7 and 8, and (iii) OVR in models 9 and 10 to capture the three-way interaction for each dimension of diversity.

### Panel Regression Results and Discussion

Table 3 reports the 2SLS and two-step GMM results. Several prior studies have used these techniques for statistical estimation as they provide robust and reliable results (Khan et al., 2021; Semadeni et al., 2014). We also performed several diagnostic checks before our final estimation that include VIFs, Chow test, normality, heteroscedasticity, autocorrelation, and Breusch-Pagan LM test.

The results reported in Table 3 suggest that ESG performance is negatively and significantly associated with COD in all the models which is consistent with the agency theory and existing literature (Eliwa et al., 2021; Raimo et al., 2021). Therefore, we find support for H1. The result implies that ESG performance reduces the information asymmetry and signals the credibility and commitment of management to lenders, therefore, lenders consider these firms sustainable and offer lower COD. Our finding is consistent with the view that lenders enhance their reputation by financing firms with sound ESG performance and using it as a marketing tool (Weber et al., 2010).

Furthermore, the two-way interaction terms (ESG x BS) and (ESG x BI) reported in Table 3 suggest that BS and BI moderate the relationship between ESG and COD. Therefore, we find support for H2 and H3. These findings are very unique. Previous literature suggests a negative relationship between ESG and COD (Raimo et al., 2021) but no prior study investigated the moderating role of BS and BI on the relationship between ESG and COD. These findings support the viewpoint that large and independent boards suffer from free rider problems and coordination costs. Furthermore, large and independent boards delay decision-making as they suffer from communication gaps and egoistic behavior (Ararat, Orbay & Yurtoglu, 2010). Moreover, outside directors do not have comprehensive knowledge about core business operations therefore, strategic decision-making is delayed as it takes longer to develop a consensus among board members. Therefore, we argue that firms with large and independent boards that delay strategic decisions and suffer from communication gaps do not benefit from ESG performance and lenders would perceive it as a negative signal increasing COD. The results are consistent with both statistical techniques.

Table 3 also reports the results for the control variables used in the study. The results suggest that Fsize is insignificant while leverage is positively related to COD. The positive relationship between leverage and COD implies that highly leveraged firms are more risky and have high COD. Growth, Fage, and Listage are positively related to COD in some models. This suggests that lenders associate higher COD with older firms as they have inferior growth potential and unfavorable long-term prospects. The control variables’ results follow a similar pattern but are not reported in other tables for brevity.

The variables of interest in these models are the three-way interaction terms such as (ESG x BS x BAD), (ESG x BI x BAD), and so on. The results suggest BS and BI negatively influence the relationship between ESG and COD in the presence of board diversity. Thus, we find support for H4 and H5. The results remain consistent across all dimensions of diversity (i.e. age, gender, education, and nationality) and both statistical techniques. These results are very unique as the three-way interaction of board diversity, BS/BI, and ESG has not been reported in the literature. The novel results are quite interesting and imply the following. First, board performance is not only dependent on board size and independence but also its diversity. Several studies also suggest that diverse boards contribute more efficiently towards monitoring and strategic decision-making (Beji et al., 2021; Harjoto et al., 2019; Issa & Zaid, 2021). Second, we argue that large and independent boards with members from diverse backgrounds perform better and support strong ESG performance. Third, some studies suggest that large boards tend to suffer from communication gaps, groupthink, and free-rider problems (Ararat, Orbay & Yurtoglu, 2010). We argue that these problems would be resolved in the presence of board diversity as it helps in breaking groupthink and brings new insight to the boardroom. Therefore, we believe that board diversity overcomes the communication and coordination problems in large boards (Harjoto et al., 2019). Fourth, the confidence and trust of lenders and other stakeholders would be enhanced in the presence of diverse boards. Consequently, lenders would perceive such firms as less risky and offer lower COD on financing. Fifth, board diversity, large and independent boards, and high ESG performance would complement each other in reducing COD.

### Environmentally Sensitive vs. Environmentally Non-sensitive Firms

The main results of our study suggest that firms incur a lower COD if they have sound ESG performance. It may be argued that ESG performance is only useful for firms that belong to environmentally sensitive industries and other firms are not able to extract such benefits. In order to test this, we divided our full sample into two sub-samples, i.e., Panel A and Panel B. Panel A includes firms that belong to environmentally sensitive industries such as metal, mining, chemicals, oil, gas, paper, and utilities (Radhouane et al., 2020) while Panel B includes all other firms. The results from 2SLS reported in Table 6 suggest that ESG performance has a negative association with the COD of firms from both environmentally sensitive and non-sensitive industries. However, ESG performance has a more profound influence in lowering the COD of firms belonging to the environmentally sensitive group. Thus, we argue that environmentally sensitive firms would benefit more from sound ESG performance as compared to environmentally non-sensitive firms.

## Conclusion

This study investigates whether the BS and BI influence the relationship between ESG performance and COD. Furthermore, this study analyzes whether board diversity complements BS and BI in strengthening the negative relationship between ESG and COD. We used a dataset of non-financial companies listed on the Chinese stock exchanges and employed the 2SLS and two-step GMM estimation approaches. The results suggest that ESG performance is negatively associated with COD and BS and BI weaken the negative influence of ESG on COD. In addition, the results suggest that board diversity complements BS and BI in strengthening the negative relationship between ESG and COD. Therefore, we argue that the board effectiveness is not optimal when it only has a large size and independent directors. Rather, we believe that a diverse board with an adequate size and independent directors will deliver better governance and provide numerous benefits to the firms’ stakeholders.

Our unique findings are robust to statistical issues such as endogeneity and alternative measurements. Furthermore, we find that ESG performance is important for firms operating in both environmentally sensitive and non-sensitive industries. Moreover, we found that environmentally sensitive firms with high ESG performance tend to have a lower COD as compared to non-sensitive firms. In addition, the study reports that the negative impact of ESG on COD is more pronounced in the 13th five-year plan as the Chinese government’s focus has shifted to greening and environmental safety.

### Theoretical Implications

The findings of our study provide several theoretical implications. First, the study extends the existing literature on the relationship between ESG and COD by investigating the moderating roles of BS and BI using the two-way interaction approach (Raimo et al., 2021). The results suggest that BS and BI weaken the negative relationship between ESG performance and COD (Cucari et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2021; Orazalin, 2019). Second, we extend the literature by providing evidence that board diversity complements board size and independence in strengthening the relationship between ESG and COD using the three-way interaction approach (Shaukat et al., 2016; Giannarakis, 2014; Manita et al., 2018). Third, our results indicate that sound ESG performance benefits both environmentally sensitive and non-sensitive firms in terms of lower COD. However, environmentally sensitive firms with sound ESG performance experience a significantly lower COD than their non-sensitive counterparts. Fourth, the study has implications for both the agency and stakeholders theories (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Spence, 1973).

### Practical Implications

The results have several practical implications for policymakers, managers, and investors. First, policymakers should make it mandatory for firms to disclose ESG information as it provides numerous benefits to the stakeholders and mitigates agency problems. Second, policymakers and stakeholders should exert pressure on firms to enhance board diversity for effective strategic decisions that are environmentally and socially friendly. Third, policymakers should enforce stringent regulations for firms involved in hazardous activities which imperil the environment and society. Fourth, firms seeking to improve their ESG performance are encouraged to enhance board diversity. Fifth, our results imply that ESG performance has vast benefits (e.g. lower COD) which are not limited to environmentally sensitive industries. Therefore, managers from all industries should strive to improve their firms’ ESG performance. Sixth, investors should carefully evaluate the board characteristics of firms including diversity before making investment decisions.

### Limitations and Future Research Recommendations

This study also has some limitations. We have used the dataset of Chinese non-financial companies over a limited time duration therefore, the findings may not be generalized in the context of other countries. Future research may investigate whether other firm characteristics can moderate the association between ESG performance and COD using cross-country data.
