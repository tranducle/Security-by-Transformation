# How do ESG controversies moderate the nexus between ESG performance and cost of capital? Evidence from European listed companies

**Filip Hampl and Dagmar Vágnerová Linnertová**
*Department of Finance, Faculty of Economics and Administration, Masaryk University, Brno, Czech Republic*

**Abstract**
**Purpose** – This study aims to investigate the effect of ESG controversies and their moderating role in ESG performance and the cost of equity and overall, short-term and long-term debt capital relationship in European listed companies.
**Design/methodology/approach** – The study employs two-way fixed effects panel linear regression models on the balanced longitudinal dataset of 231 European non-financial companies listed in the MSCI Europe Index in 2017–2022. To check the robustness, the study utilises the fixed effects logistic regression models with heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors.
**Findings** – The study reveals the significant effect of ESG performance (negative) and ESG controversies (negative) on the cost of debt capital and the substantial moderating effect of ESG controversies (positive). Additionally, it provides empirical evidence of the crossover moderating effect of ESG controversies in ESG performance and cost of equity relationship.
**Research limitations/implications** – The findings contribute to corporate practice and empirically support legitimacy and stakeholder theories.
**Practical implications** – Companies can utilise the results to proactively enhance their internal policies and behaviour to align with ESG practices and avoid ESG controversies, which will translate into reduced equity capital costs for shareholders and a lower cost of debt capital charged by creditors.
**Originality/value** – To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively investigate the influence of ESG controversies and their moderating effect in the context of the equity and debt capital cost for European listed companies.

**Keywords** ESG performance, Controversies, Cost of equity, Cost of debt, Financing cost
**Paper type** Research paper

## 1. Introduction
Environmental, social and governance (ESG) controversies encompass news on a wide range of negative topics related to a company’s impact on the environment, society and internal governance, such as environmental violations, industrial accidents, labour disputes, ethical concerns, anti-competition behaviour and product-harm scandals. ESG controversies provide shareholders, investors, creditors and other stakeholders with valuable information on how well a company’s activities adhere to ESG principles. Consequently, Svanberg *et al.* (2022) argued that ESG controversies complement ESG assessment by providing more objective information sources and are therefore useful proxies for non-compliance with ESG practices. ESG controversies not only highlight a company’s harmful practices regarding ethical and sustainability responsibilities but also have tangible consequences for financial performance (Nirino *et al.*, 2021), market value (Mardini, 2022) or perceived financial risk (Galletta and Mazzù, 2023), which might affect the financing cost.

The link between ESG controversies and the cost of capital can be conceptually outlined using two primary overlapping theories: legitimacy theory and stakeholder theory. Legitimacy theory assumes the existence of a social contract between a company and the society in which stakeholders’ expectations and values are embedded, with compliance with the contract establishing the legitimacy of a company’s operations (Patten, 1991). Stakeholders are increasingly demanding sustainable and responsible business practices due to ongoing climate change, depletion of natural resources, societal injustices and ethical policies (Amel-Zadeh and Serafeim, 2018). Therefore, following the legitimacy theory, companies improve their ESG performance to meet stakeholders’ expectations and maintain a perception of legitimacy. ESG controversies point to the failure of a company to comply with ESG practices and might be considered a breach of this social contract. When stakeholders are not satisfied with the legitimacy of a company’s operations, they will revoke their support, which might manifest as limiting access to capital through higher costs (Deegan, 2002). A similar conclusion can be reached through stakeholder theory, which posits that companies consider their stakeholders’ sustainability interests and concerns and take corresponding ESG actions to advance stakeholder wealth (Harrison and Wicks, 2013). In this way, companies build and maintain their reputation and motivate stakeholders to provide them with resources essential to success (Shin *et al.*, 2023). When a company experiences ESG controversies, it might increase stakeholders’ concerns about possible lawsuits, loss of reputation and the company’s long-term viability (Fauser and Utz, 2021). Consequently, stakeholders perceive higher risk and become less willing to provide resources to the company, which is reflected in an increase in the cost of capital. However, neither theory offers a clear answer on the moderating effect of ESG controversies on the relationship between ESG performance and cost of capital, as ESG controversies might interact with ESG performance.

The effect of ESG practices on the cost of equity and debt capital has given rise to different academic views. Studies by Albarrak *et al.* (2019), Salvi *et al.* (2020), and Mulchandani *et al.* (2022) have focussed on the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of equity. These studies argue that ESG disclosure reduces information asymmetry between the company and investors, enhancing transparency and enabling investors to evaluate risk, which in turn lowers the cost of equity. Furthermore, Raimo *et al.* (2021) and Salvi *et al.* (2022) confirm that lenders perceive a higher level of ESG disclosure as an indication of lower risk, allowing companies to obtain less expensive debt financing. Other studies have analysed the effect of ESG performance on companies’ cost of equity (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Chen *et al.*, 2023; Mio *et al.*, 2023) and cost of debt (Ge and Liu, 2015; Apergis *et al.*, 2022; Arora and Sharma, 2022) finding it more directly associated to reductions in the cost of capital. Ng and Rezaee (2015) found a negative association between ESG performance and the cost of equity, noting that only the environmental and governance pillars of ESG performance contribute to this relationship. Chen *et al.* (2023) and Mio *et al.* (2023) found that ESG performance helps reduce the cost of equity by reducing investor perceptions of risk. Similarly, Ge and Liu (2015), Apergis *et al.* (2022) and Arora and Sharma (2022) have confirmed the significant negative relationship between ESG performance and the cost of borrowing as it enhances borrower’s risk profile by sustainability-associated risks. Furthermore, Tang (2022) documented that investors’ heterogeneous beliefs have a significant moderating effect on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity, and Eliwa *et al.* (2021) suggested that the relationship between ESG performance and cost of debt is moderated by ESG disclosure levels.

However, little academic attention has been paid to the relationship between ESG controversies and the cost of equity and debt capital, despite their informational value for stakeholders (especially shareholders and creditors) in terms of a company’s adherence to ESG principles and potential increases in risk due to lawsuits or reputational damage. Most studies addressing ESG controversies have focussed on their influence on financial performance revealing negative ESG controversies are negatively associated with a company’s value due to uncertainty about its prospects and future potential (Aouadi and Marsat, 2018; Nirino *et al.*, 2021). Other studies have examined company risk, highlighting the significant moderating effect of ESG controversies on the relationship between ESG performance and financial risk (Shakil, 2021; Galletta and Mazzù, 2023), and finally, Schiemann and Tietmeyer (2022) suggested that a higher incidence of ESG controversies resulted in lower forecasting accuracy.

As far as we know, only La Rosa and Bernini (2022) have investigated the impact of ESG controversies and their moderating effect on individual ESG pillars and the cost of equity relationship. Compared to their study, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the influence of ESG controversies on both the cost of equity and debt capital, measuring the cost of equity using a risk-adjusted approach based on capital market dynamics and present a visual representation of the crossover moderating effect based on slope analysis. Furthermore, we investigate 2017–2022, whereas La Rosa and Bernini examined 2005–2012.

Although legitimacy and stakeholder theory can rationalise the effect of ESG controversies on the cost of capital, empirical evidence is lacking to support theoretical conclusions. Moreover, little is known regarding whether or the degree to which ESG controversies moderate the relationship between ESG performance and debt and equity capital cost. To fill the research gap, the study aims to investigate the effect of ESG controversies and their moderating role in the ESG performance and cost of equity and debt capital relationship in European listed companies in 2017–2022. In addition, we break down the overall cost of debt and pay particular attention to the cost of short-term and long-term debt in our analysis. We focus on the European market as European stakeholders emphasise the importance of sustainable practices and are regarded as pioneers in sustainability regulation. Thus, ESG controversies could significantly influence stakeholders’ willingness to provide companies with capital.

The study contributes to the existing corporate finance literature by highlighting the nexus between ESG controversies and the cost of equity and the cost of overall, short- and long-term debt. In addition, it reveals both the presence and the magnitude of the moderating effect of ESG controversies on ESG performance and financing cost. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively investigate the influence of ESG controversies on the cost of equity and debt capital in European listed companies using consistent methodology for both costs. The results offer empirical evidence that ESG controversies scores have a crossover moderating effect on the cost of equity (both main effects remain insignificant), with the negative conditional effect statistically significant only for companies with excessive adverse events and scandals related to ESG practices. Furthermore, they suggest that higher ESG performance and lower ESG controversies scores significantly contribute to lower cost of debt capital. There is a significant positive moderating effect of ESG controversies scores on the cost of debt capital and this effect is more substantial for short-term debt costs.

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides a literature review and hypotheses development. Section 3 describes the methodology. Section 4 presents and discusses the results, and Section 5 provides a robustness check of our results. Section 6 concludes the paper.

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis development
Both the legitimacy and stakeholder theories can be used to rationalise the link between ESG performance and companies’ financial performance, value or cost of equity and debt capital. Higher ESG performance legitimises business activities, fulfils stakeholders’ expectations and, thus, increasing stakeholders’ engagement in companies’ success (Shin *et al.*, 2023). ESG practices are significant for European stakeholders who have more interest and awareness of ESG issues and the concept of sustainability (Arnell, 2023), and prefer to allocate their assets to ESG compliant companies (Ahmed *et al.*, 2019; Eliwa *et al.*, 2021) thus motivating companies to adopt sustainable practices. Furthermore, compared to other regions, Europe is regarded as a pioneer in sustainability practices, especially regarding protecting the environment and the fight against climate change (Parker *et al.*, 2017). The European emphasis on ESG is also supported by regulatory frameworks including the European Green Deal or the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive which have influenced regulation and sustainability standards worldwide (Redondo Alamillos and de Mariz, 2022). Due to the importance of ESG practices for European stakeholders, the presence of ESG controversies, including law violations, might significantly influence European stakeholders’ willingness to provide companies with capital.

Several studies have documented empirical results showing that strong ESG practices lead to better corporate financial performance or company value (Eccles *et al.*, 2014; Velte, 2017; Ahmad *et al.*, 2021; Mardini, 2022), which is reflected in other company characteristics, including riskiness, prestige and, consequently, opportunities for obtaining capital. Velte (2017) found that in German companies, ESG performance positively impacted return on assets but not company performance as measured by Tobin’s Q. Nirino *et al.* (2021) revealed the opposite result for European listed companies, with ESG practices contributing significantly to Tobin’s Q but not accounting (return on assets and return on equity) indicators. Mardini (2022), employing a dataset of companies from 35 countries, documented the vital role of ESG factors in enhancing financial performance and accounting measures. Ahmad *et al.* (2021) identified an overall positive impact of total ESG on UK companies’ market value and earnings per share, while individual scores for the pillars of ESG also positively affected financial performance. Maury (2022), using 23 companies in developed markets, confirmed a positive relationship between ESG improvement and future profitability and Al Amosh and Khatib (2023), focussing on company performance during the COVID-19 period, concluded that ESG limited the negative effect of the pandemic on companies’ financial performance.

### 2.1 ESG and cost of equity and debt capital
The prior literature has also examined the association between ESG performance and companies’ equity and debt capital costs. Multiple studies have evidenced the lower cost of equity for companies with higher ESG performance, including El Ghoul *et al.* (2011) for US companies, Ahmed *et al.* (2019) for UK companies and Chen *et al.* (2023) for China’s A-share companies. Ng and Rezaee (2015) and Mio *et al.* (2023) investigated overall ESG performance as well as performance in its individual ESG pillars. While Ng and Rezaee (2015) showed that only the environmental and governance pillars reduce the cost of equity, Mio *et al.* (2023) found a significant negative association between overall ESG performance and the governance pillar with the cost of equity capital. In addition to ESG performance, the studies also focussed on the influence of ESG disclosure. While ESG performance measures the implementation of ESG governance policies and practices within the company, the ESG disclosure indicator measures the level of ESG information disclosure. Albarrak *et al.* (2019), Salvi *et al.* (2020) and Mulchandani *et al.* (2022) documented that more extensive disclosure of ESG practices contributes to lower cost of equity, as it reduces information asymmetry among the company and investors, enhancing transparency and leading to higher investor confidence. Albarrak *et al.* (2019) developed a measure of carbon information (part of the ESG environmental pillar) reported via companies’ Twitter accounts and confirmed that companies disseminating carbon-related information tend to have a lower cost of equity. Similarly, Salvi *et al.* (2020) showed intellectual capital disclosure levels (part of the ESG social pillar) were negatively associated with the cost of equity.

Other studies have analysed the impact of ESG performance on the cost of debt and obtained mixed results. Huang *et al.* (2018) found that improving ESG performance reduces the cost of bonds for Chinese companies and Arora and Sharma (2022) supported these findings in the context of Indian companies. Ge and Liu (2015) presented the negative effect of strong ESG performance on the cost of debt for US companies and highlighted that bondholders consider both ESG strengths and concerns. In addition, Apergis *et al.* (2022), in their review of S&P 500 companies, documented that overall ESG performance and all individual ESG pillars negatively impact bond yields, proving the primary market’s efficiency in incorporating ESG risks in bond pricing. Eliwa *et al.* (2021) revealed that European companies benefit from higher levels of ESG performance and disclosure through reduced borrowing costs. Moreover, the effect of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt is just as significant as ESG performance, suggesting creditors place the same importance on both ESG performance and transparency resulting from ESG disclosure.

On the other hand, Menz (2010) found that ESG performance only marginally impacted the cost of debt for European companies, while Hoepner *et al.* (2016) demonstrated similar results for an international sample. In focussing on US listed companies, Erragragui (2018) documented that only select elements of ESG performance significantly influence creditors’ perceptions of company risks and are subsequently reflected in the cost of debt capital. According to Erragragui (2018), only environmental concerns increase the cost of debt, while high performance levels in both environmental and governance reduce debt costs.

Although our study focusses on the effect of ESG controversies and their moderating role, we will also examine the main effect of ESG performance on the cost of equity and debt capital to support the conclusions of previous studies. Therefore, we posit the following hypotheses:

*   **H1a.** ESG performance is significantly and negatively associated with companies’ cost of equity capital.
*   **H1b.** ESG performance is significantly and negatively associated with companies’ cost of debt capital.

### 2.2 ESG controversies
ESG controversies disrupt a company’s legitimacy and stakeholder expectations, introduce uncertainty about the company’s financial performance (Melinda and Wardhani, 2020; Nirino *et al.*, 2021) and future prospects (Schiemann and Tietmeyer, 2022) and threaten the company’s reputation. Melinda and Wardhani (2020), investigating Asian listed companies, and Nirino *et al.* (2021), focussing on European listed companies, evidenced the importance of disclosing ESG controversies for investors, finding a statistically significant and negative relationship between ESG controversies and a company’s Tobin’s Q. In contrast, Aouadi and Marsat (2018) concluded that ESG controversies have no significant direct effect on financial performance measured by Tobin’s Q. Furthermore, Nirino *et al.* (2021) examined the link between ESG controversies and accounting measures (return on assets and return on equity) but found no significant effect. Regarding perceived risk resulting from ESG controversies, Shakil (2021) found ESG controversies had no significant effect on company total and systematic risk, whilst Galletta and Mazzù (2023) confirmed that a lower number of ESG controversies reduces risk and contributes to lower risk-weighted assets and higher Z-scores of banks.

Although the perceived risks and uncertainty from negative news about ESG practices might be reflected in higher financing costs, there has been scant empirical examination of this area in the literature. Gao *et al.* (2022) found that positive media reports on ESG practices significantly reduce a company’s cost of debt financing by enhancing reputation in supply chains, reducing financial risk and increasing transparency. Becchetti *et al.* (2023), investigating US listed companies, added that media coverage related to irresponsible ESG behaviour (measured by RepRisk Indicator) sends investors a credible negative signal contributing to the higher cost of equity capital. Considering the Refinitiv ESG controversies score, La Rosa and Bernini (2022) illustrated that environmental controversies lead to an increase in the cost of equity capital for European listed companies, particularly in countries with stronger market regulation due to the higher expectations investors have for more stringent oversight.

To broaden the empirical research and based on the theoretically pronounced effect of ESG controversies on the cost of equity and cost of debt capital, we set the following hypotheses:

*   **H2a.** ESG controversies are significantly and positively associated with companies’ cost of equity capital.
*   **H2b.** ESG controversies are significantly and positively associated with companies’ cost of debt capital.

### 2.3 The moderating effect of ESG controversies
The debate continues regarding how ESG performance impacts a company’s financial performance and, consequently, its financing cost. Nirino *et al.* (2021) found a significant negative moderating effect of ESG practices on the relationship between ESG controversies and financial performance. However, the authors noted that ESG practices are not sufficient to improve financial performance as they only marginally alleviate the negative effect of ESG controversies on performance. Chen and Xie (2022) evidenced a positive impact of ESG disclosure on financial performance for Chinese non-financial companies, finding that the positive effect is moderated by capital market participants with an ESG interest. Moreover, Shakil (2021) demonstrated that ESG controversies have a substantial moderating effect on the connection between ESG and financial risk. Regarding the cost of capital, previous literature suggests that various company characteristics might moderate ESG influence regarding these costs. Tang (2022) unveiled that investors’ heterogeneous beliefs moderate the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity in Chinese A-share listed companies. Bacha *et al.* (2021) demonstrated the importance of the interaction between audit quality and ESG, which negatively affects the cost of debt for French listed companies. The authors concluded that creditors consider quality auditors and ESG performance as factors that enable companies to enhance their value and avoid misallocation of resources, and are thus more likely to reward them with lower-cost debt. Eliwa *et al.* (2021) examined the moderating effect of ESG disclosure on the association between ESG performance and the cost of debt. Their results suggested that ESG disclosure is a substitute for ESG performance and, therefore, companies with low ESG performance tend to increase ESG disclosure to reduce their debt cost.

However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, only La Rosa and Bernini (2022) investigated the moderating role of ESG controversies in terms of equity cost in European listed companies in period 2005–2012. The authors revealed the statistically significant moderating effect of environmental and social controversies, suggesting that the negative impact of environmental (social) controversies on the cost of equity decreases as environmental (social) performance improves. Nevertheless, the moderating effect of ESG controversies on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt remains unexamined by La Rosa and Bernini (2022). To fill this research gap, which was also noted by Shakil (2021), who suggested that future research investigate the moderating effect of ESG controversies on the cost of equity and cost of debt, we formulate hypotheses as follows:

*   **H3a.** ESG controversies have a significant moderating effect on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity capital.
*   **H3b.** ESG controversies have a significant moderating effect on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt capital.

## 3. Data and methodology
### 3.1 Sample and data
The previous research has mostly focussed on US, Asian or international datasets. However, there is a lack of studies concerning the European region, where significant emphasis has been placed on sustainable policies in recent years. Our initial research sample comprised 432 large and mid-cap companies listed in the MSCI Europe Index capturing companies across 15 developed European countries. To ensure sample reliability and comparability, we excluded 89 financial companies (including real estate investment trusts) from the initial sample due to their different regulatory framework and accounting standards. Financial institutions follow different rules for preparing their financial statements and are more strictly regulated. This means that accounting and ESG measures are not comparable with non-financial companies (Soana, 2024), which would lead to a distortion of the results. We also omitted companies with missing data to obtain a balanced panel as well as companies that were not listed throughout the whole examined period of 2017–2022. We chose the period with respect to data availability, particularly ESG controversies scores and accounting data, to examine a sufficiently large sample for the balanced panel purposes. In previous years, more than 30% of ESG controversies data from the selected companies was missing. The final sample consists of 231 non-financial listed companies in 15 developed European countries which are part of the nine industries classified by the Industry Classification Benchmark (ICB). All included companies present their financial statements according to IFRS. The sample size is comparable to previous studies such as Nirino *et al.* (2021) whose sample consisted of 356 companies, or La Rosa and Bernini (2022) who included 151 companies. The country and industry distribution of the final sample is presented in Table 1. All data are obtained from the Refinitiv Eikon database, which has been widely used in previous studies (Eliwa *et al.*, 2021; Agnese and Giacomini, 2023). In addition, Refinitiv (2022) calculates ESG data in a standardised form. To avoid the influence of extreme values, all continuous variables are winsorised at the 1 and 99% quantiles.

### 3.2 Variables specification
Cost of capital refers to the cost a company incurs in raising funds to finance its business activities. As it encompasses the cost of raising equity capital from shareholders and the interest cost of borrowing money from creditors, we estimate four dependent variables: cost of equity (*COE*), overall cost of debt (*COD*), cost of short-term debt (*STCOD*) and cost of long-term debt (*LTCOD*). *COE* represents the return a company theoretically pays its shareholders and is calculated using the CAPM formula by multiplying the market’s equity risk premium with the beta of the stock plus an inflation-adjusted risk-free rate. *COD* reflects the marginal cost to the company of issuing new debt, calculated as the weighted cost of short-term debt and weighted cost of long-term debt based on the one-year (for *STCOD*) and 10-year points (for *LTCOD*) of an appropriate credit curve. All dependent variables are calculated by the major ESG data provider Refinitiv.

Consistent with our research goal, we focus on two independent variables: ESG score (*ESG*) and ESG controversies score (*CONT*), both calculated by Refinitiv (2022). *ESG* measures a company’s ESG performance based on disclosed information and is increasingly considered in business management and financial investments (Amel-Zadeh and Serafeim, 2018). *ESG* ranges between 0 and 100, with increasing values reflecting a higher company’s ESG performance. *CONT* is calculated based on 23 topics related to ESG controversy, considering the frequency of controversies surrounding ESG issues as well as adverse events disclosed in the global media. *CONT* reflects both the negative event occurrence in a given year and new developments related to the event in the following year. We follow the Refinitiv (2022) measurement of *CONT* ranging from 0 for the most controversial companies to 100 for those with no controversies.

Based on previous studies, we include six control variables capable of significantly influencing the cost of equity and debt capital. We include company size (*SIZE*) measured by the natural logarithm of total assets (Lorca *et al.*, 2011; Raimo *et al.*, 2021). As Lorca *et al.* (2011) noted, larger companies are characterised by less information asymmetry, higher resiliency to shocks and the expectation that they can face economies of scale in debt costs. We further employ the company’s profitability measures: return on assets (*ROA*), calculated as net income scaled by total assets (Gerwanski, 2020) and dummy variable *LOSS* reflecting the company’s losses from its operating activities (Ng and Rezaee, 2015). More profitable companies are expected to have lower uncertainty for investors and lenders and greater capacity to service the debt than less profitable ones (Raimo *et al.*, 2021). We also use leverage ratio (*LEV*), calculated as total debts to total assets, to control financial structure differences (Gerwanski, 2020; Eliwa *et al.*, 2021). Less indebted companies are expected to have better solvency and lower risk for investors and lenders than companies with higher levels of *LEV*. All these control variables also proxy for the company’s credit risk, where larger *SIZE*, higher *ROA*, lower *LEV* and no losses from operating activities reflect lower credit risk and, thus, reduce the cost of equity and debt capital.

Company age also plays a role in acquiring equity and loans. Older companies can adjust their governance structure, consolidate their business activities, build a strong asset base, gain an attractive reputation and acquire experience in financial markets. Therefore, we control company age (*AGE*) as a natural logarithm of total years from the company’s incorporation. Finally, we include accruals quality as a proxy for the financial reporting quality (*FRQ*), which is an important influencing factor for financing, as it helps reduce information asymmetry and enables stakeholders to effectively monitor the company’s financial position and performance (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Gao *et al.*, 2020). We calculate *FRQ* as an absolute value of discretionary accruals following the modified Jones model proposed by Dechow *et al.* (1995) for each ICB non-financial industry year. The higher values of discretionary accruals indicate poorer financial reporting quality.

To examine collinearity problems, we calculated the correlation matrix and concluded that multicollinearity is not a serious threat to our analyses.

### 3.3 Methods
Given the nature of the dataset, we employed panel linear regression to assess the developed hypotheses. We performed a Hausman test to choose between random effects (RE) and fixed effects (FE) panel regression for our model estimation. The results of the Hausman test suggested that the FE estimator was more appropriate for our sample, as the null hypothesis that individual random effects are exogenous was rejected ($p < 0.001$). Consistently, the result of the Chow test suggested that FE estimators were preferred over the pooled ordinary least squares approach. Since both the cross-sectional and time series dimensions of data were involved, we used the Breusch–Pagan test to assess the presence of individual, time or the combination of both fixed effects. The results indicated the combination of both effects in our models. Therefore, we employed two-way FE regression models. Using a combination of fixed effects allows us to control unobservable variables that differ across industries but are time-invariant (industry FE), as well as those that are time-variant but constant across industries (time FE). The importance of eliminating the industry-level biases in the context of ESG is emphasised by Kotsantonis and Serafeim (2019) who argue that the range of ESG performance observed is not uniform across industries resulting from the way how ESG scores are calculated. Concurrently, this method prevents the estimated coefficients from being biased by economic fluctuations across years that might influence the ESG performance of companies.

Consistent with Yuan *et al.* (2022) and Agnese and Giacomini (2023), we addressed endogeneity concerns by employing one-year lagged independent and control variables in the estimating models. The underlying rationale for this approach is that ESG performance and ESG controversies take a certain amount of time to affect investors’ and creditors’ decisions on funds provision as the capital-raising and money-borrowing processes (e.g. new bonds issuance, arranging bank loans) take longer. Moreover, the model using one-year lagged variables resulted in the lowest Akaike Information Criterion compared to other lag durations.

To assess the main effects and moderating effect of ESG controversies in the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity and debt capital, we developed the following three regression models for each dependent variable. The first two models capture the main effects of our dependent variables, and the third adds an interaction term to test the significance of the moderating effect of *CONT*.

(1) $DV_{it} = \beta_1 ESG_{i,t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^6 \beta_j CONTROL_{j,i,t-1} + \mu_l + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$

(2) $DV_{it} = \beta_1 ESG_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 CONT_{i,t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^6 \beta_j CONTROL_{j,i,t-1} + \mu_l + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$

(3) $DV_{it} = \beta_1 ESG_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 CONT_{i,t-1} + \beta_3 (ESG_{i,t-1} \times CONT_{i,t-1}) + \sum_{j=1}^6 \beta_j CONTROL_{j,i,t-1} + \mu_l + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$

where *DV* represents the appropriate dependent variables (*COE*, *COD*, *STCOD* or *LTCOD*), *CONTROL* the control variables, $\mu_l$ industry fixed effects and $\delta_t$ time fixed effects. The variable subscripts $i$ and $t$ denote company $i$ in year $t$.

## 4. Results and discussion
The computed descriptive statistics presented in Table 2 document relatively low average *COE* (7.88%) and *COD* (1.58%) during 2017–2022; minimum values even demonstrate that some companies managed to finance their activities at negative costs. However, there was a substantial increase in the cost of capital in 2022 as a result of the growth of the basic interest rates set by monetary authorities which was a response to high inflation driven by the adverse effects of the Russo–Ukrainian conflict. While the average *COE* was 7.52% and the *COD* 1.22% in 2017–2021, in 2022, the average *COE* increased to 10.28% and the *COD* to 3.38%. Despite the increase in interest rates, debt financing remains a markedly cheaper way of covering companies’ business activities. Table 2 demonstrates that European listed companies largely implement more ESG practices in their business activities. The average *ESG* for the entire period reached 72.12 out of 100 points. The companies’ emphasis on ESG practices is evidenced by a 15.54% average improvement in *ESG* over the examined period. In contrast, *CONT* values remained relatively stable over time; an average value of 82.30 out of 100 points indicates a low level of scandals, negative events or ongoing situations in which a company’s activities might have negative ESG impacts.

Table 3 presents the results of FE regression models for cost of equity (models 1–3) and cost of debt (models 4–6). The results of models 1–3 suggest that ESG performance and ESG controversies have statistically insignificant effects on a company’s ability to raise equity capital from investors and creditors at lower costs. Thus, H1a and H2a are not supported. Our findings show that higher ESG performance in large and mid-cap European listed companies does not translate into a lower cost of equity. This contrasts with US companies as evidenced by El Ghoul *et al.* (2011) and Ng and Rezaee (2015), and Chinese companies investigated by Chen *et al.* (2023). Ahmed *et al.* (2019) also found ESG performance had a significant impact on *COE* for companies traded in the UK which, compared to continental European countries, is market-oriented. On the other hand, banks in bank-oriented continental markets emphasise sustainable financing over equity providers and have developed comprehensive ESG assessment strategies for granting debt capital. In the context of ESG controversies, our results are in line with La Rosa and Bernini (2022) who found that environmental controversies had an impact on *COE* but also found no significant effect of *CONT*.

Although the main effects remain insignificant, the results of model 3 indicate the significance of the crossover moderating effect of ESG controversies in the relationship between ESG performance and cost of equity ($p = 0.070$), so we accept H3a. A significant crossover moderating effect means that the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity, conditional on low level of the moderator variable (*CONT*), is significantly different from the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity at a high level of the moderator, as shown in Figure 1 (plot A). Since the crossover effect suggests a negative relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity for companies with a low ESG controversies score, and a positive relationship for those with a high ESG controversies score, we conducted further investigation of conditional effects at different levels of the moderator based on Cheung *et al.* (2022). The results revealed that the conditional effects are significant only for very low levels of ESG controversies score up to 8.333 (the effect = -0.0004, SE = 0.0001, $p = 0.098$); for higher levels of the moderator variable, the conditional effects remain insignificant. This suggests that for companies with excessive adverse events and scandals related to ESG practices, an increase in ESG performance significantly contributes to a reduction in the cost of equity. Conversely, for companies with fewer ESG controversies, the impact of ESG performance on *COE* is not conditional upon the value of *CONT*. Our findings suggest that companies with very low levels of ESG controversies score are perceived positively by investors when improving ESG performance as they pay attention to companies’ efforts and willingness to address their past misconduct related to sustainable practices.

Our results extend the conclusions by Melinda and Wardhani (2020), who documented the significance of ESG controversies on companies’ value without taking the moderating effect into account, and La Rosa and Bernini (2022), who found the significant influence of the moderating effect on the cost of equity only in cases of environmental and social controversies.

Regarding overall debt financing, the results in Table 3 (models 4–6) suggest a statistically significant negative relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt. Companies adopting more sustainable ESG practices can obtain less expensive debt capital from creditors, which considers ESG performance information when assessing the credit risk of the borrowing company. These results are consistent with previous studies, such as Ge and Liu (2015), Eliwa *et al.* (2021) and Apergis *et al.* (2022), and support H1b. The results also evidence a negative significant influence of *CONT* on *COD*, which suggests that when a company becomes embroiled in ESG-related controversies, such as environmental violations, labour disputes or governance lapses, it leads to increased perceived credit risk and higher interest rates required by creditors. These findings support H2b and align with Gao *et al.* (2022).

Furthermore, the results support H3b on the existence of a statistically significant moderating effect of ESG controversies on the cost of debt ($p = 0.085$). Figure 1 (plot B) shows the negative relationship between ESG performance and cost of debt based on all levels of the moderator *CONT*. The slope of the regression lines becomes steeper for companies with lower ESG controversies scores, implying that companies with more ESG controversies benefit more substantially by improving ESG performance. Implementing and enhancing ESG practices for these companies mean a higher marginal reduction in the cost of overall debt financing than for companies with a lower number of ESG controversies. Our results extend the prior research documenting the significant moderating effects of ESG practices and controversies on companies’ financial performance (Aouadi and Marsat, 2018; Nirino *et al.*, 2021).

Furthermore, Table 3 presents results for *STCOD* (models 7–9) and *LTCOD* (models 10–12). As for the overall cost of debt, the results show a statistically significant influence of ESG performance (negative) and ESG controversies score (negative) on short-term and long-term debt costs. Thus, adopting ESG practices in business operations allows companies to access less expensive debt, as creditors appreciate sustainable and ethical practices and associate them with lower risks. In contrast, scandals and reported events with potentially harmful ESG impacts worsen the perception of companies in the eyes of creditors and increase the cost of debt financing. However, the estimated coefficients of the independent variables show the influence of ESG controversies on interest rates is much smaller than ESG performance. Furthermore, models 9 and 12 suggest that ESG controversies have a statistically significant moderating effect on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of short- and long-term debt. The moderating effect is positive and strongly significant for short-term debt ($\alpha = 0.01, p = 0.001$), and positive but only weakly significant for long-term debt ($\alpha = 0.1, p = 0.080$). Figure 1 (plots C and D) shows that companies with low *CONT* have steeper regression slopes than companies with better ESG reputations. This implies that an improvement in their ESG performance scores results in greater marginal reductions in the cost of both short- and long-term debt than for those with better scores. Therefore, the results support H1b, H2b and H3b.

Our findings suggest that ESG controversies play a greater role in obtaining short-term debt capital. Creditors might consider ESG controversies a potential threat to companies’ short-term profitability and cash flow due to the high degree of uncertainty surrounding the resolution and settlement of these issues. ESG controversies can also cause a short-term lowering of ESG reputation for suppliers and can damage the company’s reputation with customers, which could lead to short-term problems with the repayment of debts; therefore, creditors associate controversies with a higher risk. In the long term, companies are expected to be able to deal with most of the negative impacts of ESG controversies (e.g. recognition of provisions, changing business practices and introducing control mechanisms). Therefore, creditors do not perceive the risks related to ESG controversies (e.g. fines, lawsuits, reputation loss) as equally serious in the longer term. Additionally, ESG controversies significantly positively moderate the relationship between ESG performance and debt financing in both cases.

## 5. Robustness check
We examined the robustness of the results by all dependent variables (*COE*, *COD*, *STCOD*, *LTCOD*) binarisation at 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, and 0.80 percentile and FE logistic regression model (Stammann *et al.*, 2016) estimation for each of these percentiles for each dependent variable separately. We employed the FE logistic regression models with heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC1) standard errors according to the following equation:

(4) $FElogit : E(DV > P) = \frac{e^{\beta_1 ESG_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 CONT_{i,t-1} + \beta_3 (ESG_{i,t-1} \times CONT_{i,t-1}) + \sum_{j=1}^6 \beta_j CONTROL_{j,i,t-1} + \mu_l + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}}}{1 + e^{\beta_1 ESG_{i,t-1} + \beta_2 CONT_{i,t-1} + \beta_3 (ESG_{i,t-1} \times CONT_{i,t-1}) + \sum_{j=1}^6 \beta_j CONTROL_{j,i,t-1} + \mu_l + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}}}$

where *DV* denotes appropriate dependent variables and *P* corresponds to the percentiles.

Each set percentile signifies a different measure of a company’s cost of capital. For instance, 0.20 of a *COE* percentile means that a binarised variable *COE* takes on the value 1 if a company has a higher cost of equity than 20% of companies with the lowest cost. The results of the robustness check for the *COE* and *COD* dependent variables are presented in Table 4.

The logistic regression estimates its coefficients via maximum likelihood estimation, while the FE linear regression models use least squares for coefficient estimation. All statistically significant variables in Table 3 (models 3 and 6) are significant on at least one percentile level of our robustness check. This confirms the robustness of our findings concerning model selection. Moreover, different coefficients signify varying measures of the cost of debt and equity capital (via percentiles), but no statistically significant coefficient has changed the direction compared to significant coefficients presented in Table 3. Hence, our results are also robust to varying definitions of dependent variables.

## 6. Conclusion
The study investigated the effect of ESG controversies and the moderating role of ESG controversies in ESG performance and cost of equity and overall, short-term and long-term debt capital relationship in the European listed companies in 2017–2022. To test the developed hypotheses, we employed two-way FE panel regression models on the balanced longitudinal dataset of 231 non-financial companies listed in the MSCI Europe Index.

Our results suggested no significant effects of ESG performance or ESG controversies on the cost of equity. Although the main effects are insignificant, we provided empirical evidence of the crossover moderating effect of ESG controversies in the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity capital. Further analysis of the moderating effect indicated that the conditional effects are significant only for companies with the highest involvement in negative ESG news, malpractices or scandals. For these companies with excessively low ESG controversies scores, the results indicate that any improvement in their ESG performance is perceived positively by shareholders and leads to a lower cost of equity capital. Furthermore, we found that companies adopting and complying with ESG principles benefit from a lower cost of overall, short-term and long-term debt capital. Creditors appreciate companies’ sustainable and ethical practices when assessing credit risk; on the other hand, they perceive controversial incidents as a sign of poor ESG management, which erodes their confidence and the company’s reputation. This conclusion is consistent with both the legitimacy and stakeholder theories. In addition, we advocate the existence of a substantial moderating effect of ESG controversies in the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of debt financing, meaning that moderating effect increases strength negative relationship between ESG performance and cost of debt financing. Our results also indicated that the moderating effect is stronger when obtaining short-term debt capital, implying that ESG controversies play a larger role in the assessment of a company’s short-term ability to repay debts. The results remain robust for alternative model selection and variant definitions of the cost of capital.

Our findings have important implications for corporate practices and policy. Companies can utilise the results to proactively enhance their internal policies and market behaviour to align with ESG practices and avoid ESG controversies, which will translate into reduced cost of debt capital charged by creditors (especially lending institutions). In addition, the most controversial companies with the lowest ESG controversies score can benefit an increase in ESG performance to reduce equity capital costs as ESG performance alleviates the consequences of their bad reputation. Obtaining more favourable financing costs allows companies to optimise their capital structure, improve their economic value added and fundamental value, and strengthen their position within the competitive economy. The results offer valuable insights to investors and creditors by empirically demonstrating the importance of ESG controversies in determining the required rate of return on provided capital. Additionally, policymakers and regulatory bodies can use this information to establish regulatory guidelines and rules for lending institutions, encouraging them to consider ESG controversies when assessing creditworthiness. The findings demonstrate that capital providers consider ESG controversies when financing companies, which has societal implications. Companies will be motivated to avoid negative incidents or events involving ESG standards violations. Society can, therefore, benefit from companies’ adherence to declared sustainable policies and practices.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively investigate the influence of ESG controversies and their moderating effect in the context of the equity and debt capital cost for European listed companies. Therefore, the results contribute to the existing corporate finance literature by highlighting the link between ESG controversies and the cost of equity and overall, short-term and long-term debt and by empirically supporting the legitimacy theory and stakeholder theory related to ESG controversies. Moreover, we revealed the presence and strength of the moderating effect of ESG controversies and showed how ESG controversies affect the perception of ESG performance on the cost of capital in European listed companies.

However, our study is subject to certain limitations based on which future lines of research are proposed. We investigated only large and mid-cap companies listed in developed European countries. Our findings might not hold for financial companies, which we excluded from the sample due to a different regulatory framework and accounting measures, companies in other regions, and small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Therefore, future research could extend the dataset and include financial companies, companies based in other regions and investigate how ESG controversies and their moderating effect might also relate to SMEs. Future studies could extend the research by investigating the mediating effect of ESG controversies related to the cost of capital.
