# ESG and bank loan cost

### ABSTRACT
This study examines whether Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) performance influences bank lending conditions. Using 2916 firm-loan observations from 2016 to 2023 in Taiwan, we find that superior ESG performance significantly reduces loan interest rates and collateral requirements. Comprehensive ESG scores demonstrate substantial effects on both lending dimensions, with social responsibility proving most influential for interest rate reductions and environmental performance most effective for collateral relief. Exploiting the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous shock through difference-in-differences methodology, we provide causal evidence that ESG performance functions as crisis-resilience insurance. High-ESG firms secured considerable interest rate and collateral advantages during the pandemic, with governance quality emerging as the most influential dimension during crisis periods. Our findings advance sustainable finance literature by providing the first comprehensive analysis of ESG effects across multiple lending dimensions simultaneously.

## 1. Introduction

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) considerations have evolved from peripheral corporate social responsibility initiatives to central determinants of capital allocation in global financial markets. Since the United Nations Global Compact introduced the ESG framework in 2004, sustainable finance has experienced unprecedented growth, with global ESG assets reaching $30.3$ trillion by 2022 according to the Global Sustainable Investment Alliance. Despite extensive research demonstrating correlations between ESG performance and financial outcomes, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding ESG's systematic impact on firms' comprehensive financing costs, particularly across multiple contract dimensions simultaneously and in emerging market contexts.

Existing literature has established theoretical foundations linking ESG performance to financing costs through several mechanisms. El Ghoul et al. (2011) provided pioneering evidence that superior corporate social responsibility performance significantly reduces equity capital costs, establishing that market participants systematically incorporate ESG considerations into risk assessments and pricing decisions. Chava (2014) extended this analysis to environmental dimensions, documenting that poor environmental performance elevates borrowing costs and reduces bank financing access. These foundational studies established ESG performance as a risk mitigation mechanism, reducing information asymmetries between firms and capital providers (Myers and Majluf, 1984; Dhaliwal et al., 2011) whilst signalling superior long-term value creation capabilities.

Recent developments have reinforced ESG factors' materiality in capital market pricing mechanisms, particularly during market stress. Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021) demonstrated that investors systematically price carbon risk, whilst Flammer (2021) provided complementary evidence through green bond markets. The crisis-resilience properties of ESG performance were validated by Bae et al. (2021), who documented that firms with strong CSR profiles maintained superior capital access during COVID-19, whilst Christensen et al. (2021) established that mandatory ESG disclosure requirements enhance market efficiency by reducing information asymmetries. These studies demonstrate ESG performance has evolved from a peripheral consideration to a central determinant of corporate financing conditions.

Despite these advances, substantial empirical gaps persist. Most existing studies focus on individual financing channels whilst few provide integrated analysis of how ESG performance simultaneously influences bank lending conditions across multiple contract dimensions (Houston and Shan, 2022). Furthermore, empirical evidence remains highly fragmented across different market contexts, with limited systematic investigation of Asian emerging markets, particularly Taiwan's unique institutional environment. This study addresses three fundamental research questions. First, we investigate whether ESG performance systematically influences bank lending conditions across multiple dimensions simultaneously, including interest rates and collateral requirements. Second, we examine how ESG performance affects both tangible costs (interest rates) and intangible costs (collateral requirements) through instrumental variables modelling employing lagged loan contract terms, following Cerqueiro, Ongena, and Roszbach (2016) and Ioannidou, Ongena, and Peydró (2015), which capture interdependent relationships between these lending terms (Berger and Udell, 1995). Third, we explore whether ESG performance provides differential financing advantages during the COVID-19 pandemic, when heightened uncertainty amplifies the value of non-financial risk signals (Acharya and Steffen, 2020). To ensure exogeneity of treatment assignment in our difference-in-differences analysis, we classify firms based on their average ESG performance from 2016 to 2019, with the top quartile designated as the treatment group.

## 2. Methodology

This study employs panel data from 2916 firm-year observations of Taiwanese listed companies spanning 2016–2023, sourced from the Taiwan Economic Journal database. Following established conventions in corporate finance research (Fama and French, 1993), we exclude financial institutions and state-owned enterprises due to their distinctive regulatory frameworks and capital structures (Shleifer and Vishny, 1994). All continuous variables undergo winsorisation at the 1st and 99th percentiles to mitigate outlier effects (Wooldridge, 2010).

We examine two primary dimensions of bank lending conditions as dependent variables. Bank loan interest rates are measured using the average interest rate across all loans obtained by each firm in a given year, providing a comprehensive assessment of overall borrowing costs rather than focusing solely on isolated maximum rates. Collateral requirements are operationalised through a binary indicator that equals one if loans require pledged assets as security, and zero otherwise, reflecting banks' assessment of credit risk and demand for security against potential default losses (Stulz and Johnson, 1985). ESG performance constitutes the primary explanatory variable, measured using comprehensive sustainability scores provided by the Taiwan Economic Journal database. The TEJ ESG scoring system evaluates firms across environmental protection, social responsibility, and corporate governance pillars using methodology aligned with Global Reporting Initiative standards, generating scores ranging from zero (worst performance) to 100 (best performance). We separately examine comprehensive ESG scores and individual pillar scores to identify differential effects across sustainability dimensions (Giese et al., 2021).

The empirical specifications incorporate comprehensive control variables including the Taiwan Corporate Credit Risk Index, which provides standardised credit ratings reflecting default probability assessments (Altman, 1968), total asset growth rates, current ratio capturing short-term liquidity, interest rate structure, profitability measures including gross profit margins and return on assets, accounts receivable turnover measuring working capital management effectiveness, employee education levels proxying for human capital quality, CEO turnover frequency capturing managerial stability and quality, and voluntary CSR disclosure representing corporate transparency beyond mandatory requirements. These controls account for alternative determinants of lending conditions and reduce omitted variable bias.

We implement a multi-method approach combining instrumental variables (IV) and Ordinary Least Squares estimation strategies to address potential endogeneity concerns. Recognising that interest rates and collateral requirements are jointly determined through bank-borrower negotiations rather than independently set, we employ IV estimation to model simultaneous relationships (Berger and Udell, 1995). The IV system comprises two equations capturing bidirectional relationships between tangible and intangible borrowing costs:

$$rate_{i,t} = \alpha_1 mrtg_{i,t} + \alpha_2 ESGi,t + \alpha_3 TCRI_{i,t} + \alpha_4 X_{i,t} + \epsilon_{1i,t} \tag{1}$$
$$mrtg_{i,t} = \beta_1 rate_{i,t} + \beta_2 ESGi,t + \beta_3 TCRI_{i,t} + \beta_4 Z_{i,t} + \epsilon_{2i,t} \tag{2}$$

where $X$ and $Z$ represent vectors of exogenous control variables. Following established literature on loan contracting (Cerqueiro et al., 2016; Ioannidou et al. 2015), lagged values of interest rates and collateral requirements as instrumental variables satisfying relevance and exclusion restrictions. These instruments prove theoretically suitable as prior loan contract terms exhibit temporal persistence in loan contracting relationships, whilst maintaining exogeneity with respect to current period shocks after controlling for firm fixed effects and time-varying covariates.

To examine whether ESG performance provides differential financing advantages during crisis periods, we implement difference-in-differences (DID) methodology exploiting the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous shock (Angrist and Pischke, 2009). To ensure treatment assignment is exogenous to pandemic-induced outcomes, we classify firms based on their average ESG performance from 2016 to 2019, the pre-pandemic baseline period. Firms in the top quartile of this pre-pandemic ESG distribution constitute the treatment group, whilst all others serve as controls. The DID specification takes the following form:

$$Y_{i,t} = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 treat_i + \lambda_2 post_t + \lambda_3 (treat_i \times post_t) + \lambda_4 ESG_{i,t} + \Lambda X_{i,t} + \xi_{i,t} \tag{3}$$

where $treat$ indicates treatment group membership (firms in the top quartile of pre-pandemic ESG scores), $post$ denotes the pandemic period (2020–2022), and the interaction term captures the DID estimator representing the causal effect of high ESG performance on financing conditions during the crisis. Standard errors employ firm-level clustering throughout (Petersen, 2009) to account for within-firm correlation in residuals over time.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Descriptive statistics and baseline estimates

Descriptive statistics reveal moderate ESG adoption with considerable cross-sectional variation. The comprehensive ESG score averages 54.94 points, ranging from 33.27 to 80.04. The slightly lower governance scores suggest firms face greater challenges achieving governance excellence. Average loan interest rate stands at 2.0 % with standard deviation of 1.0 %, whilst collateral requirements appear in 73 % of observations.

Ordinary least squares estimation demonstrates that superior ESG performance significantly reduces both loan interest rates and collateral requirements. The comprehensive ESG score exhibits negative and statistically significant coefficients across both lending dimensions, with social responsibility demonstrating the strongest effect on interest rate reduction, followed by environmental performance and governance. This finding aligns with stakeholder theory predictions that strong employee relations, community engagement, and customer loyalty create operational resilience valued by creditors (Freeman, 1984; Lins et al., 2017). For collateral requirements, environmental performance demonstrates the strongest individual pillar effect, suggesting that lenders view ecological performance as particularly effective signals of operational risk and regulatory compliance, thereby substituting for traditional physical security (Sharfman and Fernando, 2008). These findings support information asymmetry theory's proposition that ESG performance serves as credible quality signals (Akerlof, 1978; Myers and Majluf, 1984) whilst corroborating risk management perspectives emphasising sustainability's role in operational risk mitigation (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Albuquerque et al., 2019).

### 3.2. Instrumental variables estimates and structural relationships

Instrumental variables estimation addressing simultaneity between lending terms provides robust support for systematic ESG effects. The comprehensive ESG score demonstrates statistically significant negative relationships with loan interest rates, with all three dimensions exhibiting theoretically predicted negative coefficients. For collateral requirements, individual pillar analysis reveals that all dimensions contribute significantly to reductions, suggesting lenders view comprehensive ESG excellence as credible signals reducing default risk. These systematic relationships align with recent evidence from diverse markets. Kim et al. (2025) analyse over 30,000 syndicated loans, documenting significant spread reductions for high-ESG borrowers, with effects concentrated among firms with strong environmental and governance performance. Their decomposition analysis parallels our findings that social responsibility demonstrates particularly strong impact on interest rates in Taiwan's banking market, whilst environmental factors prove more influential for collateral decisions. Recent studies confirm robust negative correlations between ESG ratings and loan spreads (Goss and Roberts, 2011; Cheng et al., 2014).

The structural relationship coefficients reveal important complementarities justifying the IV approach. Collateral requirements exhibit strong negative associations with interest rates, indicating banks systematically trade off security and pricing when structuring contracts (Berger and Udell, 1995). Our instrumental variable strategy, employing lagged values of interest rates and collateral requirements following Cerqueiro et al. (2016) and Ioannidou et al. (2015), effectively addresses endogeneity concerns whilst exploiting temporal persistence in loan contracting. The banking sector's own ESG adoption provides additional evidence for sustainability's materiality. Agnese and Giacomini (2023) demonstrate ESG factors significantly reduce European banks' funding costs, whilst Andrieș and Sprincean (2023) find superior ESG performance yields lower debt costs and enhanced valuations. Baek and Kang (2025) document that ESG performance enhances asset quality whilst reducing funding costs. These findings suggest ESG considerations have permeated financial intermediation comprehensively, with banks experiencing as borrowers the same sustainability premiums they apply when lending, reinforcing that sustainability performance operates as credible risk signal throughout credit markets (Dhaliwal et al., 2011).

### 3.3. Crisis-Period effects and resilience properties

Difference-in-differences estimation provides robust support for crisis-period ESG advantages. Employing treatment group classification based on firms' average ESG scores from 2016 to 2019, we ensure that high-ESG status is predetermined relative to the pandemic shock, thereby eliminating reverse causality concerns. The comprehensive ESG interaction term demonstrates statistically significant negative coefficients for loan interest rates, indicating that high-ESG firms experienced additional interest rate reductions during the pandemic compared to low-ESG counterparts. Dimensional decomposition reveals that governance performance provides the strongest crisis-period protection for interest rates, followed by comprehensive ESG and social responsibility, whilst environmental effects, though present, prove comparatively modest. The pronounced governance dimension effect suggests that strong board oversight, transparent disclosure practices, and robust internal controls prove particularly valuable signals to lenders during periods of heightened uncertainty, when information asymmetries intensify and traditional financial metrics become less reliable (Shleifer and Vishny, 1997; Gompers et al., 2003).

For collateral requirements, crisis-period ESG advantages prove even more pronounced. The comprehensive ESG interaction term exhibits large negative and highly significant coefficients, with governance quality demonstrating the strongest collateral-reducing effect, followed by comprehensive ESG performance and environmental dimensions, whilst social effects prove weakest amongst the pillars. This pattern suggests lenders view governance quality and as particularly credible signals substituting for physical collateral when asset values become volatile and traditional security proves less effective during crises. The dominance of governance effects across both lending dimensions aligns with theoretical predictions that corporate governance mechanisms reduce agency costs and information asymmetries, thereby serving as effective substitutes for traditional credit risk mitigation tools during stress periods (Meckling and Jensen, 1976; Anderson et al., 2004). These crisis-resilience properties align with broader evidence that ESG performance provides insurance-like benefits during financial stress. Danisman and Tarazi (2024) examine bank lending behaviour during financial crises, finding that banks increase lending to high-ESG firms whilst reducing credit supply to low-ESG counterparts during stress periods, consistent with flight-to-quality dynamics. Al Amosh and Khatib (2023) document that ESG performance remained stable or even improved during COVID-19 for firms with strong pre-pandemic sustainability practices, whilst Gao and Geng (2024) demonstrate that high-ESG firms experienced lower stock return volatility and superior operating performance during the pandemic. The underlying mechanism operates through multiple channels: ESG-excellent firms maintain more diversified stakeholder relationships providing operational flexibility; demonstrate superior crisis management capabilities; and possess strong governance structures enabling effective decision-making under uncertainty (Albuquerque et al., 2019; Eccles et al., 2014).

Our dimensional decomposition extends recent work emphasising disaggregated ESG analysis. Liu et al. (2023) provide compelling evidence that banks extending credit to high-ESG firms experience significantly lower nonperforming loan ratios, suggesting that lenders systematically recognise ESG excellence as credible signals of creditworthiness. Houston and Shan (2022) demonstrate that ESG profiles systematically affect multiple dimensions of banking relationships including loan pricing, covenant structures, and relationship intensity. Qian et al. (2023) extend these insights to emerging market contexts, documenting that ESG performance reduces loan spreads and covenant strictness for Chinese listed firms, whilst effects prove more pronounced for firms facing higher information asymmetries. This emerging market evidence proves particularly relevant for understanding Taiwan's institutional environment, suggesting that ESG-financing mechanisms operate robustly across diverse economic contexts. The structural trade-offs revealed through IV estimation illuminate mechanisms through which banks price credit risk across multiple contract dimensions simultaneously, with superior ESG performance serving as reliable risk signals reducing banks' dependence on physical collateral whilst enabling third-party ESG certifications to produce significant credit enhancement effects (Bolton and Kacperczyk, 2021; Flammer, 2021). Carnevale and Drago (2024) conduct a comprehensive literature review concluding that the preponderance of evidence supports negative relationships between ESG performance and loan pricing, consistent with our baseline estimates, strengthening confidence that our results reflect genuine ESG effects rather than Taiwan-specific anomalies.

## 4. Conclusion

This study establishes ESG performance as a fundamental determinant of corporate financing conditions in Taiwan's capital markets. Analysing 2916 firm-year observations spanning 2016–2023, we demonstrate that sustainability practices reduce borrowing costs and collateral requirements, with effects particularly pronounced during COVID-19, supporting targeted investments across ESG dimensions (Khan et al., 2016; Giese et al., 2021). Our findings advance sustainable finance literature by providing Asian emerging market evidence (La Porta et al., 1999), employing instrumental variables methodology with lagged loan contract terms as instruments (Cerqueiro et al., 2016; Ioannidou et al., 2015) that capture interdependent lending mechanisms (Berger and Udell, 1995), and establishing causal crisis-resilience properties through difference-in-differences analysis using pre-pandemic ESG classifications. Notably, governance quality emerges as the most influential ESG dimension during crisis periods, suggesting that strong corporate governance provides particularly credible signals when information asymmetries intensify. For practitioners, results justify ESG expenditures through concrete financial returns and insurance-like benefits during financial stress (Edmans, 2011; Ferrell et al., 2016). Future research should examine cross-country comparisons and specific ESG practices to identify highest-impact sustainability initiatives (Friede et al., 2015).
