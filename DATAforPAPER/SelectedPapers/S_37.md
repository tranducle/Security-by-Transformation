### Abstract

**Purpose** – This study examines the impact of ESG scores on the cost of capital, utilizing ESG ratings from 115 UK companies listed on the FTSE 350 between 2019 and 2023.

**Design/methodology/approach** – The study applied static panel data linear regressions, and the data for 115 UK companies listed on the FTSE 350 were collected from the LSEG Datastream.

**Findings** – The empirical results reveal a significant positive relationship between ESG scores and the cost of capital, as measured by the cost of equity, cost of debt and WACC. Additionally, the results show that ESG scores are positively correlated with beta, suggesting that companies with high ESG performance may increase systematic risk, possibly due to the challenges of integrating ESG into corporate strategies.

**Practical implications** – These findings are particularly relevant for managers, shareholders and policymakers. In particular, the study highlights whether ESG practices lead to lower financial risk or impose short-term capital costs, thereby assisting firms in aligning their sustainability goals with financial performance.

**Originality/value** – This article is motivated by the low number of studies that examine the impact of ESG scores on the cost of capital and systematic risk and contributes to the academic literature on ESG by considering the European Union’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD).

**Keywords:** ESG, Cost of capital, WACC, FTSE 350, Systematic risk

**Paper type:** Research article

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, environment, social and governance (ESG) has evolved from a niche concept to a critical driver of corporate decision-making, investor expectations and regulatory developments. ESG measures the sustainability and societal impact of a company’s activities, providing a critical framework through which investors assess corporate behaviour and future financial performance (Li et al., 2021). Marr (2024) asserts that by 2035, ESG will be synonymous with sound business practices, driven by the necessity for companies to proactively address environmental and social concerns, including reforestation and circular economies. As ESG becomes increasingly integrated into business models, firms that fail to adopt these practices risk damaging their reputations and alienating investors.

The significance of ESG factors in the contemporary business landscape is underscored by Runyon (2024), who reported that by mid-2024, over 50% of middle-market Chief Financial Officers (CFOs) had integrated sustainability into their core business strategies. This shift is largely driven by the European Union’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD). CSRD aims to promote a sustainable and responsible business environment and serve as a mechanism for enhancing market stability and investor confidence in Europe (Ali et al., 2024). Furthermore, the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) is expanding ESG reporting requirements to private companies, reflecting a broader change in expectations regarding corporate transparency and accountability, particularly concerning environmental and social impacts.

Despite the increasing emphasis on ESG, the path to full integration has been contentious. Holland and Malone (2024) noted that anti-ESG sentiment surged in 2023, with several US states enacting laws that restrict ESG-based investment strategies. This backlash highlights the polarized landscape in which ESG operates, where political, financial and regulatory forces often pull in opposing directions. On one hand, companies are encouraged to enhance their ESG scores to attract eco-conscious consumers and investors as ESG performance has become an increasingly relevant investment criterion for many investors (Hamza and Jarboui, 2024). On the other hand, they encounter legal and political challenges that may elevate the short-term costs associated with ESG compliance.

In this context, ESG considerations extend beyond merely achieving sustainability goals; they also encompass the management of financial performance. ESG initiatives are recognized as vital to long-term corporate sustainability and resilience, yet they may incur substantial short-term costs. This has specific implications for financial metrics, such as the cost of capital, which is directly influenced by a firm’s perceived risk and stability. As regulatory frameworks, such as the CSRD, mandate more comprehensive ESG reporting, the relationship between ESG ratings and financial outcomes will become increasingly critical for both corporate leaders and investors.

ESG considerations are increasingly becoming integral to corporate strategies; however, their impact on the cost of capital remains uncertain and widely debated. Existing studies yield mixed results regarding whether strong ESG performance mitigates financial risk and reduces capital costs or imposes additional short-term financial burdens on firms. In particular, Moussa and Elmarzouky (2024) suggest that while ESG reporting improves corporate transparency and fosters long-term value creation, it may also result in higher capital costs in the short term due to perceived risks and the expenses associated with ESG implementation. Furthermore, Singhania and Gupta (2024) pinpoint the significant short-term costs linked to ESG implementation, including compliance expenses and necessary operational changes.

In the context of the FTSE 350, where firms are facing increasing pressure from regulators such as the CSRD and rising investor demands for sustainability, the relationship between ESG ratings and the cost of capital has not been thoroughly examined. According to Vermeulen (2024), ESG ratings are increasingly influencing investment decisions among FTSE 350 companies; however, their specific effects on a firm’s cost of equity, cost of debt, and overall cost of capital remain underexplored. This research aims to address this research lacuna by investigating the impact of ESG ratings on the cost of capital for companies listed on the FTSE 350 between 2019 and 2023. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first study to assess the impact of ESG scores on the systematic risk, cost of equity, cost of debt and weighted average cost of capital (WACC) of FTSE 350 companies.

The paper contributes to the existing literature in two ways. First, this article is largely motivated by the relatively few studies that examine the impact of ESG scores on the systematic risk and cost of capital. Therefore, this study contributes to the academic discourse on ESG by utilizing data from FTSE 350 companies. Second, this paper attempts to decipher the impact of ESG regulations on the cost of capital and systematic risk following European Union’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD).

This research is critically relevant for managers, investors, policymakers, and academics, as it provides insights into the financial implications of integrating ESG factors. As ESG considerations increasingly shape corporate strategies and investment decisions, understanding how ESG ratings influence the cost of capital is essential for many stakeholders. For managers, the study highlights whether ESG practices lead to lower financial risk or impose short-term capital costs, thereby assisting firms in aligning their sustainability goals with financial performance. Investors receive valuable information on whether higher ESG scores genuinely mitigate financial risks or result in increased borrowing and equity costs, enabling them to make more informed decisions regarding sustainable investments. Policymakers gain insights into how ESG regulations, such as the CSRD, impact firms’ capital costs, allowing them to refine regulatory frameworks without discouraging ESG adoption.

The paper is organized as following. Section 2 presents a review of the relevant literature. Section 3 presents the data and the methodology employed in this study. Section 4 reports the empirical findings and Section 5 summarizes the main findings of the study.

## 2. Literature review

### 2.1 ESG ratings and cost of equity
The relationship between ESG practices and the cost of equity has become an increasingly important topic in the finance literature, particularly since the mid-2010s. Dhaliwal et al. (2014) examined how CSR disclosures, a vital component of ESG ratings, impact the cost of equity. Their analysis utilized data from 5,135 CSR reports published by 1,093 firms across 31 countries between 1995 and 2007. The primary focus of this study was to understand how CSR disclosures help bridge the information gap between firms and investors, thereby reducing the cost of equity. The study used a two-stage regression model to tackle potential self-selection bias and found that CSR disclosures were significantly associated with lower costs of equity. This effect was particularly pronounced in countries with strong stakeholder orientations and higher levels of financial opacity.

Ng and Rezaee (2015) further delved into the impact of sustainability practices on the cost of equity capital, considering both financial (ECON) and non-financial (ESG) aspects of sustainability. Their study used a comprehensive sample of over 3,000 firms from 1990 to 2013, assessing the cost of equity through proxies such as the industry-adjusted earnings-price ratio and the implied cost of equity. They found that economic sustainability, particularly in terms of growth opportunities and research efforts, was negatively associated with the cost of equity, suggesting that financially sustainable firms benefit from lower equity costs. Furthermore, the study emphasised the critical role of ESG performance in reducing the cost of equity, primarily through robust environmental and governance practices. The authors demonstrated that the combined impact of financial and ESG performance leads to an even more substantial reduction in equity costs, as investors perceive firms with strong sustainability profiles as less risky.

Becchetti et al. (2023) examined the impact of corporate social irresponsibility (CSI), as measured by ESG misconduct, on the cost of equity. The study analysed data from 731 firms listed in the MSCI USA Index between 2007 and 2017. Using the RepRisk Index (RRI) to monitor reputational risks associated with ESG controversies, the authors identified a significant positive correlation between ESG misconduct and the cost of equity. Firms with higher RRI scores experienced elevated equity costs, as investors demanded greater returns to offset the increased risk. The study also highlighted sectoral differences, revealing that industries such as agriculture and mining were more sensitive to ESG risks. Additionally, the negative impact of ESG misconduct was more pronounced in firms that had previously maintained high ESG scores, suggesting that investors respond more acutely to negative news from companies regarded as ESG leaders.

More recent research has shifted the focus to investigating the impact of ESG performance on the cost of equity in emerging markets, where regulatory frameworks and market conditions differ significantly from those in developed economies. Chen et al. (2022) examined the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of equity for Chinese A-Share companies from 2010 to 2020. The study analysed 1,532 listed companies, comprising 15,633 observations over the years. The findings indicated a strong negative correlation between ESG performance and the cost of equity, particularly in firms with strong governance practices. This suggests that effective governance can help mitigate market risks and enhance investor confidence, thereby reducing equity costs. The study also pointed out that ESG performance diminishes market risk and promotes equity diversification, attracting a broader investor base and further decreasing the cost of equity. These findings underscore the growing importance of ESG practices in emerging markets, where global investors prioritize sustainability factors in their investment decisions.

### 2.2 ESG ratings and cost of debt
Raimo et al. (2021) examined the impact of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt financing, using data from 919 firms (8,264 observations) across nine sectors globally for the period from 2010 to 2019. Employing a fixed effects regression model, the authors found a significant negative association between ESG disclosure scores and the cost of debt. This finding suggests that companies with greater transparency in ESG reporting tend to secure lower borrowing costs, thereby reducing information asymmetry between borrowers and lenders and leading to more favourable debt terms. Building on this research, Eliwa et al. (2021) studied the impact of ESG practices on the cost of debt within the European Union. Their study encompassed multiple industries and highlighted those firms with effective ESG practices and powerful governance frameworks experienced lower costs of debt. The authors attributed this result to enhanced creditworthiness and reduced perceived risks associated with firms that integrate robust governance into their ESG strategies.

Gigante and Manglaviti (2022) employed a regression discontinuity design (RDD) to explore the causal impact of ESG performance on the cost of debt for European non-financial companies from 2018 to 2020. Contrary to the findings of previous studies, their research revealed no statistically significant relationship between changes in ESG scores and the cost of debt. This suggests that the direct impact of ESG performance on debt financing may be more complex than previously assumed, potentially affected by industry-specific risks or broader market conditions.

Amiraslani et al. (2022) examined a global dataset of bond market transactions and found that firms with high ESG scores benefited from lower bond yields, particularly during periods of financial distress, such as the financial crisis period 2008–2009. The study emphasised that strong ESG performance enhances social capital and trust among bondholders, thereby reducing perceived risk and, consequently, the cost of debt. This research underscores the protective role that ESG factors can play in the bond market, especially during times of economic uncertainty.

Using a sample of 4,846 firms across 60 countries, categorized into either civil law or common law systems, Koutoupis et al. (2025) explored the relationship between ESG scores and the components of cost of capital and found a negative relationship between ESG and cost of capital (measured by the cost of equity and cost of debt) for firms operating in civil law systems. In contrast, they authors found that firms in common law systems have a negative relationship only with the cost of equity.

Overall, the extant literature demonstrates that ESG factors can significantly decrease the cost of debt by enhancing transparency and reducing perceived risks among lenders. However, the extent of this impact varies depending on the context, industry and specific ESG dimensions.

### 2.3 ESG ratings and weighted average cost of capital (WACC)
The impact of ESG scores on a company’s cost of capital components, such as the cost of equity and debt, has been extensively studied. However, there has been less research on how ESG scores affect the WACC. Hoepner et al. (2016) examined the effects of corporate and country-level sustainability characteristics on the cost of debt by analysing 470 loan agreements from 28 countries between 2005 and 2012. The results indicated that country-level sustainability and robust environmental and social frameworks were linked to reduced debt costs, thereby lowering the WACC. Firms operating in such environments experienced an average decrease of 64 basis points in loan spreads, demonstrating how macro-level sustainability can improve corporate financing by reducing debt-related costs.

Lins et al. (2017) examined the role of social capital, an essential component of ESG criteria, during the financial crisis of 2008–2009. Their study demonstrated that firms with higher levels of social capital, attained through robust ESG practices, experienced lower capital costs during the crisis. The decrease in WACC was driven by reduced equity and debt costs, as investors and lenders perceived these firms as more stable and reliable during periods of economic uncertainty. Consequently, this study underscored the importance of ESG in maintaining favourable financing conditions during financial crises, emphasising its role in risk management. Atan et al. (2018) used panel data from 2010 to 2013 to analyse the ESG scores of 54 Malaysian companies and found that ESG scores have a significant positive impact on the companies’ WACC.

Giese et al. (2019) conducted a comprehensive analysis of how the integration of ESG factors affects stock valuation, risk, and the overall cost of capital for companies. They found that firms with higher ESG scores benefited from lower equity costs, as investors perceived them to be less risky. This, combined with earlier findings of reduced debt costs, suggests that ESG integration leads to a lower WACC. Broadstock et al. (2019) examined the role of ESG performance during the COVID-19 pandemic using data from Chinese firms. The study revealed that companies with higher ESG ratings experienced a lower WACC during the crisis, which was attributed to reduced equity and debt costs. The authors provided further evidence that ESG factors serve as a protective buffer in volatile markets, helping firms maintain favourable financing conditions even in adverse economic environments.

Moussa and Elmarzouky (2024) examined the effect of ESG disclosure on the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) for non-financial firms in the UK, as represented in the FTSE All-Share Index, over the period 2014–2018. They found that ESG reporting is positively associated with the cost of capital, as measured by WACC. However, corporate governance was found to moderate this relationship, weakening the positive correlation and reversing it to a negative association in firms with strong governance practices. The current study differs from that of Moussa and Elmarzouky (2024) by assessing the impact of ESG on systematic risk, the cost of equity, the cost of debt, and the WACC of FTSE 350 companies.

### 2.4 ESG ratings and risk profile
This sub-section explores how ESG performance affects company risk, including default risk, idiosyncratic risk, corporate risk-taking behaviour and reputational risk. Reber et al. (2021) explored the influence of ESG disclosures on idiosyncratic risk during initial public offerings (IPOs) in the USA. The study analysed 1,312 IPOs from 2002 to 2018 and found that firms voluntarily disclosing ESG information during their IPOs experienced significantly lower idiosyncratic risk in the first year of aftermarket trading. This reduction in risk, measured by volatility metrics such as Value-at-Risk (VaR), was especially pronounced in the manufacturing and healthcare sectors. Li et al. (2022) investigated the impact of ESG practices on the default risk of 3,539 Chinese listed firms from 2015 to 2020. The results revealed a strong negative relationship between ESG ratings and default risk. In particular, higher ESG scores were linked to significantly lower probabilities of default, particularly over extended time horizons.

He et al. (2023) explored the relationship between ESG scores and corporate behaviour among publicly listed firms in China. The study analysed 26,427 firm-year observations from 2010 to 2020 and revealed that higher ESG scores were associated with more conservative corporate strategies, such as maintaining lower cash holdings and net leverage ratios. This risk aversion was particularly pronounced in firms with lower transparency and weaker corporate governance, indicating that effective ESG practices significantly mitigate risk-taking, especially in environments with less robust internal controls. Khorilov and Kim (2024) provided valuable insights into the connection between ESG practices and firm risk in the Korean market. Their study analysed 7,834 firm-year observations from 2011 to 2022 and revealed that higher ESG scores were linked to reductions in both total and idiosyncratic risks. Moreover, the study indicated that the impact of ESG on systematic risk was more complex, especially during the COVID-19 crisis. Based on data from China’s A-share listed companies from 2015 to 2022, Bao et al. (2024) explored the impact of ESG rating disparities on corporate risk, with a focus on the mediating role of financing constraints. They found that ESG rating disparities significantly increase corporate financial risk, particularly in non-state owned enterprises and heavy-polluting industries. Discrepancies in governance ratings exert the greatest impact on corporate risk, underscoring the critical role of corporate governance.

More recently, Ren et al. (2025) employed the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) to measure a firm’s idiosyncratic risk and culled data from Chinese-listed manufacturing firms from 2013 to 2021 to examine the relationship between ESG disclosure transparency and idiosyncratic risk. They also incorporated artificial intelligence (AI) intellectual property protection and institutional investor distraction as moderating variables in examining this relationship. Their results indicated a U-shaped relationship between ESG disclosure transparency and idiosyncratic risk. Furthermore, the findings showed that AI intellectual property protection shifts the turning point of the U-shaped curve to the right and steepens the U-shaped link by strengthening the positive impact of ESG disclosure transparency.

Overall, the studies mentioned above provide compelling evidence that ESG performance significantly influences a firm’s risk profile, generally leading to a reduction in various types of risk. However, the relationship between ESG and systematic risk may vary depending on the context, especially in volatile market conditions.

## 3. Research design

### 3.1 Data
This study uses data from 115 companies listed on the UK FTSE 350 index. To form the final sample, I required all companies to have a market capitalization of at least £1 billion and complete ESG ratings for the period from 2019 to 2023. The final sample comprises 63 companies from the FTSE 100 and 52 companies from the FTSE 250, spanning 10 industries, while excluding the financial sector due to their distinct financial structure. The total number of observations is 575, thus forming a balanced sample.

To examine the relationship between ESG scores and the cost of capital, I gathered the following data from the LSEG Datastream:

1.  **ESG Ratings (ESG):** This includes both aggregate ESG scores and scores for the environmental (ENV), social (SOC) and governance (GOV) pillars.
2.  **Cost of Equity (CoE):** The return required by equity investors, reflecting the perceived risk associated with the company’s equity.
3.  **Cost of Debt (CoD):** The effective interest rate that the company pays on its borrowed funds.
4.  **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC):** The average rate of return required by all company’s investors, weighted according to the proportion of equity and debt in the firm’s capital structure.
5.  **Systematic risk (Beta):** A measure of a company’s stock volatility in relation to the overall market.
6.  **Total Debt to Total Assets (TD):** This leverage ratio indicates the percentage of a company’s assets that are financed by debt.
7.  **Return on Assets (ROA):** A profitability ratio that measures how efficiently a company uses its assets to generate earnings.
8.  **Price-to-Book Ratio (PB):** A valuation metric that compares a company’s market value to its book value.
9.  **Total Assets (TA):** The total value of all assets owned by the company.

To illustrate the evolution of ESG performance over the examined period, Table 1 shows the distribution of companies across three ESG rating categories: AAA-AA, A-BB, and B-CCC from 2019 to 2023. In 2019, 43 companies were classified in the first category (AAA-AA), a number that increased to 80 in both 2022 and 2023. Conversely, the number of firms belonging to B-CCC category decreased from 21 in 2019 to just 6 in 2023. This trend suggests a progressive enhancement of ESG practices, possibly due to increasing regulatory pressures and heightened investor demand. Finally, the number of firms belonging to A-BB category was 51 in 2019 and fell to 29 in 2023.

### 3.2 Methodology
To examine the impact of ESG scores on the cost of capital, I employ static panel data regression analysis, which allows for the control of unobserved heterogeneity and enhances the validity of the results. I use fixed effects models which adeptly handle unobserved individual heterogeneity, allowing each entity its own intercept. This isolates the impact of independent variables over time, mitigating biases from time-invariant characteristics that could skew the relationship between the dependent variable and independent variables.

The two baseline regression models are as follows:

$$Cost_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 * ESG_{i,t} + \beta_2 * TD_{i,t} + \beta_3 * ROA_{i,t} + \beta_4 * PB_{i,t} + \beta_5 * TA_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t} \quad (1)$$

$$Cost_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 * ENV_{i,t} + \beta_2 * SOC_{i,t} + \beta_3 * GOV_{i,t} + \beta_4 * TD_{i,t} + \beta_5 * ROA_{i,t} + \beta_6 * PB_{i,t} + \beta_7 * TA_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t} \quad (2)$$

Where the subscripts $i$ and $t$ represent the firm and year, respectively. The dependent variable $Cost_{i,t}$, is (1) the cost of equity (CoE), (2) the cost of debt (CoD), (3) the WACC and (4) the systematic risk (Beta). All regression models control for both year and firm fixed effects. Finally, I allow the error term ($\epsilon_{i,t}$) in Equations (1) and (2) to be serially correlated for the same company. Therefore, in all regression models, the standard errors are adjusted for heteroscedasticity and within-firm clustering.

### 4.2 Regression results
Table 4 presents the results of panel data regression analysis. Specifically, Model (1) demonstrates a positive and statistically significant relationship between ESG scores and CoE, with a coefficient of 0.571, statistically significant at the 5% level. This indicates that higher ESG ratings are associated with an increased cost of equity. These findings suggest that firms incur compliance and operational costs related to maintaining strong ESG practices. A similar positive and statistically significant effect of ESG scores is observed on the cost of debt (CoD), WACC and beta. Notably, the coefficient of ESG, when regressed against CoD, is statistically significant at the 1% level. This implies that firms with higher ESG ratings face higher borrowing costs. One possible explanation is that lenders perceive ESG investments as an increasing financial burden or risk. These findings align with the research of Fandella et al. (2023), which indicates that firms with high ESG scores typically report an increased CoD due to costs associated with governance and sustainability initiatives. The positive impact of ESG scores on both WACC (with a coefficient of 0.839, statistically significant at the 1% level) and beta (with a coefficient of 1.914, statistically significant at the 1% level) reinforces the notion that higher ESG scores correlate with increased overall capital costs and heightened systematic risk. The leverage ratio (TD) is positive in all regressions and statistically significant in Models (1) and (3), suggesting that high levels of leverage are associated with a higher cost of capital. Profitability, as measured by ROA, is negative and statistically significant when regressed against the cost of debt (CoD).

### 4.3 Robustness tests
To assess the reliability of the main findings, I conducted separate regressions for FTSE 100 and FTSE 250 companies. This segmentation is well-justified due to the notable differences in firm size, operational scale and investor sentiment between these two groups. The average market cap for FTSE 100 companies in the dataset was £24,959 million, compared to just £2,305 million for FTSE 250 firms. This substantial disparity in size suggests that the financial mechanisms driving ESG performance are likely to differ across these segments. Larger companies in the FTSE 100 are well-established, attracting global investors and facing greater regulatory scrutiny. They possess more resources to invest in ESG initiatives, which could affect their cost of capital in distinct ways. In contrast, FTSE 250 firms, with smaller market capitalizations, often experience higher volatility and may struggle to implement ESG practices without adversely affecting their financial performance. Investor expectations also vary between large-cap and mid-cap companies. By conducting separate regressions, this analysis offers insights into whether the impact of ESG on financial performance differs based on a firm’s market segment.

Separate regression results for FTSE 100 and FTSE 250 firms demonstrate significant variability in how ESG performance affects financial metrics, highlighting the influence of firm size and market segment on these relationships. More specifically, Table 6 indicates that ESG scores are positively and significantly related with CoE at the 5% level and with CoD and WACC at the 1% level, respectively. These findings suggest that larger companies with abundant resources may face higher capital costs due to their strong ESG performance. This could be attributed to increased regulatory scrutiny and higher investors’ expectations regarding ESG practices. The positive correlation between ESG and CoD suggests that creditors may view ESG investments as adding financial risk due to the complexities involved in their integration.

## 5. Conclusions

This study examines how ESG performance influences the cost of capital for companies listed on the FTSE 350 during the period from 2019 to 2023. The findings indicate that higher ESG scores are associated with an increased cost of capital, as measured by the cost of equity, cost of debt and WACC. These results suggest that investors perceive firms with strong ESG performance as riskier in the short term, likely due to the upfront costs related to implementing ESG initiatives. Furthermore, the findings imply that companies adopting ESG practices may not experience an immediate reduction in capital costs, contrary to expectations in financial markets. Additionally, the results reveal a positive correlation between ESG scores and beta, suggesting that companies with high ESG performance may increase systematic risk, potentially due to the challenges of integrating ESG considerations into corporate strategies, particularly within the environmental pillar.

This research presents significant managerial implications as it challenges conventional assumptions regarding the positive impact of ESG performance on financial measures, including the cost of capital. Most prior literature has suggested that strong ESG practices reduce perceived risk, allowing companies to benefit from lower costs of equity and debt. However, this study uncovers conflicting evidence for companies listed on the FTSE 350 index. Specifically, the finding that higher ESG scores correlate with increased costs of equity and debt indicates that, in the short run, financial markets may view ESG initiatives as a cost burden rather than a risk mitigation tool. Policymakers should promote a long-term orientation in ESG strategies, recognising that the financial benefits of ESG investments may not materialize immediately. Incentive structures—such as tax relief, favourable financing terms, or ESG-linked government grants—should reward firms for sustained ESG performance rather than short-term signalling.

Moreover, the findings suggest that shareholders should exercise caution when investing in ESG, particularly in business sectors where short-term returns are anticipated. While ESG-oriented strategies may contribute to the long-term enhancement of a firm’s reputation and market position, managers must weigh this against any short-term financial repercussions. The positive relationship between ESG scores and systematic risk (beta) highlights some of the operational challenges associated with integrating environmental, social and governance components into core corporate strategies. The observed positive association between ESG scores and systematic risk (beta) highlights the operational challenges of embedding ESG into core corporate strategies. Policymakers should fund academic-industry partnerships, pilot programs, and innovation grants aimed at developing scalable, cost-effective approaches to ESG integration, particularly for large listed firms.

While this study contributes to the growing literature on the relationship between ESG performance and the cost of capital, several areas for further research emerge that could expand the findings and address existing gaps. First, future research could explore the impact of ESG performance on smaller companies. This study focused on firms with a market capitalization of £1 billion and above, thereby excluding many smaller firms from the FTSE 350. Smaller companies often have fewer resources and may encounter different challenges when implementing ESG initiatives. Future research should also explore whether these smaller firms experience distinct financial outcomes, risks, or benefits compared to larger firms when adopting ESG practices, thereby offering a more nuanced perspective on ESG’s impact across varying company sizes. Second, extending the timeline of analysis could yield valuable insights into the long-term effects of ESG performance on financial outcomes. This study examined a five-year period (2019–2023), which may be insufficient to capture the long-term benefits or risks associated with ESG practices. Future studies could expand the analysis over a longer time horizon to better understand how ESG integration influences capital costs and firm risk across multiple economic cycles. Finally, this study employed a quantitative approach to measure the relationship between ESG and financial metrics. Future research could incorporate qualitative methods, such as interviews with corporate managers or ESG analysts, to gain deeper insights into the motivations behind ESG practices, the challenges of ESG integration and the strategies companies use to overcome these obstacles. This qualitative perspective could complement the quantitative findings, providing a more holistic view of the relationship between ESG and financial performance. By addressing these areas, future research could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complexities surrounding ESG performance and its financial implications, offering valuable insights for investors, managers and policymakers.
