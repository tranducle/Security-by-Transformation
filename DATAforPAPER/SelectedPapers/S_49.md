# ESG and cost of capital components: Does the legal system matter?

**Abstract**

**Purpose** – This study aims to explore the relationship between environmental, social and governance (ESG) scores and the components of cost of capital in two distinct legal systems, while also examining the unique effect of each ESG pillar on these components.

**Design/methodology/approach** – Using a sample of 4,846 firms across 60 countries, categorized into either civil law or common law systems, the authors test this study’s hypotheses using ordinary least squares and instrumental variables two-stage least squares.

**Findings** – The findings reveal a negative relationship between ESG and the components of the cost of capital for firms operating in civil law systems, whether measured by the cost of debt or the cost of equity. In contrast, firms in common law systems exhibit a negative relationship only with the cost of equity. Furthermore, the findings indicate that the influence of each ESG pillar on the components of the cost of capital varies across these two legal systems.

**Originality/value** – To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study represents the first investigation into the relationship between ESG scores and the components of cost of capital across two distinct jurisdictions. Additionally, it provides evidence regarding the distinct impact of each pillar of ESG on the components of cost of capital.

**Keywords** ESG scores, Cost of debt, Cost of equity, Civil law, Common law

**Paper type** Research paper

## 1. Introduction

Environmental, social and governance (hereafter ESG) [1] and the cost of capital [i.e. cost of equity (CE) and cost of debt (CD)] have been investigated in the past, but not in terms of the legal systems around the world (El Ghoul *et al.*, 2011; Martínez-Ferrero and García-Sánchez, 2017; Michaels and Grüning, 2017; Atan *et al.*, 2018). This is quite interesting for research because the literature illustrates that ESG practices reflect differences in culture, traditional financing options and legal origin (Eliwa *et al.*, 2021; Zattoni and Cuomo, 2008). In this context, our study aims to shed light on whether the legal system plays a significant role in determining the relationship between ESG score and the cost of capital.

Empirical evidence demonstrates that common law countries provide different protection for investors’ rights compared to civil law countries (La Porta *et al.*, 1998; Djankov *et al.*, 2008). This distinction is further emphasized in emerging markets such as Jordan, where debt financing is often preferred to support ESG initiatives, underscoring the impact of capital structure decisions on sustainability performance (Al Amosh *et al.*, 2024). Similarly, theoretical frameworks support the idea that a decrease in the cost of capital is associated with a reduction in firm risk (Fandella *et al.*, 2023). In this regard, financial analysts consider ESG performance as an indicator for achieving various objectives, including among others, risk mitigation (Ng and Rezaee, 2015). Adeneye *et al.* (2023) further explore this connection by demonstrating how ESG practices influence financial strategies, particularly the speed of adjustment to target leverage, underscoring the financial benefits of sustainable practices.

Much of the literature reviewed in the next section illustrates no clear relationship between ESG and the CE/CD. We argue that such interpretation does not consider the role played by the legal system. Therefore, we empirically examine whether the civil law or common law system has a significant impact on the relationship between ESG and the components of cost of capital. Additionally, we explore the unique effect of each ESG pillar on these components. Finally, we address the potential issue of endogeneity by using instrumental variables two-stage least squares (IV/2SLS) to substantiate our results.

This study makes significant contributions to the literature by addressing critical research gaps in understanding the relationship between ESG practices and the components of the cost of capital across different legal systems. While prior research has extensively explored the relationship between CSR and financial mechanisms, including equity and debt financing (El Ghoul *et al.*, 2011; Saeed and Sroufe, 2021), the moderating effect of legal systems on these relationships remains underexplored. Existing studies mainly focus on specific markets or general ESG impacts without adequately disentangling the role of legal frameworks, such as civil law and common law systems, in shaping these relationships.

This paper directly addresses this gap by presenting empirical evidence on how legal systems moderate the relationship between ESG performance and the components of cost of capital. Using a large data set of 4,846 firms across 60 countries, categorized into civil law and common law systems, this study empirically demonstrates that civil law countries exhibit a stronger negative relationship between ESG scores and the components of cost of capital compared to common law countries. Specifically, the findings show that while all ESG pillars significantly reduce the cost of capital in civil law systems, the influence of each pillar on the components of the cost of capital varies between the two legal systems. These findings extend the work of Eliwa *et al.* (2021) and Bacha *et al.* (2021), who examined the relationship between ESG practices and financial costs but did not consider the moderating role of legal frameworks.

Moreover, by using institutional theory (North, 1990) and stakeholder theory (Freeman, 1984), this research provides a comprehensive theoretical lens to explain how legal systems, shaped by their governance priorities, influence the effectiveness of ESG practices in reducing financial risks and improving cost efficiency. Unlike prior studies that often report mixed or inconclusive results regarding ESG’s impact on financing costs (Ok and Kim, 2019; Hamrouni *et al.*, 2020; Bacha *et al.*, 2021), this study provides empirical evidence by incorporating the legal system as a critical moderating variable. Our paper responds to calls in the literature for deeper contextual analyses of ESG performance (Ducassy and Montandrau, 2015; Amor-Esteban *et al.*, 2018).

Methodologically, this study also addresses the limitations of prior research (e.g. Goss and Roberts, 2011; Hamrouni *et al.*, 2020) by mitigating endogeneity concerns using IV/2SLS approaches. This ensures more robust and unbiased estimates, reinforcing the credibility of findings related to ESG’s financial impact.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to provide cross-jurisdictional evidence on the moderating effect of legal systems on the ESG-cost of capital relationship. By integrating diverse legal environments, this research highlights how institutional environments shape the financial benefits of ESG practices, offering valuable insights for policymakers aiming to design legal frameworks that incentivize sustainable corporate behavior. For business practitioners and investors, the findings underscore the importance of aligning ESG strategies with the institutional characteristics of the markets in which they operate.

Finally, a key contribution of this study is that it enhances the findings of Priem and Gabellone (2024) who identify a negative relationship between ESG and cost of capital and emphasize ESG’s substitutive role in weaker institutional environments. Therefore, our research takes this further by dissecting these effects across distinct legal systems and individual cost components. By offering a more detailed and comparative analysis, our study deepens the understanding of how legal systems shape the ESG cost of capital nexus.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the literature review and hypotheses development. Section 3 outlines the data and methodology, while Section 4 presents the results of the analysis. Section 5 highlights the main conclusions of the study.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

### 2.1 Environmental, social and governance performance and cost of capital under different legal system

Ducassy and Montandrau (2015) argue that country-level factors are crucial in determining the relationship between CSR performance and cost of capital, requiring further investigation. Specifically, using data from Iran and Iraq, Faysal *et al.* (2020) provide evidence showing how governance mechanisms such as ownership structures play a key role in shaping capital costs in emerging markets. These findings underscore the need to consider specific governance frameworks when analyzing cost structures.

Prior research examines the impact of institutional ownership (Harjoto and Jo, 2015), investor protection (Breuer *et al.*, 2018) and the orientation of firms toward shareholders or stakeholders (Desender *et al.*, 2020) on the relationship between CSR performance and the CE. Harjoto and Jo (2015) found a negative correlation between CSR and CE. They concluded that while mandated CSR provides immediate benefits in terms of reduced CE because of transparency and compliance, voluntary CSR does not have a significant immediate effect on reducing CE. They claimed that the benefits of voluntary CSR manifest over a longer horizon as firms build reputations and improve stakeholder trust. Desender *et al.* (2020) observed that in stakeholder-oriented systems, the correlation between CSR and CE is positive, whereas in shareholder-oriented systems, it turns negative. This paper aims to examine the impact of legal systems on how ESG performance influences the cost of equity capital and cost of debt.

Amor-Esteban *et al.* (2018) point out that there is a difference in ESG performance across countries with different legal systems. Countries with civil law systems demonstrate a stronger dedication to sustainability, highlighted by their comprehensive legal frameworks that prioritize the interests of stakeholders suggesting that this commitment is manifest in the sustained efforts toward ESG and the enhanced transparency in ESG disclosures observed in these countries, in contrast to the practices prevalent in common law countries (Amor-Esteban *et al.*, 2018; Halkos and Nomikos, 2021; Chouaibi *et al.*, 2022). Additionally, civil law countries often increase stricter environmental regulations, resulting in better environmental outcomes (DasGupta and Roy, 2023). Similarly, Al Amosh *et al.* (2024) reveal that in emerging markets such as Jordan, debt financing is leveraged more effectively than equity financing to support sustainability goals, which aligns with the stakeholder-centric model prevalent in civil law countries.

Furthermore, common-law countries adopt a reactive approach to environmental issues. On the contrary, civil law countries support a stakeholder-centric model of corporate governance. This model prioritizes a broad array of stakeholder interests, potentially enhancing the social dimension of ESG performance (Adams *et al.*, 2011; Yoshikawa *et al.*, 2021). Consequently, civil law countries not only encourage direct corporate involvement in social matters but also reflect differing outcomes in social responsibility when compared to common law nations, where market forces and shareholder interests are more common (Amor-Esteban *et al.*, 2018). This aligns with the findings of Arianpoor *et al.* (2023), who demonstrate that enhanced sustainability reporting positively affects enterprise value, particularly when supported by strong governance frameworks.

According to the previously mentioned papers, the performance of ESG variables and the level of cost of capital may differ between countries with common law systems and those with civil law systems. Consequently, it is important to examine the impact of ESG performance on the cost of capital in various legal environments, as proposed by Ducassy and Montandrau (2015). Hence, we categorize the sample into countries based on whether they follow common law or civil law systems.

### 2.2 Environmental, social and governance performance and cost of debt

Previous empirical studies examining the correlation between ESG performance and the CD have yielded conflicting and inconclusive findings. This indicates that the empirical question of whether there is a correlation between CSR and CD remains unresolved (Bacha *et al.*, 2021; Maaloul *et al.*, 2023).

Numerous studies have investigated the impact of ESG performance on the CD, consistently revealing a significant and negative relationship (e.g. Oikonomou *et al.*, 2014; Ge and Liu, 2015). Goss and Roberts (2011) discover that in the US banking sector, lower-quality borrowers with discretionary CSR investments face higher loan costs, whereas this factor is negligible for higher-quality borrowers. Similarly, Ye and Zhang (2011) find that Chinese firms with extremely low or high CSR levels incur higher debt financing costs. In the USA, Oikonomou *et al.* (2014) and Ge and Liu (2015) demonstrate that better CSR performance correlates with lower corporate bond yield spreads and improved credit ratings. Extending this trend, Cooper and Uzun (2015) observe a lower CD for firms with strong CSR across US industries, particularly in manufacturing and finance. Using data from the Tehran Stock Exchange over the period 2014–2019, Arianpoor *et al.* (2023) provide additional insights by showing how sustainability reporting positively impacts enterprise value and reduces financial risks, which in turn may reduce CD, especially when moderated by effective management governance. Finally, Priem and Gabellone (2024) argue that there is a negative relationship between ESG scores and the cost of capital in 600 large, mid and small capitalization companies across 17 countries that are component of the EURO STOXX 600 Index. They also suggest that this effect is particularly significant in countries with weaker legal systems, where ESG can act as a substitute for institutional shortcomings, lowering firms’ borrowing and equity costs.

In China, Huang *et al.* (2018) identified an insurance-like effect of CSR in reducing the risk premium of corporate bonds. Hamrouni *et al.* (2020) highlight a negative impact of overall CSR disclosure on cost of debt in French firms by enhancing transparency and reducing perceived risk. Eliwa *et al.* (2021) across 15 EU countries and Raimo *et al.* (2021), using data from the S&P 1200 Global Index, find that better ESG performance and disclosure lead to more favorable debt financing conditions. Bacha *et al.* (2021) confirm this trend in French non-financial firms, observing preferential costs for socially responsible firms. Finally, Maaloul *et al.* (2023) link strong ESG performance and disclosure to enhanced corporate reputation and reduced debt costs, using data from Fortune’s “World’s Most Admired Companies.”

In contrast, using a sample of 332 firms from the Dow Jones Sustainability Indexes, Magnanelli and Izzo (2017) demonstrate that there is a positive relationship between CSR performance and the CD. Furthermore, Menz (2010), Hoepner *et al.* (2016) and Gigante and Manglaviti (2022) suggest that there is no effect of CSR performance on the CD.

Based on the aforementioned papers, previous research indicates that there may be a link between ESG performance and the CD. Regarding the negative effect of ESG performance on CD, shareholder theory [2] and stakeholder theory [3] suggest that ESG practices generate positive synergies between the company and its stakeholders and shareholders, leading to improved reputation and lower financing costs (Oikonomou *et al.*, 2014; Ge and Liu, 2015; Cooper and Uzun, 2015; Hamrouni *et al.*, 2020; Bacha *et al.*, 2021). Cooper and Uzun (2015) argue that companies participating in ESG activities can achieve a competitive edge and lower their capital costs. Based on stakeholder theory, Oikonomou *et al.* (2014) and Bacha *et al.* (2021) suggest that companies with better ESG performance may experience lower borrowing costs because of an improved reputation and decreased risk for the firm. Similarly, based on shareholder theory, Ge and Liu (2015) argue that ESG may increase CD because it can be seen as taking resources away from activities that are more profitable, which would raise financial risk and consequently, the CD.

The theory of legitimacy [4] provide another explanation for the negative effect of ESG performance on the CD. Eliwa *et al.* (2021) and Maaloul *et al.* (2023) find that firms that exhibit stronger ESG practices are seen as more legitimate by stakeholders, such as lenders, suggesting that this perception of legitimacy can result in reduced borrowing costs as lenders perceive these firms as less risky.

In contrast, regarding the positive relationship between ESG performance and the CD, according to agency theory [5], managers may use ESG as a strategy to distract attention, which could result in greater information asymmetry and higher debt costs (Bacha *et al.*, 2021). ESG may be viewed as a positive factor in determining the CD, as they have the potential to divert resources and increase the level of risk opacity (Hamrouni *et al.*, 2020; Bacha *et al.*, 2021).

Based on agency theory, this study assumes a positive relation between the level of ESG performance and the CD. In this regard, higher ESG performance may be perceived as an indication of managerial opportunism or a misalignment of managerial actions with the interests of debt holders, thereby increasing agency costs. This perceived misalignment and the added complexity in evaluating ESG initiatives lead lenders to demand higher risk premiums, ultimately resulting in elevated costs of debt. Therefore, we formally state the following hypothesis:

**H1.** There is a positive association between ESG performance and CD.

Finally, based on stakeholder and legitimacy theories, this study assumes a negative relation between the level of ESG information disclosed by firms and the CD. From a stakeholder perspective, high ESG performance demonstrates a firm’s commitment to reducing risks and meeting creditor expectations, thereby lowering perceived default risk. Legitimacy theory complements this by highlighting how ESG performance aligns the firm with societal norms, enhancing its legitimacy and reducing reputational and regulatory risks, which collectively incentivize creditors to offer lower interest rates. Therefore, we formally state the following hypothesis:

**H2.** There is a negative association between ESG performance and CD.

### 2.3 Environmental, social and governance performance and cost of equity capital

There is a controversial relationship between ESG performance and the CE which requires further examination (Ok and Kim, 2019; Chen *et al.*, 2023).

El Ghoul *et al.* (2011), Ng and Rezaee (2015), Crifo *et al.* (2015), Ok and Kim (2019) and Chen *et al.* (2023) consistently demonstrate a negative correlation between ESG performance and the CE capital. El Ghoul *et al.* (2011) specifically observe that US firms with higher corporate environmental responsibility incur lower equity costs compared to those with weaker ESG practices. They further note that investments in CSR activities contribute to this reduction. Crifo *et al.* (2015) highlight that neglecting ESG concerns restricts access to private equity, raising capital costs and ultimately diminishing shareholder value. Ng and Rezaee (2015) corroborate these findings using data from more than 3,000 firms in the KLD Stats database, but they pinpoint only environmental and governance aspects as influencing equity costs, with no significant impact from social sustainability factors. Contrarily, Ok and Kim (2019), analyzing South Korean firms, discover that superior ESG performance, particularly in social and governance realms, substantially lowers equity costs. They argue that investors anticipate long-term returns from such firms, accepting lower immediate returns. However, they find the environmental factor to be non-influential in this regard. Tanjung (2023) suggests that non-ESG Indonesian firms’ cost of capital is lower than that of ESG firms. Finally, Chen *et al.* (2023), examining Chinese listed firms, conclude that strong ESG performance not only reduces equity costs but also enhances shareholder profitability and attractiveness to investors, positively affecting stock prices.

In contrast, Dahiya and Singh (2021) observe a positive correlation between the ESG performance of Indian manufacturing firms listed on the National Stock Exchange of India and their CE capital. This implies that investors do not perceive ESG efforts as value-enhancing but rather view them as unnecessary expenses for companies. Similarly, by examining a selection of top global technology companies, Nazir *et al.* (2022) argue that there is a strong and meaningful correlation between ESG ratings and the CE. This implies that investors view ESG investments primarily as a financial burden.

In summary, the argument above implies that prior research suggests a potential correlation between ESG performance and CE. According to Jiménez and Grima (2020), there are three main theories used from previous empirical research to analyze the ESG performance–CE nexus. According to stakeholder theory, firms that participate in ESG activities can decrease their CE by reducing the risk associated with the company and increasing its value (Li and Foo, 2015; Martínez-Ferrero and García-Sánchez, 2017; Breuer *et al.*, 2018), suggesting that effectively managing and meeting the needs of various stakeholders results in a reduction in risk and positive financial outcomes for the company (El Ghoul *et al.*, 2011; Michaels and Grüning, 2017; El Ghoul *et al.*, 2018).

The theory of legitimacy also explains the negative effect of ESG performance on the CE (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Martínez-Ferrero and García-Sánchez, 2017; Michaels and Grüning, 2017), suggesting that a company’s activities to enhance its ESG performance are in line with societal values and expectations (Michaels and Grüning, 2017; Weber, 2018). It strengthens the company’s credibility, leading to potential financial advantages such as a reduced CE (Michaels and Grüning, 2017) because the company is perceived as less risky and more attuned to the long-term welfare of society (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Weber, 2018).

In contrast, according to agency theory, there is a positive relationship between ESG performance and the CE because companies with strong ESG performance are often seen as effectively managing potential risks related to the environment, society and governance (Breuer *et al.*, 2018; Nazir *et al.*, 2022). It means that they closely align with the interests of shareholders and create long-term value (Ng and Rezaee, 2015; Nazir *et al.*, 2022).

Based on agency theory, this study assumes a positive relation between the level of ESG performance and the CE capital. Under the perspective of agency theory, strong ESG performance reduces agency costs and information asymmetry by aligning managerial actions with shareholder interests and enhancing transparency, thereby lowering perceived risks. This improved trust and reputation attract a broader, sustainability-focused investor base, reducing the equity risk premium and, consequently, the CE capital. Therefore, we formally state the following hypothesis:

**H3.** There is a positive association between ESG performance and CE capital.

Finally, based on stakeholder and legitimacy theories, this study assumes a negative relation between the level of ESG information disclosed by firms and the CE capital. According to stakeholder theory, neglecting ESG concerns alienates investors and other key stakeholders, leading to financial uncertainty and a higher required rate of return. Legitimacy theory complements this by emphasizing that a lack of alignment with societal expectations undermines the company’s legitimacy, resulting in reputational damage and heightened regulatory or market risks. Together, these theories suggest that inadequate ESG performance amplifies systemic risks and erodes trust, ultimately increasing the cost of equity capital. Therefore, we formally state the following hypothesis:

**H4.** There is a negative association between ESG performance and CE capital.

## 3. Data and methodology

This study uses a balanced data set of 4,846 firms from 60 countries to explore the connection between the CD and ESG scores, as well as the relationship between the CE and ESG scores for firms in both civil law and common law countries. We selected 60 countries for our sample as ESG scores data was available only for these countries in the Refinitiv database, ensuring the robustness and consistency of our analysis. This sample is superior to other similar studies that used international ESG score data sets (see, Azmi *et al.*, 2021; Shin *et al.*, 2023; Yu and Van Luu, 2021). The analysis covers the period from 2015 to 2022, with 25,331 observations. Among these, 10,690 are from firms in 43 civil law countries, whereas 14,641 are from firms in 17 common law countries. The data variables used in this analysis, as presented in Table 1, were obtained from the Refinitiv platform [6]. The specific variables examined in this study include the cost of debt (CD), cost of equity (CE), ESG score (ESG), firm performance (FP), firm leverage (FLE), market capitalization (MC) and firm size (FS). To calculate the relationship between CD and ESG, as well as between CE and ESG, we used the following regressions:

Column 4 presents the outcomes of IV/2SLS, which addresses endogeneity bias and ensures result consistency. The findings support H4, indicating a negative and statistically significant association between ESG and CE (coefficient of ESG is −0.018; p < 0.01).

To provide a clearer understanding of the impact of ESG on CD and CE, we split our sample into civil law countries (Table 4, Panel B) and common law countries (Table 4, Panel C). This approach highlights potential differences between these two sub-samples and offers insights for firms operating in different legal environments. Similarly, we use different estimators to strengthen our empirical arguments for both sub-samples. The results of the relationship between ESG and CD for firms in civil law countries are presented in Table 4, Panel B. Specifically, we observe a negative and significant association between ESG and CD in all estimators at the 1% level. This suggests that firms in civil law countries with higher ESG scores are associated with lower CD. Overall, these empirical results confirm H2 for firms in civil law countries. Accordingly, the results in Columns 3–4 support H4 for firms in civil law countries, as the relationship between ESG and CE is negative and significant in all estimators at the 1% level. This finding supports the notion that firms in civil law countries are more inclined to invest in ESG initiatives because of weaker legal environments. As a result, these firms try to improve their ESG scores, signaling their commitment to stakeholders and potentially securing funding at lower rates to compensate for the weaker legal framework (see Priem and Gabellone, 2024). This suggests that creditors demand a premium because of fewer rights in these environments.

On the other hand, for the sample of firms in common law countries, Panel C of Table 4 shows that the relationship between ESG and CE is negative and significant in both estimators, while the relationship between ESG and CD is insignificant. This is consistent with the view that, in common law countries, firms may perceive ESG as a strategic choice rather than a regulatory imperative (Chouaibi *et al.*, 2022).

### 4.2 Additional analysis

In this subsection, we explore the distinct effect of the environmental, social and governance pillars on CD and CE. Table 5 shows that, in civil law countries, all three ESG pillars have a negative and significant impact on CD. This suggests that firms in these countries engage in ESG activities to improve sustainability performance, leading to lower CD. For the CE, the results indicate that only the environmental and social pillars have a negative and significant impact on CE for firms in civil law countries. In common law countries, however, none of the ESG pillars show a significant effect on CD. Nonetheless, all three ESG pillars exhibit a negative and significant impact on CE, suggesting a different dynamic in how ESG performance influences financing costs in these legal systems (see Priem and Gabellone, 2024). Our results clearly demonstrate that investing in ESG can lead to a lower cost of capital, particularly for firms operating in weaker legal environments (see Priem and Gabellone, 2024).

## 5. Conclusion

While previous empirical research has yielded inconclusive results regarding the association between ESG performance and the components of cost of capital, this study aims to provide clarity by investigating the impact of the civil law or common law system on this relationship. Drawing from a sample of 4,846 firms across 60 countries, categorized into either civil law or common law systems, we employ OLS and IV/2SLS. Our findings reveal a consistent negative relationship between ESG and the components of cost of capital for firms operating within civil law systems, regardless of whether measured by the CD or the CE. In contrast, firms in common law systems tend to display a negative relationship solely with the CE. Additionally, our analysis highlights that the influence of each ESG pillar on the components of the cost of capital varies across these two legal systems.

Our study offers some important implications. Managers can benefit from these findings by investing in ESG initiatives to reduce the cost of capital and enhance profitability. Investors can also benefit by capitalizing on the advantages of investing in firms with strong ESG performance, which may lower risk and promote long-term value creation. From a social and environmental perspective, our results suggest that firms investing in both these two dimensions of ESG not only reduce their cost of capital more effectively, but also contribute to long-term social and environmental value creation. In practice, this aligns with the growing demand for more sustainable businesses and societies.

We acknowledge certain limitations that may inspire future research. First, our sample is based on firms with ESG scores. Future research could examine the cost of capital for firms without ESG scores and compare the findings with our results. Second, our data set is retrieved from the Refinitiv database. Expanding the sample to include international data from other databases, such as Bloomberg ESG database, would be valuable for further validating our results. As our study focuses on listed companies, investigating this issue for non-listed firms would also be an important extension. Finally, the role of technology cannot be overlooked. Future research could explore the mediating role of Extensible Business Reporting Language, artificial intelligence and blockchain technology in the relationship between ESG and the cost of capital (see Abhishek *et al.*, 2024).
