### Abstract
The relationship between ESG and firm value has been a relevant subject of study in recent years. We conducted a hybrid literature review to understand the literature's findings on this relationship and its implications in terms of cost of capital. First, a keyword co-occurrence analysis on a 122 ABS ranked journals articles selection from Scopus database was adopted to identify and investigate the main research fields of the current literature. Then a content analysis through the bibliographic coupling of the most globally cited contributions was made, defining a final sample of 50 articles obtained through a minimum threshold of at least 15 total global citations (TGCs). We found that studies on the cost of debt configuration are more aimed at determining the implications on firm value, while most contributions on the cost of equity focus on the assessment of the risk–return profile for the investor or the construction of an ESG portfolio. Furthermore, we found that most of the literature has a consensus view on the lack of transparency behind the ESG ratings and their construction methodology, stating that disagreement on ESG ratings not only limits the results of empirical analysis, but can also negatively affect firm value due to a higher level of uncertainty.

**KEYWORDS**
cost of capital, ESG, firm value, review, sustainability rating

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Does the implementation of ESG policies increase firm value? To date, this question is under consideration in several areas of research, gaining prominence from the most recent evolution of the multiple contexts associated with the economic system, which historically relates a specific topic of corporate social responsibility (CSR) known as ESG to the quality of its disclosure and ranking methodologies in terms of corporate risk–return profile measured through its cost of capital (Brooks & Oikonomou, 2018; Fatemi et al., 2018; Sassen et al., 2016). This approach has been adopted and developed by heterogeneous studies that rely on different research methodologies and theories whose scope of work is to investigate how the firm value can be affected by the ESG factor through its implications on the firm risk profile (He et al., 2023).

The firm has been considered as one of the main instruments for achieving economic, environmental, and social sustainability, but whether the pursuit of these objectives has a positive impact on its own economic value has been one of the most relevant research question among the literature studies (D'Amato & Falivena, 2020; Kumawat & Patel, 2022; Rjiba et al., 2020). On the other hand, the assessment of this value configuration is a well-defined process, characterized by its methods, models, formulae, and criteria (Damodaran, 2016). With the advent of various ESG measurement methodologies, such as ESG scores, ESG indexes, and ESG quality of disclosure, the level of corporate commitment to sustainability has also become ascribable to a quantitative measure, meaning that the ESG connection with the firm value can be investigated in terms of risk–return profiles on a new level of potential empirical evidence (Billio et al., 2021). Nevertheless, this scenario is also characterized by several issues, which are typically related to multiple ESG metrics adopted by different agencies (Dorfleitner et al., 2015) since different operators imply different methodologies that are not specifically disclosed as they often generate dissimilar ESG ratings for the same firm (Berg et al., 2022).

The interest in the relationship between ESG and firm value is recent and complex, but nonetheless of growing concern to both the literature, managers, and policy makers. In particular, the latter, motivated by the pursuit of public welfare, cannot but express their support for economic operators investing in sustainable assets, assigning these policies a priority role in their agenda, implementing regulations that significantly affect management strategy. However, we still question whether the pursuit of these investments and their potential benefit to the community can be considered an appropriate driver also for maximizing firm value. If this is the case, ESGs should precisely promote those companies that adopt them, thus penalizing those that do not (Baldini et al., 2018). Otherwise, the pursuit of ESG objectives may harm the system in the long term, especially if shareholders are not aware of the real problems that CSR aims to solve (Flammer, 2013). Nonetheless, have been several years since the priority given to the welfare of the community has been studied as a crucial factor for the legitimacy of the firm to operate due to its strong connection with public interest (Deegan et al., 2002; Dowling & Pfeffer, 1975). However, it has not yet been defined whether the firms, especially those owned by noninstitutional investors, should really have an obligation to pursue these CSR objectives, even if this issue has been studied through theories, which are 10 years old and still relevant to date (Campbell, 2007; Friedman, 1970). For these reasons, we regard the breadth of literature on this topic as an essential field of research: if this relationship exists and benefits the firm valorization process, it can be considered a concrete reason to develop such policies, both for managers and policy makers, as they can pursue both economic and social interests.

Because of the complexity and the wide range of contributions that are aimed at investigating this issue, following the approach proposed by other recent review-type studies (Rahi et al., 2024) we pursued our scope of work through a hybrid literature review methodology. Including both bibliometric and content analysis techniques, we were able to define a comprehensive framework focusing on the relationship between the ESG topic, identified from the broader CSR area of research, and the firm value, through one of its fundamental driver expression of its risk–return profile and widely adopted in multiple CSR recent studies (Bosi et al., 2022; Fandella et al., 2023; Khanchel & Lassoued, 2022; Prasad et al., 2022). Therefore, the intention of this contribution is to propose a literature review that has sought empirical evidence of ESG impacts in terms of firm value through its own cost of capital, that is, we aim to represent the current state of the literature trying to answer the following research question: what has the literature achieved about the relationship between ESG and firm value in terms of cost of capital? We sought to find out whether the literature was able to answer this question through the analysis of corporate risk–return profile, which is a fundamental firm metrics that can be highly affected by the ESG factor (He et al., 2023).

Given the relevant implications of these studies for both academic, management, and institutional purposes, as to our knowledge this field of research has not yet been subjected to a specific literature review process, the intention beneath this study is to carry out an investigation aimed at contributing to the firm value effect through cost of capital implications of ESGs. The need for this type of work is also supported by the fact that even the literature about the broader subject of CSR relationship with alternative firm performance measures, such as the financial one, lacks a comprehensive framework to date (Rahi et al., 2024). Moreover, given the significant role of the firm risk configuration for ESG studies, we also found no review-type studies about the sole ESG and cost of capital relationship, aside for a few contributions that involved this association as secondary evidence or as a broader review result (Kraussl et al., 2024; Jain & Tripathi, 2023; Bosi et al., 2022). Among these studies, we found Jain and Tripathi (2023) work to be a significant input for our scope of work, as they also highlighted a significant interest from the literature about the cost of capital and firm value relation with the ESG subject.

The paper is structured in five main sections: the first one focuses on the theoretical framework; Section 2 describes the methods and criteria we used to develop the samples we used for the hybrid literature review and its analysis criteria; Section 3 focuses on the bibliometric analysis; Section 4 focuses on the content analysis. All the evidence we found is then discussed in the final section.

## 2 | THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

The potential benefits of implementing ESG initiatives are a well-established paradigm (Baldini et al., 2018), but as a persistent positive relation regarding these activities and firm value has not been proven yet (Gillan et al., 2021), its existence and potential systemic consequences in the long run based on the most accredited valuation approaches (Damodaran, 2016) are still debated. To measure the firm value is a whole different process from the one aimed at measuring the value generated for the socio-economic system in which the enterprise operates: it is not uncommon to observe companies from nongreen sectors or that adopt nonESG policies whose stocks achieved return levels equal or even greater than the green ones (Larcker & Watts, 2020). On the other hand, is also well-known that companies implementing ESG policies experience an incentive effect from the investment community, gaining more trust from the market, which usually increases their prices by lowering the risk borne by the investors (Ardia et al., 2023). In any case, performance is idiosyncratically linked to any new activity or policy that a firm decides to pursue (Taouab & Issor, 2019). For this reason, some studies have analyzed the consequences of developing green strategies in terms of firm performance finding that they can translate into different benefits such as cost savings, increased markups, gaining access to subsidies and grants, or borrowing debt capital at better market conditions (Dal Maso et al., 2023). However, it has also been empirically proven that such consequences on the firm economic return are not always incremental as nonESG activities can outperform even common benchmarks in terms of both magnitude, frequency (Fabozzi et al., 2008), and risk (Rezaeian & Racine, 2024).

This firm value proposition and its declination in terms of CSR have been studied by many authors who relied on different methods and theories. The scope of work adopted for this study focuses on a specific CSR subject, which consists of a group of three ethical finance pillars (Environment, Social, and Governance) that when combined can represent a specific assessment target, also known as ESG factor (UN, 2004). These tools are meant to provide information about the sustainability impact that a firm has on environment and society. This is a constituent feature of the sustainable finance process, which is aimed at integrating the investor decisions with ESG concerns whether in terms of environment (e.g., climate change, pollution, and resource depletion issues), social (e.g., inclusion, labor practices, and inequality issues), or governance (e.g., board organization and executive remuneration issues) (Naseer et al., 2024). This assessment is usually made through the so-called ESG rating, which is an instrument that defines these three dimensions with an overall evaluation. It is a sustainability rating characterized by a specific focus on environmental, social, and governance issues as well as being primarily intended for investment decisions. Consequently, ESG ratings are sustainability ratings, but the reverse is not always true, as the latter should be intended as a more generic type of measure, which may include dimensions beyond ESG factors and may also be intended for purposes other than investment decisions (EU, 2020).

There are different approaches to calculate an ESG rating in quantitative terms (so-called ESG score), as it can be based on different sub-scores at multiple sub-levels involving various methodologies adopted by different operators. For example, it can be based on the aggregation of the three single pillars scores (E, S, or G scores) each measured through multiple subfactors scores (e.g., the E score can be measured through emissions levels, climate risks exposure, waste management quality, and natural resources depletion). However, ESG ratings are not the only tools that can be used to assess a firm's ESG performance. Indeed, the ESG concept can be related to different measures, applications and approaches, ranging from an overall quantitative rating (ESG score) to the nonfinancial reporting strategies (so-called ESG information) or the more specific nonfinancial disclosure (so-called ESG disclosure). For this reason, to reduce any bias involving all these different ESG assessments, we adopted the general definition of “ESG topic,” wherewith the “topic” term is to be considered just as a comprehensive way to include all these ESG declinations. Nevertheless, since different theoretical proposals involve different ESG topics, it is essential to define the theoretical framework that relates these subjects to the firm value proposition.

To date, the ESG topic is related to the firm value through different theories, some of which can be traced back to proposals from over decades ago, resulting in a complex and significant evolution that needs to be addressed with an interdisciplinary approach. Among all the main theories, the dichotomy between the Shareholder Theory (Friedman, 1970) and the Stakeholder Theory (Freeman, 1984; Freeman & David, 1983) have been historically one of the most debated (Freeman et al., 2010; Laplume et al., 2008). Both theories approach the process of maximizing firm value, but with two different proportions, which differ in terms of firm social responsibility objectives and recipients of the value created. In the first case, the only parties who should benefit from the firm valorization are the shareholders, and this can only be achieved through profit maximization. Therefore, this theory denies the existence of a CSR purpose, adopting an approach strongly linked to its financial performance as the main and only constituent for the firm value definition. The Stakeholder Theory, unlike the Shareholder Theory, implies that firm value is maximized only by satisfying the different interests of all the stakeholders involved, as they all invest various resources, which lead to different claims depending on their type (Clarkson, 1995). In this case, CSR naturally becomes a requirement as it is fundamental for the satisfaction of all the company's stakeholders, which must be measured in both financial and nonfinancial terms. In order to relate the firm's value proposition to these theories, empirical studies that have addressed the ESG topic had to adopt a sustainability measure that can be related to both economic and environmental, social, and governance sustainable performance of the firm. These contributions usually involve as a quantitative assessment of nonfinancial performance the so-called ESG rating, also known as ESG score (Drempetic et al., 2020), while others prefer to rely on another qualitative measure, known as ESG information, which refers to the nonfinancial data provided by companies in terms of environmental, social, and governance objectives and results (Tsang et al., 2023). Related to Stakeholder Theory, the Legitimacy Theory (Dowling & Pfeffer, 1975; Suchman, 1995) has also become a relevant reference for economic studies on ESG and firm value (Rahi et al., 2024). This theory states that the firm's resilience and performance are linked to its conduct and behavior, which must be aligned with the society's values and expectations. A dominant role is attributed to the firm's ability to communicate with its stakeholders, which can only be accomplished by adopting a proper disclosure policy. For this reason, the ESG topic involved in this type of investigations takes on a particular configuration, usually measured and quantified in terms of the quality of its disclosure, also known as ESG disclosure (Fatemi et al., 2018; Tsang et al., 2023).

All the theories cited so far recognize the role of the investor, who has an essential function in terms of firm value as he bears the firms' risk in exchange for an expected return on his investment. However, this is not a matter of prioritization among different stakeholder interests, but just the historically proved empirical evidence that the structure of the firm's equity has a fundamental impact on its own value (McConnell & Servaes, 1990). A growing and healthy business is fundamental to the welfare of society, as it creates new jobs, new products, new revenues for governments, and new ideas that, if properly developed, can enhance the lifestyle of individuals. Nevertheless, due to the nature of the capital allocation process, a rational investor will always priorities economic return as, in accordance with the fundamental principles of finance, alternative investments characterized by the same level of risk and higher payoffs will always be preferable (Cochrane, 2011; Fisher & Hall, 1969; Heaton, 2002). For these reasons, another relevant theory that relates firm value to the ESG topic is the Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952), which implies the study of stock prices, their return, volatility and implications for the investor in terms of portfolio construction strategies and risk diversification methodologies (Fama, 1977; Fama & French, 2004; Lintner, 1965; Markowitz, 1952; Ross, 1976; Sharpe, 1964). For all these studies, the approach is usually linked to the ESG rating configuration (Halbritter & Dorfleitner, 2015) but can also be developed through the construction of an ad hoc measure usually involving ESG securities and their risk–return profiles (Girerd-Potin et al., 2014). Comparing these theoretical implications with those discussed so far, depending on the theoretical framework of the research, it seems clear that the ESG topic can be declined into various and diverse patterns that are measured through different ESG configurations capable of defining the firm value in multiple ways.

Another relevant theory for the ESG and firm value studies is the so-called Agency Theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Its main feature is the ownership separation from control: the owner (i.e., shareholder) delegates an agent (i.e., management) to pursue his or her interests through the administration of the company. This theory focuses on the potential conflict of interest that can arise through this power delegation system, which occurs if the agent starts to pursue its own interests instead of the shareholder ones. This happens because if the latter has a long-term orientation, the former, on the contrary, is typically driven by short-term interests. As a rule, the more information the agent has with respect to the owner, the higher the agency costs, which means that if the owner implements an adequate exchange of information with his management, it can reduce this information asymmetry issue. For these reasons, this theory is directly related to nonfinancial ESG disclosure, as the choice to pursue ESG objectives results strongly dependent from the firm governance structure (Yu et al., 2018).

To complete the theoretical framework that is usually adopted to define the relationship between ESG and firm value, another essential theory is the so-called resource-based view (Penrose, 2009). In this case, the assumption is that a firm's internal resources and their interconnections are the essential features for a long-term competitive advantage, that is, the key factor in determining its own value. This approach refers to ESG for two main reasons: first, the company's reputation, which is also a function of its ESG activities, is regarded as an intangible asset that can be measured as the level of trust that stakeholders have in the firm, meaning that it can be assumed as a measure of the firm's value related to the stakeholders themselves (Warin & Teodoresco, 2012). Second, this asset concurs to the value creation process of the firm as one of its most important strategic resources (Pfister et al., 2020; Warin & Teodoresco, 2012). For these reasons, the implication of this theory usually requires two configurations of ESG topics, namely ESG rating and ESG disclosure (Christensen et al., 2022).

Amidst all these theories and their ability to relate different values configurations to different ESG dimensions, it is essential to clarify when and how these mechanisms have a quantitative impact on the firm valuation process. Indeed, our research aims to define the perimeter of the field of literature on this subject involving one of the fundamental parameters of the firm value measurement, that is, the cost of capital (Modigliani & Miller, 1963). This tool has been often related to the ESG topic among CSR and management academic studies, becoming a recognized peculiar feature of the firm value paradigm for economic field of research (Christensen et al., 2021; Gillan et al., 2021; Usul & Yilmaz, 2023). However, as pointed out by some recent contributions (Branco, 2024), this approach can lead to a restrict financial literature excluding the other aforementioned CSR theories and relative implications. To produce a cohesive framework and overcome this limitation, we adopted an interdisciplinary approach without preferring one research area over the other.

The choice to use the cost of capital as a firm value driver depends on multiple factors; first, it is not uncommon to rely on the cost of capital for corporate sustainability studies, demonstrating both the interdisciplinarity and the diffusion of this analysis tool also for nonstrictly financial studies (Agliardi & Agliardi, 2019; Lemma et al., 2019; Steffen, 2020; Zhai et al., 2022). Second, the cost of capital represents a firm risk quantitative measure evolved from over a century of research, starting from the beginning of the twentieth century (Fisher, 1907) and resulting in a powerful measure for both academic studies and professional applications to date. Finally, the mechanism by which the cost of capital is linked to the firm value is relatively intuitive: being a measure of the firm risk, the higher the cost of capital the greater the uncertainty of its future economic and financial results and, consequently, the lower its value. Consequently, comparing two firms that are identical but differ only in terms of cost of capital, the company with a lower cost of capital, which translates into a minor risk associated with its expected returns, will result in a higher firm value (Damodaran, 2007). This is a solid mechanism that allows to overcome many limitations that can be encountered with other performance indicators. Hence, it is also possible to compare different studies based on different theories, relating their findings to a homogeneous firm value approach.

We did not find any study aimed at investigating this research area of the literature through a review-type contribution, as the only few studies we identified as potentially related to our research include only the cost of capital subject but just as a result and not as a research methodological input (Kraussl et al., 2024; Jain & Tripathi, 2023; Bosi et al., 2022). Moreover, these contributions do not involve any relationship between the firm's value, its risk profile, and alternative ESG topic configurations as part of their research objectives. However, a peculiar input for our scope of work was given by Jain and Tripathi (2023), who summarized the overall ESG field of research through an investigation involving a content analysis on 190 papers. They found 11 distinct themes that are related to the ESG dimension with two consisting, respectively, in the cost of capital and firm value. Because the cost of capital is an essential measure involved in the firm valuation process, these results together with the literature framework discussed so far, support both the novelty of our approach, which is aimed at further investigating the ESG implications on the cost of capital, and our scope of work, which is aimed at understanding how this relationship can eventually be oriented to explain any effect on the firm value by the current state of the literature.

## 3 | METHODOLOGY

Following the approach suggested by Rahi et al. (2024) and Gaur and Kumar (2018), we implemented a hybrid review by developing both citation analysis and content analysis methods. We also involved multiple literature review techniques, both quantitative and qualitative, to reduce discretionary choices that could create biases for our research results (Bretas & Alon, 2021; Zupic & Cater, 2015) resulting in a nonrigid approach (Massaro et al., 2016).

The first step was to identify all scientific contributions useful for the purposes of our analysis. To this end, we used the Scopus database, which contains a significant number of peer-reviewed journals that exceeds by more than three times the ones contained in other databases (Bretas & Alon, 2021). Then we implemented a two-factor search criterion via Scopus search field “Article Title, Abstract, Keywords.” The first factor was aimed at capturing all contributions on the ESG topic, while the second factor was aimed at relating it to all models and definitions commonly used by both theory and best practice in reference to the cost of capital. The first factor was searched through the query “ESG” OR “sustainability rating,” searching in both cases for all the contributions that could potentially investigate any type of ESG topic. Furthermore, the term “sustainability rating” was adopted both as an alternative to the ESG score keyword as well as an alternative term for a more generic ESG rating measure, due to its frequent adoption in this field of research (Drempetic et al., 2020; Khan et al., 2016; Parguel et al., 2011). This method allowed us to have a comprehensive database, including studies that also dealt with unconventional ESG profiles (Girerd-Potin et al., 2014). The second factor was identified through the following input query: (“COE” OR “WACC” OR “cost of capital” OR “weighted average cost of capital” OR “CAPM” OR “capital asset pricing model” OR “cost of equity” OR “cost of debt” OR “beta” OR “equity risk premium” OR “market risk premium”). These keywords are all relied to the cost of capital definitions, and it core components. In particular, all variables commonly used to construct the cost of capital both in his equity (i.e., coe, cost of equity) and debt (i.e., cost of debt) versions were researched through the CAPM and the WACC models, involving both their abbreviated and extended forms (i.e., CAPM, WACC, capital asset pricing model, and weighted average cost of capital) and essential components (i.e., beta, equity risk premium, market risk premium, cost of equity, and cost of debt). At the end of this phase, we identified a total of 314 papers.

A second level of screening was then implemented aimed at detecting all contributions belonging to the subject areas of “Economic, Econometric and Finance” and “Business Management and Accounting.” Social and environmental studies were not considered because they are not related to the economic research field of firm value. This criterion was applied for a period between January 2000 and June 2024 included, and only papers published and completed in English were searched excluding all review-type contributions. With this second level of selection, 161 articles were identified.

Then, we performed a third level of screening removing all those sources, which may not be considered genuine scientific productions (Morris et al., 2009): through the most recent Academic Journal Guide database (2021), only ABS-ranked journals were selected. With this last screening phase, an initial sample of 122 articles was acquired.

Following the approaches adopted by several authors (Fatima et al., 2023; Jain & Tripathi, 2023; Rahi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023), we performed a bibliometric analysis including co-citation, co-authorship, and keyword co-occurrence, running both the Biblioshiny R package and VOS viewer software on the initial sample obtained as shown in the table above (Table 1). After this process, we improved the quality of the sample through a bibliometric coupling analysis via the VOS Viewer software. In this case, starting from the initial sample of 122 articles and following the global citation relevance criterion historically proposed by Moravcsik and Murugesan (1975), we selected only documents with a TGC count of at least 15 based on Scopus database (Pillania, 2011). With this fourth and final phase of screening we obtained the final sample, consisting of 50 articles that we used for the content analysis.

Finally, following the methodology developed by other reviews on ESGs (Coelho et al., 2023; Jain & Tripathi, 2023), we proceeded with a qualitative content analysis based on the results of the bibliographic coupling process that involved the reading and evaluation of all the articles we gathered within our final sample. Since our aim was to show the state of the literature on ESG and firm value, we assessed each article according to its affiliation with the firm value estimation process, labeling them as positive if the investigation led to an increase in terms of firm value and vice versa if negative. We also assessed them as “uncertain” if the study concluded that there is an ambiguous effect in terms of firm value, involving both the positive and negative outcomes. Coherently with our research question, this classification was only made if the result is derived from the ESG and firm value relationship through the cost of capital implication. For this purpose, we adopted the following criterion: a positive effect occurs if the ESG topic used by the authors decreases the cost of capital by incrementing the firm value and vice versa in the case of a negative effect. For this reason, the results are classified in terms of “Effect on Firm Value”. Consequently, if the sought relationship is absent, we marked the article as nondeterminable (n.d.). This approach occurred even if such a connection could potentially be derived from the document, but only through our deductions. We decided against such a subjective interpretation because if the structure of the article is made to serve a different purpose from the one we are investigating, our interpretation may not be consistent with the original structure and methodology adopted by the authors. Moreover, our priority was to minimize all the biases, and this cannot be achieved by forcing a different result even if it might serve our review. However, if an analysis originally aimed at a different research question was still able to demonstrate an effect on the firm value through ESG implications on the cost of capital as a secondary outcome, we classified its result.

## 4 | BIBLIOMETRIC ANALYSIS

The initial sample consists of 122 documents (articles), 64 sources (journals), and 328 authors, characterized by an annual growth rate of 29.6%, an average document age of 1.98 years, 38.7 average citations per document, 344 authors keywords, and 6.944 references.

Figure 1 highlights a clear early stage of the literature: ESG studies involving the cost of capital are in a fast-growing phase, which began around the 2019–2020 period. Furthermore, the first document has been published in 2011, followed by several years with no significant increase in terms of publications as in 2018 the overall production counts a total of only 7 contributions.

The production starts slowly from 2011 and shows a sharp increase starting from 2020, reaching over 120 articles by 2024.](FIGURE 1 Annual scientific cumulate production (articles) from 2011 to June 2024.)

Most of these articles were written by three authors, while only 10.6% (13 out of a total of 122) were single authored. Through the analysis of Lotka's Law (Lopez-Fernandez et al., 2016) we found that 307 authors wrote one article each, with a frequency of 93.6%, 17 authors wrote two articles each with a frequency of 5.2% and only four authors wrote three articles each with a frequency of 1.2%, showing that most researchers were involved in several single contributions. So far, the output provided by our analysis indicates that there is little to no follow-up of accomplished studies made by the same authors after their first publications.

To establish whether there is a lack of dominant propositions, we searched the most local cited references: overall, the documents within our sample involved 6.944 different references that were cited 7.750 times, for an average citation per reference equal to 1.12. Moreover, the most cited paper only has 14 local citations. On the other side, 6.455 documents, which are equal to almost the 93.0% of the total production, were cited only once implying a high level of heterogeneity among the reference structure based on local citation. This evidence supports the presence of a complex research structure characterized by a noncohesive scientific production based on an assorted mix of approaches, which also suggests the absence of any dominant propositions.

Focusing on the main sources resulting from this analysis, we elaborated the cumulative journal production curve to identify the topmost productive journals and their evolution over time since 2011 (Figure 2).

Figure 2 shows the cumulative production of the most productive journals over time: the total production of the most productive sources reached a cumulative peak of 36 documents in June 2024 (29.5% of the total production), with the first journal reaching a total of 10 articles published to date that is equal to 8.2% of the total production made by all the 64 journals. Furthermore, the most productive journals production starts to become significant only from the 2019–2020 period. All these results are aligned and coherent with the overall production trend shown within Figure 1, supporting the same conclusions made before in terms of early stage of the literature and absence of dominant literature statements.

To validate the identification of the top journals (Figure 2), we followed the approach suggested by Bookstein (1979) by conducting a Bradford's Law test (Bradford, 1934), which estimates the impact of sources based on an exponential diminishing return criterion in order to identify the most important journals for this specific research field. (Figure 3).

According to the Bradford's Law test, the top five journals identified through the cumulative production analysis (Figure 2) are also to be considered the most influencing sources on the researched topic to date. However, 45 journals out of a total of 64 (70,3%) only have one article published up to June 2024, which is another significant indicator of the literature's early stage. Moreover, given the fast-growing trend of the scientific production, it confirms both a significant rising interest on the subject suggesting no literature dominant propositions to date about the ESG relationship with the cost of capital.

### 4.1 | Keywords co-occurrence analysis on the initial sample

To understand the thematic configuration of the field of research, an exploration of the author keyword co-occurrence is required (Donthu et al., 2021). Because of the young age of the literature, but also considering the need for a clear and conceptual classification, we applied for all the author keywords with a co-occurrence level at least equal to two. In doing so we were able to remove the occasional keywords co-occurrences without penalizing the taxonomy process.

Figure 4 shows the overall conceptual framework based on the keywords used by the authors, who produced the 122 studies gathered within our initial sample. The keyword co-occurrence's structure is based on a total of 42 terms (i.e., nodes) classified into three colored clusters (Red, Green, and Blue), which are connected through a total of 204 links. These connections represent the keywords co-occurrence combinations among the scientific production to date. The width of the lines represents the total link strength (TLS) between each keyword: the thicker the line the higher the co-occurrence frequency. The distance between each node represents the strength of their relationship while the dimension of the nodes represents the single keyword occurrence frequency.

The keyword co-occurrence analysis shows a complex network where the most frequent terms are ESG (48 occurrences, 35 connections), cost of equity (20 occurrences, 18 connections), CSR (18 occurrences, 24 connections), and cost of debt (17 occurrences, 23 connections), resulting in literature structure suitable for our research purposes. Moreover, Figure 4 provides clear evidence that the term ESG is significantly more relevant than those related to the cost of capital. Consequently, these subjects—whether in terms of total cost of capital, cost of equity, or cost of debt—should be considered subordinate to ESG studies. However, the strong link between these different cost of capital configurations and ESG ratings, disclosure, and various types of performance is also significant, suggesting the existence of multiple ways to measure and approach different ESG topics as occurred through the literature framework section. In terms of interconnections between thematic groups, one of the most important subjects is ESG disclosure, which is strongly related to both the red and blue clusters through, respectively, the “cost of equity” and “cost of debt” nodes, signifying that disclosure is an essential area of interest for both types of studies. Other relevant connections between the clusters are made through the “sustainable finance,” “governance,” and primarily “environment” keywords. In particular, the cost of debt area of research is linked to ESG through the intermediate “CSR” node (blue cluster), which is also explained through the “environment,” “social,” and “governance” pillars (green cluster), with the environment pillar being the most significant both in terms of interconnections and proximity to the center of the keyword co-occurrence structure.

### 4.2 | The blue thematic group analysis

The blue cluster is primarily related to the cost of debt subject through a predominant interest in the ESG disclosure topic. Unlike the red cluster, the cost of debt area of research appears to be farther located from the ESG keyword, even though a strong connection between the two terms is also acknowledged. The blue cluster's main keyword that is capable of bridging “ESG” with “cost of debt” is “CSR,” indicating also a significant dependence on CSR studies to investigate the researched issue. However, it is also possible to highlight the three ESG dimensions (Environment, Social, and Governance) as they are all well-distinguished green cluster nodes, with “environment” and “social” being the most important ones also for the blue cluster due to their location.

Focusing on the left side of this thematic group, it is possible to gather significant information about the main features associated with cost of debt studies. In terms of performance, “environmental performance” and “sustainable performance” are essential features associated with those studies that appear to be focused mainly on bonds. Moreover, “tobin's q” also emerges as a fundamental metric associated with those contributions involving both firm performance and sustainability in terms ESG and cost of debt relationship studies. Another relevant feature of this cluster is provided by the positioning of the “emerging markets” node, which is one of the farthest from the “cost of debt,” being located between the green cluster nodes while still being a blue cluster keyword. Since the green cluster is more focused on portfolio construction and asset allocation strategies, this scenario implies that “emerging markets” is developed by both thematic groups, with a stronger derivation from the ESG and CSR subjects.

In terms of peculiarities, the “media” and “default Risk” keywords are both to be considered peculiar blue cluster topics, even if their frequency and relationship with ESG do not appear to be relevant. This suggests the presence of only a few contributions about both these issues, providing evidence of underdeveloped areas of research: first, the default risk, which is a common area of research for cost of debt financial studies, is significantly less explored in terms of ESG relations. This research gap is reinforced by the fact that the “default risk” term is related to “cost of debt,” “ESG,” and “ESG disclosure” through a very weak connection, confirming the need for a broader set of applications aimed at fully investigating this topic. Second, the “media” keyword is one of the most external nodes among the whole cluster, indicating a subject that is still not fully consolidated into this thematic group. Nevertheless, this keyword appears to be related only to “ESG,” “CSR,” and “cost of debt,” while it could be an interesting issue also for other studies involving the mediating effect of media in terms of nonfinancial disclosure impact on the ESG-cost of capital relationship. Finally, “debt costs,” which we expected to be another peculiar blue cluster feature, is located at the top-left of the red cluster having no linkage with the cost of debt one. This suggests the presence of a different thematic group involving expenses related to debt financing, indicating the presence of an interdisciplinary research field. Finally, in terms of performance, multiple types of measurement are involved, characterized by different positioning, frequency, and connections. The “firm performance” node next to “sustainability” is significantly more relevant than the others, but it is also located considerably far from the “cost of debt” keyword being also associated with the “disclosure” green cluster term. These results suggest diversified content structures and investigation approaches, but also imply a less cohesive and focused methodology. This may not be considered an overall negative feature, as it implies a wider set of initiatives and methods, but it also denotes the absence of dominant literature in terms of performance measurement approaches.

### 4.3 | The red thematic group analysis

While the blue cluster is essentially centered around the cost of debt, the red cluster has its center on the “cost of equity” and “cost of capital” terms. This cluster also includes the “ESG” node, which is the core of the overall keyword co-occurrence structure. However, “ESG” is not located in the middle of the red cluster but at the center of the whole keyword co-occurrence configuration, meaning that even if cost of equity studies should be more related to the ESG topic, it still is also a core feature for both the blue and green thematic groups. Moreover, while for the blue cluster the most significant explanatory feature associated with the cost of debt is “ESG disclosure,” in this case “ESG ratings” is shown as one of its fundamental descriptive factors. Consequently, most of these studies are expected to be focused on the relationship between stocks and their issuer ESG rating rather than their ESG disclosure. However, “ESG disclosure” is a key feature also for the red cluster as it is strongly linked to “cost of equity” and located between the two groups, suggesting that both ESG topics are relevant for this thematic group.

The “beta” subject, which is an expression of firm systematic risk based on the Capital Asset Pricing Model (i.e., CAPM), it is not directly related this risk–return configuration, even if it is a fundamental parameter for the cost of equity calculation. This suggests that “beta” is primarily used as an independent variable, provided that also its positioning is one of the farthest from the cost of equity and ESG connection among the whole red cluster. Another isolated red node from the “ESG” keyword is “family business,” suggesting a research interest for this specific company type among the cost of equity studies. However, it is positioning, dimensions, and interconnections imply an underdeveloped state of the literature on this thematic area in relationship with ESG.

The “information asymmetry” keyword, which is related to “ESG ratings,” “cost of equity,” and “cost of debt,” implies the effects of heterogeneity among different ESG scores and its implications in terms of cost of capital. Nevertheless, it also appears to be one of the smallest red nodes, meaning that only few studies are investigating this issue to date. Closer to the center of the cluster's structure is “REM,” which stands for “Real Earnings Management,” another relevant area of research usually associated with the “information asymmetry” issue. REM is typically opposed to AEM (i.e., “Accrual-based Earning Management,”) which is a practice based on manipulating accounting data for financial report production purposes. REM, on the other hand, is based on real actions from the management, which are also made to influence the financial report results. This must be considered another potentially significant area of research, but, as shown by the keyword co-occurrence structure, it still appears to be an understudied topic related to ESG, suggesting the presence of another literature gap. However, this subject is closely related to “ESG ratings” and “cost of equity” meaning that, differently from “family business,” it has a better orientation toward the relationship between ESG and cost of capital.

“Capital structure” should represent the so-called firm's gearing, meaning a metric capable of defining different combinations of equity capital and debt capital that can be measured for different context, such as for a single company, industry, or sector. For this reason, it is also to be considered as a fundamental feature for the financial leverage structure, which is another topic strongly related to cost of capital since it can affect its measurement in all its configurations (Damodaran, 2007). Moreover, given the relevance and complexity that characterize this topic, even if its connections suggest the presence of multiple contributions about its employment regarding ESG ratings investigations, surprisingly it do not have any strong link with “cost of capital” nor “cost of equity” keywords, highlighting that the “capital structure” measure is mainly used as an independent variable, suggesting the same issue already discussed with reference to the “beta” subject.

In terms of performance, the main types of performances are the financial and social ones. “Financial performance” should be particularly relevant also for the cost of debt studies but, in this case, the literature adopts the “firm performance” definition, meaning for a broader classification capable of involving the financial performance as well, but not only. “Social performance,” which appears to be a red cluster peculiarity, is also linked to the cost of debt thematic group primarily through the “CSR” node. The presence of multiple performance keywords, as well as the significant differentiation among them in terms of relationship with other subjects, suggest that the performance measure is one of the most complex and articulated concepts to identify, reinforcing the same conclusions made with the blue cluster analysis. However, in this case social performance appears significantly more related to ESG than all the other types mentioned so far, even if, in general terms, it is not possible to define any preferred performance type for any thematic cluster nor for the overall ESG-cost of capital area of research.

### 4.4 | The green thematic group analysis

Lastly, the green cluster represents the third main area of research, consisting in a set of nodes among which the most relevant are identified as the three ESG pillars: “environment” (11 occurrences, 19 links), “social” (3 occurrences, 17 links), and “governance” (5 occurrences, 12 links). These nodes are mainly related to cost of debt studies, while being located at a greater distance from the red cluster. This suggests that if cost of equity contributions focus primarily on ESG as an overall topic (thicker red lines to the main node), the green cluster, as well as the blue cluster partially, are more suitable to accept contributions focused on its single dimensions (thicker green lines to E, S, and G dimensions). However, the green cluster, which is located on the left side of the keyword co-occurrence structure, do not have any of these pillars located in the center of its configuration, providing a signal for a potential subsidiary role of ESGs for this field of research, which involves these subjects mainly through an interdisciplinary connection with the “CSR” node from the blue thematic group.

The green cluster is mainly focused on portfolio construction criteria and asset allocation methods for ESG investing strategies. This evidence is supported by the nodes “ESG investing” (seven occurrences), “portfolio choice” (six occurrences) and “asset pricing” (six occurrences), being second only to “environment,” which is the most relevant keyword among this thematic group. Moreover, its structure can be summarized into two different subclasses: the ESG investing one, which involves portfolio strategies and asset pricing contributions and that is closely related to the “sustainable finance” red node, and the sustainable investing one, which involves primarily other specific subjects such as “green bonds,” “climate change,” and “covid-19.” Both subclasses are supported by “SRI” (Socially Responsible Investing) and “impact investing,” two subjects strongly related to each other. These evidences provide some hints about two main features that highlight the interdisciplinarity level of this research group: on one side, a strong interdisciplinary requirement is needed in order to reach the ESG-cost of capital relationship subject, while on the other hand, the presence of peculiar analysis that involve multiple specific scenarios, which are both used by other research group (“green bonds”) or acquired from them (“emerging markets”).

Finally, these results also provide useful information about possible gaps in the green cluster literature: first, impact investing and asset pricing, even if relevant in terms of network connections, they appear underexplored in terms of ESG topics due to their positioning. This evidence suggests the presence of another potential area that will need to be further investigated. Second, green bonds are mentioned, but their relationship with sustainable investing and portfolio allocation could be further analyzed to better understand their role from a cost of capital perspective. This is supported also by the fact that no cost of capital configuration, whether total, equity or debt, is directly involved in the green cluster through a strong link, even if some weak connections are still present, such as the one between “asset pricing” and “cost of equity,” or the one between “cost of capital” and “climate change” or “emerging markets.” Finally, “smart beta,” which is a relevant topic based on different indexation approaches for portfolio strategies (Arnott et al., 2005), is the most isolated green node, defining another potentially subject characterized by a rising interest but that do not appears as much developed in terms of ESG topic to date.

Overall, multiple studies investigate issues that are explained through various thematic areas, confirming the interdisciplinarity of this field of research. However, no keyword such as “value,” “firm value” or “valuation” nor other synonymous or combination of these terms was encountered in this phase. This evidence confirms the need for a content analysis aimed at understanding how many of these studies properly relate ESG and its cost of capital implications to the firm value. ### 5.1 | Bibliographic coupling of the final sample

This approach returned 50 papers with a minimum of at least 15 TGCs and a maximum of 447 TGCs, that we defined as our final sample. This dataset was used as an input for the documents bibliometric coupling analysis, which is a selection process based on the same citations shared by the articles within the final sample. Because this approach classifies the affiliation between different contributions based on the same references, differently from the keyword co-occurrence analysis, the clusters obtained through the bibliographic coupling consider both the evolution of the literature, as it is defined by the development of different theories and studies equally assumed by different contributions through time, and the impact on the scientific community, as it is based on a TGC criteria capable of identifying the most cited studies, being most suitable for our content analysis purposes (Moravcsik & Murugesan, 1975; Pillania, 2011). As for the keyword co-occurrence investigation (Figure 4), the output of this process also generated a total of three clusters (Figure 5).

Each node represents one of the 50 articles contained within the final sample: the bigger the node, the more global citations the document has. The lines connecting the nodes represent the so-called TLS which, in this case, signifies the number of references shared between two papers (wider lines are equal to higher numbers of shared citations), while the nodes positioning represents the similarities in terms of thematic area of research. The output of this process involves the following thematic clusters: Cluster 1 (Red) consisting of 23 articles, Cluster 2 (Green) consisting of 19 articles, and Cluster 3 (Blue) consisting of eight articles. ### 5.2 | Cluster 1 (red): ESG for portfolio choices and risk–return estimates

The red cluster (Table 4) has been labeled “ESG for portfolio choices and risk-return estimates.” This set of articles approaches our research subject in a similar manner, relying on a predominantly quantitative approach. Most of these outputs were elaborated with the application of Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952) through the Arbitrage Pricing Theory model (Ross, 1976) the CAPM (Lintner, 1965; Sharpe, 1964) and the multi factorial model (Fama & French, 1993). Measuring the expected return of an ESG portfolio to compare its result with a nonESG one has been one of the main research objectives we have encountered. That is the reason why many of these methods took into consideration the relationship of the cost of capital with ESG information, but not for direct implications on the firm's valuation process. Instead, their outputs can be classified in terms of portfolio expected return and risk diversification implications for the investor (Alessandrini & Jondeau, 2020; Ferrat et al., 2022; Fiskerstrand et al., 2020; Jacobsen et al., 2019; Pedersen et al., 2021; Sherwood & Pollard, 2018; Tang & Zhang, 2020; Zerbib, 2022; Zhai et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). We found a similar methodology for almost all these productions, which adopted quantitative methods typically based on regression models to evaluate how the ESG securities performance and its implications on the cost of capital can affect the investor's expected return and behavior. Some authors have included equity securities but also debt securities in their data, for example, by comparing the returns of green bonds with those of brown bonds (Pastor et al., 2022; Tang & Zhang, 2020). Among them, Tang and Zhang (2020) methodology aims at relating the green bond performance (debt side) with the price performance (equity side) in terms of benefits for the shareholders. Others have focused their research on a specific market other than the typical U.S. or E.U. ones (Fiskerstrand et al., 2020; Sherwood & Pollard, 2018; Zhai et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022) determining that taking into consideration different markets can drastically change the results, such as in the case of the Norwegian stock market that appears to have no connection with the ESG factor (Fiskerstrand et al., 2020). Another scope of research, we encountered was the elaboration of an adjusted CAPM-ESG model to prove whether and why the investors should or should not adapt their portfolio risk–return construction criteria to ESG factors (Pedersen et al., 2021; Zerbib, 2022).

This cluster also includes some peculiar contributions: among them, Becchetti et al. (2023) adopted a unique approach, involving an ESG-cost of capital proposition based on the company reputational information divulged by media, which was measured adopting the RedRisk Index, to assess its implications in terms of cost of equity. This study do not fit the purpose of our review as the main inflation factor capable of determining a potential firm value effect is related to the media releases that, even if based on ESG data, can be affected by multiple biases and factors that do not involve ESG information. Cohen (2023) studied the relationship between U.S. indexes excess returns and the individual ESG score pillars. This study identifies multiple E, S, and G effects that affect differently the stocks returns but, because these results are identified in terms of index performances, as they can be determined by a combination of positive and negative effects on the single companies, we could not assess this contribution in terms of “firm value.” The same was done with Dumrose et al. (2022), who investigated the EU taxonomy implications in terms of environmental scores concluding that the E rating is positively and significantly affected by firm level taxonomy performance.

We found that among these studies six of them were coherent with the purpose of our review: Avramov et al. (2022) concluded that in the event of uncertainty about the ESG rating the cost of capital will have negative effect on firm value. This study stated the crucial role that must be attributed to correct and clear information, as it can affect the company's risk profile. Another study that obtained a similar result is the one conducted by Gibson et al. (2021), who found empirical evidence about the ESG rating disagreement effect in terms of financial analysts' valuation of the firm. This study tested different ESG scores produced by different agencies, concluding that a more transparent ESG methodology disclosure is required to induce the market to properly valuate the so-called green stocks. Finally, Pastor et al. (2022) concluded that the ESG rating has a positive effect on the cost of capital, which translates in a higher firm valorization when the climate change concern arose. However, these authors stated that this phenomenon was due to an unexpected global interest in climate change, which strongly affected investor preferences and market trends. Consequently, as the market is becoming more aware, the unexpected concern for climate change will lose relevance over time, possibly leading to an uncertain effect in terms of risk in the long term.

These last three contributions have all concluded that ESG information, due to its uncertainty and unclear methodology, will potentially increase the firm's cost of capital and thus reduce its own value. On the other hand, Cornell (2021) determined that a green company with a high ESG rating will induce a lesser risk for the investor, reducing the firm's cost of capital and, consequently, increasing its value. This contribution, which is built on a theoretical analysis of the mainstream of the relationship between ESG and expected return for the investor, led to an interesting dichotomy: a higher ESG rating will reduce the company's cost of capital, which will ultimately lead to higher prices, but it also means that the expected return for the investor will be lower in the long run. Therefore, depending on his risk preference, if the investor wishes higher yields, he will have to invest in other securities thus bearing a higher risk. Consequently, the ESG implications on the cost of capital can lead to a positive outcome in terms of firm value but only if related to a short-term approach, while if investors prefer higher expected returns, they will need to invest in nonESG titles involving a long-term approach. These results are also supported by Ferrat et al. (2022), but in this case, because the scope of work was mainly related to expected returns, we did not classify this output in terms of firm value. All these studies develop the importance of the investing time-period orientation, highlighting completely different outcomes based on short, medium, and long-term investment orientations.

Girerd-Potin et al. (2014) stated that investing in a nonsocially responsible asset entails a premium risk that increases the cost of capital. A significant feature of this work is the ESG topic implicit in CSR performance: the authors used so-called Vigeo sub-ratings, which are different from the most recent ESG ratings. Moreover, they studied the effect of this risk-premia through the multifactorial model proposed by Fama and French (1993). This paper appears to be one of the first contribution that attempted to highlight the effect of socially responsible investments (SRI) on both small and large firms. The authors concluded that the effect on the cost of equity appears to be positive as socially responsible firms are rewarded with lower cost of capital, as investors look for additional risk premium to hold nonsocially responsible stocks.

Finally, Giese et al. (2019) concluded that higher ESG ratings imply a lower cost of capital meaning for a higher firm value thus confirming a positive effect. This contribution obtained these results through the implementation of Discount Cash Flow (i.e., DCF) and multiples methods, which are some of the most important valuation models to date. The authors, other than stating this value effect through the cost of equity, also concluded that ESG rating can positively impact the idiosyncratic risk, which usually translates into a higher level of profitability. We consider this study as one of the most robust contributions consistent with our research purposes as all these findings were obtained through proper firm valuation approaches, methods, and criteria.

In conclusion, the red cluster mainstream of thought implies that ESG matters in terms of risk–return for both the investor and financial markets, but it can also be assumed as a driver to value the risk profile of a company. The overall structure we derived from this thematic group is based on a predominant focus on the relationship between ESG rating, its disclosure, and the cost of equity, which can affect the risk–return profile for both portfolio choices and stocks valuations. Nevertheless, focusing our analysis on this relation in terms of firm value, although we did not find a cohesive stream of thought among this production, we did encounter a relevant and shared skepticism about nontransparent ESG rating methodologies and their disagreement implications, with a common opinion about the possibility to obtain different results based on different companies, both in terms of country and industry.

### 5.3 | Cluster 2 (green): ESG impact on financial markets and cost of debt

The green cluster (Table 5) has been labeled “ESG impact on financial markets and cost of debt.” Most of these articles studied the relationship between the ESG and debt dimension, but some of them also included the cost of equity effect (Raimo, De Nuccio, et al., 2021; Wong et al., 2021 Dahiya & Singh, 2020; Gjergji et al., 2021; Ng & Rezaee, 2015). In this case, all documents produced a quantitative analysis based on multiple theoretical principles, with the Agency Theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) being the most relevant one to contextualize the relationship between the debt dimension and agency costs. All these authors have investigated empirical data adopting multiple regression models, usually adopting the cost of capital configuration as the dependent variable. Furthermore, we encountered a relevant distinction between ESG disclosure and ESG performance, enhancing these two dimensions with different approaches even though they may have identical consequences in terms of firm value (Eliwa et al., 2021; Ng & Rezaee, 2015).

The studies we found unsuitable for our purpose are the following. Buallay (2019) studied ESG implications for banks and financial institutions in terms of accounting results, which are unrelated to our area of analysis: accounting performances are crucial information for the firm valuation process, but they cannot be assumed as drivers capable of defining the firm value per se (Damodaran, 2016). Amiraslani et al. (2023), who investigated the relationship between bond spreads and environmental and social performance, found that there is no connection between the two except in the case of the 2008 financial crisis. Bodhanwala & Bodhanwala (2019) made a study about ESG portfolios differentiating the results based on developed and undeveloped countries distinction, while Papathanasiou et al. (2022), who also adopted a portfolio-based methodology, involved ESG stocks in terms of volatility-spillover effect. Finally, Gao et al. (2022) made an interesting study about the mediating effect of media ESG based information in terms of cost of debt, which appears to be lower if associated with positive media disclosure. This study is similar the one yet encountered within the red group (Becchetti et al., 2023) and, for the same reasons, we did not consider it suitable for our classification process.

Apergis et al. (2022) studied the effects of ESG score on the bond yields, focusing their research on how ESG rating can affect them. The authors confirmed the following relationship between these two dimensions: the higher the ESG rating, the lower the yield spread of newly issued bonds, which translates into a lower cost of debt. The same results were achieved by the regression models proposed by Eliwa et al. (2021), Gerwanski (2020), Maaloul et al. (2023), and Raimo, Caragnano, et al. (2021), emphasizing the interdisciplinary role of ESG ratings for both cost of equity and cost of debt thematic areas of research. These studies highlighted the market's trend to prefer highly ESG-rated companies, as capital borrowers assume a lending position toward them for a lesser cost. This behavior is well explained by Gerwanski (2020), who concluded that ESG reporting can reduce asymmetries between the issuer and the investor through an easier assessment of the firm's risk, which allows for a lower cost of debt in terms of capital lending. Analogous conclusions were made by Maaloul et al. (2023), who implemented this effect by identifying the transition between ESG information and the cost of debt implications, recognizing that the company reputation has a mediating role that can enable this conversion. The result was that both ESG performance and disclosure increase corporate reputation, implying a lower cost of debt with a positive effect in terms of firm value. However, Eliwa et al. (2021) found that even if there is positive effect in terms of cost of capital, the market can fail to distinguish between ESG performance and ESG disclosure, with ESG performance being used as a main measure of the effective commitment to ESG strategies.

All authors from the green cluster analyzed so far have presented very similar conclusions, showing how studies on the cost of debt are much more similar and cohesive in terms of theory, methodology and results if compared to those grouped within the red cluster. In addition, among the green cluster there are also some authors who focused on the cost of equity (Azmi et al., 2021; Dahiya & Singh, 2020; Ng & Rezaee, 2015; Raimo, De Nuccio, et al., 2021). Azmi et al. (2021) examined the relationship between financial institutions in emerging economies and ESG activities. The authors' investigation led to an interesting outcome: the financial institutions' value is affected even by a small amount of ESG activity, with a leading position taken by environmentally sustainable ones. The effect on value is measured both through higher cash flows and a lower cost of equity, but the cost of debt implications remains negligible. Thus, although the data sample used for their analysis is different from the more common sample of listed industrial firms, these results are not aligned with the cost of debt positive effect encountered so far during the green cluster content analysis. Moreover, this study found that in case of higher ESG activities, diminishing returns to scale were recorded, concluding that the Stakeholder Theory could not properly explain the relationship between ESG and bank value. The latter evidence is significant as the authors stated that there is a certain threshold after which the stakeholder becomes indifferent to environmental, social, and corporate activities. These results are aligned with the ones observed by Buallay (2019), who related the bank performance in terms of accountability measures to ESG scores. However, because these studies are limited to the financial institutions firm type, we have classified these results as n.d. in terms of firm value effect, even though we believe that these kinds of investigations should be further researched for nonfinancial companies as well.

Dahiya and Singh (2020) dedicated their empirical research to a cluster of Indian listed firms, concluding that the relationship between ESG score and cost of equity has negative implications in terms of value. The authors explained their results by assessing investors' disinterest in CSR due to a lack of awareness. On the other hand, Raimo et al., (2021) concluded that higher ESG disclosure is usually correlated with a lower cost of equity, which enhances access to financial resources as well as reduces the cost of debt, linking this effect to both equity and debt profiles. Ng and Rezaee (2015) concluded that CSR performance disclosure has different effects on the cost of equity: more robust environmental and governance dimensions lower the cost of equity, while the social one has only a marginal relationship with it. Considering the ESG performance and its relationship with CSR disclosure, they also found that when ESG performance is highly rated, its effects on the cost of equity are stronger.

Another peculiar contribution is the one produced by Atif and Ali (2021), who analyzed the implications of ESG disclosure on corporate default risk, concluding that there is an optimistic relationship between ESG information and the survivability of the firm, which is obtained through the reduction of its default probability. This outcome is achieved through a lower cost of debt, confirming the positive behavior of ESG disclosure in terms of value for the firm. Moreover, the assessment of the cost of debt in terms of insolvency can overcome the limitation of the cost of debt as an isolated driver, giving this type of application a stronger outcome in terms of firm value. In any case, this study emphasized that the positive effect in terms of default risk reduction has been only found for mature firms.

Gigante & Manglaviti (2022) also assessed the ESG scores implications in terms of cost of debt, but through a peculiar approach: instead of using the cost of debt as a data input, they adopted the interest expense on debt to total debt ratio, which was then used as a dependent variable for a sharp RD model. Adopting this alternative methodology, they found divergent results based on an ESG grouping criteria: companies with higher average ESG scores appear to have a positive effect in terms of cost of debt given by an increase in terms of ESG performance, even though the authors did not find statistically significant results. On the other hand, for those companies with lower average ESG scores, better ESG performances are not sustained by positive effects in terms of cost of debt. This is one of the few contributions encountered so far that implies different results based on the ESG score positioning. Another study featured by mixed result is the one produced by Hamrouni et al. (2020): focusing on a France listed companies' sample, they found that ESG disclosure score has both negative and positive effect based on the different sub pillars disclosure levels: environmental disclosure has a positive effect on cost of debt, while social disclosure has the opposite relation (an increase in social disclosure increases the cost of debt) and the governance disclosure appears to be nonrelated to the cost of debt configuration. For these reasons, the overall ESG disclosure impact can differ based on the different disclosure combinations of its sub pillars.

Further studies have dedicated their attention to the relationship between the ESG implications on the cost of debt of Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) (Eichholtz et al., 2019; Feng & Wu, 2023) and the ESG implication on the total cost of capital, that is, WACC. (Gjergji et al., 2021; Wong et al., 2021; Gholami et al., 2022). Regarding the studies on REITs, because this is a very specific type of business, the results cannot be investigated in general terms being classified as n.d. in terms of firm value due to the same reasons involved for the bank firm-type studies. However, they concluded that environmental performance has an inversely proportional relationship with the cost of debt, which means that it has a positive relationship with the REITs' value, primarily due to a higher level of corporate transparency. Similar results, even if not related to REIT, are the ones obtained by Kong et al. (2023), who found a positive relationship between cost of debt and ESG ratings for the Chinese listed family business firms, meaning for a potential increase in this type of company's value. This is another significant approach that differentiates from the others because of the focus on another specific company type.

Other relevant contributions that adopted nonstandard listed EU or US companies as a sample are the ones made involving WACC as a cost of capital measure. Gjergji et al. (2021) analyzed the cost of capital implications on a cluster of small and medium-sized Italian listed firms concluding that environmental disclosure has a negative effect in terms of firm value. The authors stated that for non-large companies there is a higher cost–benefit trade-off in disclosing ESG information: since smaller companies are usually characterized by a lower diversification degree and a lesser contractual power with financial institutions, this relation is to be considered as a function of the firm dimension. Another study involving WACC together with cost of equity and cost of debt is the one produced by Wong et al. (2021). They applied their research to a cluster of Malaysian listed firms and, contrary to Gjergji et al. (2021), they revealed a positive relationship between ESG score and firm value, implying the role of cost of capital as a value driver. In particular, the authors concluded that the firm's cost of equity decreased upon receiving an ESG rating, investigating the effect of being ESG rated rather than adopting the ESG rating score measure as a metric. Therefore, the ESG rating was designed through a dummy variable indicating whether the company has an ESG score or not. Finally, Gholami et al. (2022) focused on an Australian listed firm sample investigating both the WACC and the idiosyncratic risk in relationship with the ESG scores. The authors concluded that higher ESG scores positively affect both the total cost of capital and the idiosyncratic risk reducing them essentially due to a higher level of transparency and trust, suggesting that firms can leverage this mechanism also to lower the cost associated with additional financing capital.

Concluding the green cluster content analysis, most recurrent evidence supported that both ESG disclosure and ESG score have a positive relationship with the cost of debt, meaning that the higher the ESG quality, the lower the cost of debt. However, a lower cost of debt cannot automatically be translated into a higher firm value, due to the possible compensatory effect of the cost of equity (Modigliani & Miller, 1963). This means that even if these results are indeed positive in terms of firm value because of the lower interest rate and lower debt risk profile, if a lower cost of debt is matched by a much higher cost of equity, the final effect on the corporate valuation process could also be negative. Therefore, even if the results of the green cluster are homogeneous and relevant for our research scope, the exclusive interaction with the cost of debt cannot fully express the final effects on firm value, even if the ESG implications on the cost of capital have been intensively investigated with shared results within the cluster. For these reasons, we found the WACC studies more reliable for our analysis, as they also involve both the cost of debt with the cost of equity interaction and the capital structure implications in terms of total cost of capital.

### 5.4 | Cluster 3 (blue)—Particular cases of ESG implications on the cost of capital

The blue cluster (Table 6) has been labeled “Particular cases of ESG implications on the cost of capital.” These articles address the researched relationship in specific scenarios that may be defined by peculiar subjects that do not behave like a traditional firm (Chen et al., 2023; Czerwińska & Kazmierkiewicz, 2015; Ur Rehman et al., 2016; Rao et al., 2023) rather than specific markets (Chen et al., 2023; Czerwińska & Kazmierkiewicz, 2015; Fandella et al., 2023; Rao et al., 2023; Ur Rehman et al., 2016).

Agnese & Giacomini (2023) investigated the ESG score implications in terms of fixed-rate bond issued by a sample of EU banks. Even if we do not valuate this study as not align with the purposes of our analysis, the authors findings demonstrate that lower cost of issuing bonds is achievable through higher ESG scores, with the governance score being the most significant one. Fandella et al. (2023) developed a research based on cost of equity, cost of debt and WACC relationship with both the ESG combined ranking and ESG scores investigating BRICS countries stocks. Their results provide a positive relationship between ESG and the different cost of capital configuration, meaning that the overall effect on the firm value can be judged as positive. However, if China is excluded from the sample, this result do not endure as the effect of ESG combined ranking seems to increase the cost of debt and the WACC on the remaining stocks, suggesting for a negative effect in terms of firm value although the effect on cost of equity is still decremental.

Another study is the one proposed by Czerwińska and Kazmierkiewicz (2015). The latter contribution studied the volatility effect of ESG disclosure analyzing the Polish market, which revealed that companies with higher ESG ratings had lower return uncertainty and forecast error. Nevertheless, we found no significant evidence of a clear effect in terms of firm value. Another specific sectorial study is the one proposed by Rao et al. (2023), who investigated the relationship between ESG practices and Return of Equity (ROE) among a sample of Indian firms. Also in this case, as the accountability ratio research by the authors is a performance metric not relatable to the firm cost of capital, we did not consider this contribution align with our review scope of work.

Closing this type of investigations, other authors (Geczy et al., 2021; Jin, 2018) involved portfolio analysis in their research. Both these contributions aimed at explaining the differences between responsible and traditional investments operated by mutual funds. The methodology used by Jin (2018) adopted the five-factorial model (Fama & French, 2015) resulting that equity funds can benefit from including corporate social performance as a criterion for their investments and portfolio diversification process. This type of study is very similar to Ur Rehman et al. (2016), who also relied on Portfolio Theory for a comparison between ESG indexes and conventional composite Asian indexes. The conclusion was that, apart from the market volatility of ESG indexes being higher than that of conventional indexes, the ESG equity indexes' performance of multiple Asian countries is close to the conventional ones. On the other hand, Geczy et al. (2021) conclusions provide an important result in terms of costs associated with capital allocations based on SRI, as the adoption of mono-factorial models, such as the CAPM, can lead to significant different results if compared to the adoption of multi-factorial models such as the one proposed by Fama and French (1993).

Ultimately, Chen et al. (2023) investigated the Chinese A-Share companies' cost of equity reaction to ESG performance, resulting in a positive effect in terms of firm value. These authors also highlighted a further relevant phenomenon: market heterogeneity subjected to ESG performance has a diversification effect in terms of market risk, which translates into a further reduction of the equity risk premium for the individual firm. The authors, instead of only considering the implications of the firm's cost of equity due to ESG performance, they also looked for other results in terms of relationships between different risk profiles.

The blue cluster is characterized by studies that focus on very peculiar samples, but the methodology and considerations the authors stated from their results opened to potential new approaches that can be applied to a larger and most common population of firms in terms of follow-up studies. This thematic group includes several contributions that adopt significant different approaches both in terms of data input and methods, highlighting the will of the literature to explore differently the researched relationship. However, due to the limited number of contributions allocated within this cluster in comparison with the red and green ones, this also suggest a novelty field of research that still need to be furthered developed.

### 5.5 | Overlay visualization and trend progression

The content analysis showed for all the clusters a general interest in the methodology used for the ESG rating construction. A relevant conclusion we have encountered several times is that the heterogeneity among different ESG scores and the lack of clarity beneath their determination criteria interfere with the possibility to properly analyze the achieved results. Therefore, we found a tendency to focus on these construction criteria primarily through the E, S, and G pillars individual analysis, which appears coherent with the necessity to recognize the implications of different methodologies used for the total ESG rating quantification. Furthermore, the different results obtained from similar studies suggests that more transparency is needed for ESG rating as the mere existence of these controversies can affect the value of the firm (Billio et al., 2021; Gibson et al., 2021). To confirm these results, we further investigate the evolution of the literature through an overlay visualization (Figure 6). This method allows to show the change in interest over time of the literature reviewed so far, confirming that the scientific production about the cost of capital implications on ESG is a recent topic as it appears to be significant only since the last 4 years. Furthermore, it shifted from an initial focus on CSR, asset pricing and risk assessment studies, to information asymmetry issues related to the ESG topics, while maintaining a strong interest in the research areas of cost of capital, ESG disclosure, and ESG ratings. Moreover, it is also possible to highlight other novelty topics and theories involved into the most recent contributions, such as REM and Tobin's Q, which are peculiar of the first half of 2024.

## 6 | CONCLUSIONS

This hybrid review was developed from a raw sample of 314 papers aimed at investigating the relationship between ESG and firm value through the cost of capital implications. We encountered a major gap in the literature, as our investigation did not find significant and cohesive evidence to define whether ESG has an established relationship with firm value that can be explained through its cost of capital profile. Therefore, our research contributed to raising awareness of this issue for follow-up studies on the subject.

We found a recent, heterogeneous and fast-growing state of the literature, both in terms of authors and journals production. Besides, the keyword co-occurrence analysis allowed us to classify this area of research into three main thematic groups focusing, respectively, on the implications of the cost of equity (red cluster), the cost of debt (blue cluster), and portfolio strategies (green cluster) to explain the relationship between ESG and cost of capital. Moreover, all but one of these studies relied on quantitative methods with different and various regression models approaches. Regarding the methodologies, we found the following issues: first, some regression models were built with independent variables eventually differing from the independence requirement, as no multicollinearity test was made even if some of these variables are mathematically and logically built from each other. Moreover, these models' results can be affected by an overlap effect, which can also affect the output interpretation. Second, according to the different theories assumed, the ESG topic was measured through various approaches: we found this process to be consistent with the theories beneath the type of analysis, but at the cost of a shortcoming in terms of comparability between these results. Notably, the breadth of the main theories involved in this field of research was significant: although we aimed to classify each document according to the most relevant ones, most of these contributions are still only explained by a mix of them, involving the absence of a specific and dominant theoretical theory specifically designed to explain the relationship between ESGs and risk–return firm profiles. We argue that this is evidence justifies the lack of a dominant theoretical proposition, being consistent with our theoretical framework and bibliometric analysis results. This is also amplified by the evidence that some of these studies were not aimed at determining the relationship between ESG and firm value through the cost of capital: even though the researched terms were still involved across all the documents, in these cases the cost of capital did not serve the purpose of explaining the ESG—firm value relationship. This specific scenario was identified for 29 papers out of a total of 50 (58.0%), whereas this researched relationship has been classified as n.d. This evidence was achievable because of to the methodological choice to pursue the ESG relationship with firm value only through the research of ESG and cost of capital contents, as it allowed us to identify all these different declinations of the researched factors in addition to firm value.

Through our investigation, we found that most of the studies that focused on the implications of ESG on the cost of equity were aimed at explaining expected returns for the investors, not including the firm's value proposition in their scope of work. This is the case for 13 out of 19 articles (68.4%) identified within the red cluster. On the other hand, this was not the case for most of the analyses aimed at evaluating the cost of debt implications, since among the green cluster we found only eight articles out of a total of 23 (34.8%) not suitable of our scope of work. However, as stated for the green cluster content analysis, even if lower cost of debt can potentially lead to a lower cost of capital and, therefore, higher firm value, this evidence also needs to be compared with the cost of equity trend to exclude an inversely proportional relationship between the two.

Based on these findings, we can conclude that the current state of the literature is not sufficiently developed to answer our research question to date as all these studies contribute to identify the main limitations of the current exploration of the subject in various ways. In particular, the distinction between different ESG topics has relevant consequences both on theoretical and empirical profiles. The most relevant one we encountered within our investigation is the one related to ESG ratings as most of the studies, even within different clusters, concluded that the different methodologies underlying each of these scores not only prevent the empirical study from achieving effective results, but are also a cause of the increased cost of capital according to their level of disagreement. Moreover, at a micro level, the single E, S, and G scores, when studied individually, have very different impacts on different types of firms, whose effects are also distorted by the different ESG scores applied to the same firm. This outcome emerged as in some cases only specific ESG pillars have positive effects, while in others the results were reversed. Lastly, as the ESG score is undeniably bound to CSR disclosure, we found studies that did not adopt its standard configuration, opting for other CSR measurements and concluding that these results also differed in terms of ESG performance or disclosure. For all these reasons, confirming the overlay visualization outcome, our results suggested that the literature is likely to shift its approach based on ESG rating from an ex-post to an ex-ante investigation, trying to define how this ESG measure is built and how this criterion can potentially affect the firm value. These results are still open for multiple follow-up proposals, such as investigating the ESG topic and firm value relationship through different drivers or the ESG score heterogeneity implications on the firm risk–return profile.

To our knowledge, there is no other review about the cost of capital implications of the ESG topic in terms of firm value. There could encounter a few about the generic relationship between ESG specific topics and firm value but without any firm valorization driver like the one we adopted. Also, we could not find any review about the researched subject that aims to develop an interdisciplinary review as the ESG factor is usually researched with specific thematic constraints. Moreover, considering other review-type contributions about ESG involving other subjects such as financial performance (Rahi et al., 2024), investors' expectations (Kraussl et al., 2024), and a sustainability reporting (Bosi et al., 2022), we contributed to enrich the ESG area of research involving the firm risk–return debate, as other recent literature review contributions highlighted the need for this type of analysis (Jain & Tripathi, 2023).

In terms of limitations, our work is restricted to a firmly keyword research criteria, including only those contributions that adopt both ESG and cost of capital configurations in their research. Moreover, these documents are limited to ABS ranked journals from the Scopus database, involving a TGC screening criteria to identify the most relevant contributions for the content analysis. In terms of tools, we used the Bibliometrix R package and VOS Viewer software, even though other alternative bibliometric instruments are also eligible for this type of research.
