# Talk the talk and walk the walk: how social trust affects ESG disclosure’s financing impact

**Nanzhi Xue$^a$ and Qi Zhang$^b$**

$^a$Glorious Sun School of Business and Management, Donghua University, Shanghai, China; $^b$School of Management, Shandong University, Jinan, Shandong, China

### ABSTRACT
This study examines the role of social trust in shaping the financing effect of ESG disclosure. Empirical results reveal that elevated trust levels intensify the inverse association between firms’ ESG disclosure and debt financing costs. This enhancement stems from increasing the informational value of ESG disclosure and improving ESG practices. Moreover, the influence of social trust is more salient among enterprises located in less market-oriented regions, operating within environmentally sensitive sectors, or characterized by lower analyst coverage and weaker reputation. Overall, these findings demonstrate that social trust is a prerequisite for ESG disclosure to generate expected economic impacts.

## 1. Introduction

Heightened attention to ESG (environmental, social, and governance) considerations has spurred a notable expansion in corporate sustainability reporting globally, as capital market participants increasingly appreciate its relevance to firm valuation. Whether firms derive financing benefits from ESG disclosure remains a critical issue attracting substantial academic attention. While the extant literature posits that enhanced corporate financial information disclosure mitigates firms’ financing costs by reducing information asymmetry with capital providers (Sengupta, 1998; Lambert, Leuz, and Verrecchia 2007), the empirical evidence regarding the financial consequences of ESG-related transparency remains inconclusive. For example, (Dhaliwal et al. 2011; Plumlee et al. 2015; and Fonseka, Rajapakse, and Richardson 2019) document a negative relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of capital. In contrast, Richardson and Welker (2001) report a positive association, whereas Clarkson, Fang and Richardson (2013) observe no statistically significant relationship.

These mixed results raise the question of why ESG disclosure fails to mitigate firms’ capital costs in certain instances. We posit that a crucial determinant, the institutional environment, may be overlooked despite its pivotal role in influencing ESG disclosure (Jin, Shi, and Zhang 2019). In emerging markets, where regulations on ESG disclosure remain less developed than those in developed markets, firms may have room to exaggerate or ‘greenwash’ their ESG performance by manipulating the ESG disclosure (Bothello et al. 2023). In light of this, investors may hesitate to rely on ESG information for decision-making purposes, thereby casting doubt upon the tangible benefits of ESG disclosure.

Nevertheless, moral ethics and traditional culture factors play dominant roles in emerging markets (Gong, Xu, and Gong 2018). These factors could serve to mitigate the impact of underdeveloped formal institutions governing ESG disclosure. This paper examines social trust as a form of informal institution that shapes individuals’ mutual behavioral expectations (Durlauf and Fafchamps 2005; Fukuyama 1995). In high-trust regions, firms are more likely to engage in substantive ESG reporting and enhance ESG performance to foster an image of integrity and social responsibility. In other words, firms would not only ‘talk the talk’ but also ‘walk the walk’ regarding ESG disclosure and practice. As a result, investors tend to view ESG disclosure as more credible and are more willing to integrate such information into valuation and capital allocation decisions, thereby reducing the expected rate of return. In contrast, in situations where social trust is absent, leading to a failure in imposing informal constraints on managerial behavior, investors may be reluctant to trust and rely on ESG information disseminated by firms, rendering ESG disclosure seemingly ineffectual in such contexts.

Therefore, the actual benefits of ESG disclosure are likely contingent upon the level of social trust, an idea that remains largely unexplored within the extant literature. We aim to address this research gap by exploring how social trust impacts the link between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt financing, referred to as the financing effect of ESG disclosure, thus revealing the crucial prerequisites for realizing ESG disclosure benefits. We do not contend that ESG disclosure exerts a direct and universal effect on firms’ capital costs; rather, our findings emphasize the role of social trust in shaping this association. In doing so, our research delves into the necessary conditions for non-financial information disclosure to yield anticipated economic effects.

Using a sample of Chinese listed firms between 2012 and 2020, we investigate whether and how social trust shapes the extent to which ESG disclosure affects firms’ cost of debt. We focus on the Chinese setting is motivated by several considerations. First, China’s vast territory exhibits substantial regional variations in social trust, offering a valuable setting to examine how social trust influences corporate behavior. Second, China’s ESG framework remains underdeveloped, with disclosure regulations lagging behind those in developed markets. Consequently, informal institutions like social trust serve as key enablers of ESG behavior in the Chinese context. Third, given China’s status as a leading emerging market, examining its ESG practices offers valuable insights with potential implications for comparable economies across the developing world.

Given this context, we specifically focus on the cost of debt rather than the cost of equity. This choice is motivated by the distinct characteristics of China’s credit market and the nature of debt financing. First, debt remains the dominant source of external financing for Chinese firms (Ding, Xiong, and Zhang 2022; You et al. 2025). Consequently, the cost of debt has become a decisive factor for firms’ survival and development. Second, the debt market is more sensitive to ESG information asymmetry. Creditors primarily focus on repayment capacity and default risk, and thus they are more responsive to signals that can reduce information asymmetry and enhance firms’ long-term sustainability, such as ESG disclosure (Goss and Roberts 2011). Third, creditors, notably banks, are typically in closer geographical proximity to firms (Chen and Mao 2025; DeYoung, Glennon, and Nigro 2008; Petersen and Rajan 1994). This proximity makes it especially relevant to investigate how local social trust conditions the relationship between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt in the Chinese context. Finally, from a methodological perspective, data on debt costs are relatively transparent and readily available in the Chinese capital market. By contrast, the cost of equity often requires model-based estimation, which relies heavily on underlying assumptions. As such, focusing on debt costs allows for a more robust empirical identification. Accordingly, examining how social trust conditions the relationship between ESG disclosure and firms’ cost of debt is particularly pertinent in China’s institutional setting.

We find that the inverse association between ESG disclosure and the cost of debt is significantly amplified in regions characterized by higher levels of social trust. Specifically, a one-standard-deviation increase in social trust strengthens the negative effect of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt by about 0.171 percentage points, representing approximately 8.1% of the average cost of debt in the sample. To establish causality, we exploit the staggered implementation of China’s social credit system as a quasi-natural experiment. A difference-in-differences (DID) approach reveals that, after policy implementation, firms headquartered in cities designated as national model cities experience a markedly stronger reduction in debt financing costs associated with ESG disclosure. To mitigate concerns over potential self-selection due to non-random firm location, we employ propensity score matching (PSM) method. Our results remain consistent across a series of robustness checks, including alternative variable specifications, the exclusion of third-party certification influences, and various fixed effects models.

Next, we explore the underlying mechanisms. We conjecture that social trust could constrain firms’ false disclosure, ensuring that their ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’. We also posit that social trust could encourage firms to engage in ESG practices to address ESG concerns, ensuring that they ‘act with perseverance’. These two mechanisms alleviate creditors’ concerns about firms’ ESG information quality and stabilize creditors’ expectations of firms’ ESG performance, thus reducing the debt financing costs. We assess ESG disclosure quality through proxies for greenwashing and information transparency. Firms located in high-trust regions exhibit lower tendencies toward greenwashing, and their ESG disclosure is more substantive and decision-relevant. To capture firms’ underlying ESG practices, we rely on ESG performance ratings and find a stronger alignment between reported ESG disclosure and actual ESG outcomes in these regions. These results highlight the role of social trust in enhancing both the credibility and implementation of ESG initiatives.

Finally, we examine whether the formal institutional environment, industry, and firm characteristics modulate our baseline results. First, we employ the provincial marketization index as a proxy for institutional maturity and find that the effect of social trust is more salient in regions with weaker formal institutions, suggesting that informal mechanisms play a compensatory role in such settings. Second, when firms are segmented based on industry pollution levels, the trust effect proves more significant among those operating in environmentally sensitive sectors. Third, analysis based on analyst coverage reveals that firms with limited external monitoring exhibit a stronger reliance on social trust to enhance ESG credibility. Lastly, we examine the role of firm reputation and provide evidence that reputation and social trust function as substitutes rather than complements in amplifying the financing benefits of ESG disclosure.

This research advances the literature through several distinct contributions. First, this study emphasizes the crucial role of social trust in shaping the economic effects of ESG disclosure. Prior research has extensively examined the financing effects of CSR and ESG disclosure, reporting mixed evidence of negative (e.g. Dhaliwal et al. 2011; Dunne and McBrayer 2019; Michaels and Grüning 2017; Plumlee et al. 2015), positive (e.g. Amarna et al. 2024; Moussa and Elmarzouky 2024; Richardson and Welker 2001), and null effects (e.g. Clarkson et al. 2013; Khanchel and Lassoued 2022). However, the role of institutional and social factors, especially in emerging markets, has been largely neglected. By demonstrating that social trust amplifies the financing benefits of ESG disclosure in China’s distinctive institutional context, this study provides novel insights into how societal-level factors condition the effectiveness of ESG disclosure.

Second, this research advances the literature on social trust and corporate finance. Prior work has predominantly examined social trust’s role in areas such as information disclosure (Guan et al. 2020; Pevzner, Xie, and Xin 2015), corporate investment (Ang, Cheng, and Wu 2015), external financing (Amiraslani et al. 2023), trade credit (Wu, Firth, and Rui 2014), corporate governance (Goodell et al. 2023), and ESG performance (Amiraslani et al. 2023; Zhu and Wang 2024).$^1$ Nonetheless, empirical evidence remains scarce regarding how social trust affects the benefits derived from firms’ ESG disclosure. Our study fills this gap by theoretically framing and empirically testing the influence of social trust on the financing advantages of ESG disclosure, thereby broadening the scope of research on social trust and corporate finance.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature and develops the hypotheses. Section 3 describes the sample, variables, and research design. Section 4 presents the empirical findings, followed by an examination of underlying mechanisms in section 5. Section 6 offers cross-sectional analyses, and Section 7 concludes with implications for firms and policymakers.

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis development

### 2.1. Literature review
Consistent with the aims of this research, our literature review centers on the financing implications of corporate ESG disclosure and the economic role of social trust.

The financing effects of CSR/ESG disclosure have been widely examined, yet the findings remain mixed. Several studies document a negative relationship, showing that CSR/ESG disclosure reduces the cost of capital by mitigating information asymmetry and estimation risk (Dhaliwal et al. 2011; Dunne and McBrayer 2019; Michaels and Grüning 2017; Plumlee et al. 2015). Bhuiyan and Nguyen (2020) report that creditors perceive CSR disclosure as a mechanism to alleviate potential business risk, leading to lower expected returns. Raimo et al. (2021) further demonstrate that better ESG disclosure is associated with lower debt financing costs across global listed firms. Consistent evidence is also found in regional contexts, including European firms (Eliwa, Aboud, and Saleh 2021; Hamrouni, Uyar, and Boussaada 2020), Chinese firms (Fonseka, Rajapakse, and Richardson 2019; Gong, Xu, and Gong 2018; Xu, Xu, and Yu 2021; Yang, Wen, and Li 2024; You et al. 2025), and Chilean firms (Lavin and Montecinos-Pearce 2022). In contrast, other research points to a positive relationship. Richardson and Welker (2001) find that certain CSR reporting dimensions may increase financing costs for Canadian firms. Moussa and Elmarzouky (2024) argue that ESG disclosure can increase complexity and uncertainty, thereby raising the cost of capital, particularly for firms with high ESG risks. Similarly, Amarna et al. (2024) show that investors may interpret ESG disclosure unfavorably, leading to higher cost of equity. García-Sánchez et al. (2021) provide evidence that strategic CSR disclosure may exacerbate information asymmetry, thereby increasing the cost of capital, while Kim, Li, and Li (2014) indicate that opportunistic CSR activities can mask bad news, raising crash risk and, in turn, the required risk premium. Finally, a third stream of literature finds no significant relationship between CSR/ESG disclosure and the cost of capital. For instance, Clarkson et al. (2013) report no statistical association, while Khanchel and Lassoued (2022) observe that environmental disclosure initially reduces capital costs but later shows no effect. More recent work highlights the context-dependence of these effects, suggesting that institutional, firm-level, or industry-level conditions shape whether disclosure lowers or raises financing costs (H. B. Christensen, Hail, and Leuz 2021; Truong et al. 2024).

The second line of literature examines how social trust, as an essential element of informal institutions, influences economic outcomes and firm behavior. From a macroeconomic perspective, social trust is regarded as a crucial factor shaping national economic development (Cui 2017; Knack and Keefer 1997). From a microeconomic perspective, how social trust influences corporate investment decisions (Ang, Cheng, and Wu 2015), financing decisions (Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales 2004), trade credit (Wu, Firth, and Rui 2014), cash holding (Dudley and Zhang 2016), corporate governance (Goodell et al. 2023), and ESG performance (Zhu and Wang 2024) has been examined in existing literature. Regarding information disclosure, Pevzner, Xie, and Xin (2015) find investors place greater credibility on firms’ earnings announcements in high-trust regions. Similarly, Guan et al. (2020) show that managers are more willing to issue earnings forecasts in high-trust regions, as investors regard voluntary disclosures as dependable indicators of future profitability. Berglund and Kang (2013) focus on auditors’ perceived credibility of financial reports and find that social trust reduces auditors’ perception of reporting risk. Jha (2019) finds that financial reports are higher quality when firms are headquartered in high-trust regions, as social trust involves more altruistic norms and denser networks that induce managers to fulfill their obligations.

This literature review suggests that inconsistent findings on CSR/ESG disclosure’s financing effects may stem from overlooking institutional environments. Because informal institutions often play pivotal roles in emerging markets, incorporating these institutions into research on ESG disclosure is imperative (Shahab and Ye 2018; Yin and Zhang 2012). Consequently, this research centers on social trust, an essential informal institutional factor, and aims to elucidate its influence on the financing effect of ESG disclosure.

### 2.2. Hypothesis development
According to disclosure theory, ESG disclosure provides relevant environmental, social, and governance information for investors to mitigate information asymmetry and estimation risk, ultimately leading to lower required returns (Dhaliwal et al. 2011; Raimo et al. 2021). However, due to ESG information’s non-comparability and potential credibility issues, as well as firms’ opportunistic behaviors (Ingram and Frazier 1980), investors may be reluctant to trust and rely on ESG information in decision-making. Currently, formal regulations regarding ESG disclosure are lacking in most emerging markets, leaving room for firms to perform opportunistic behavior in ESG disclosure, such as exaggerating ESG performance or even disseminating false information (Gong, Xu, and Gong 2018). Consequently, investors may perceive ESG information as greenwashed or lacking credibility. In this situation, social trust emerges as a critical informal institution that influences both firms’ ESG disclosure practices and investors’ perceptions thereof.

Drawing on institutional theory, firms’ disclosure behavior is shaped by the regulatory frameworks and normative pressures of their external environment. Companies disclose ESG information to maximize benefits within their specific context (De Villiers and Alexander 2014). In high-trust regions, firms’ information disclosures are perceived by investors as more credible (Jha 2019; Pevzner, Xie, and Xin 2015). Therefore, firms in these regions prefer to make more ESG disclosure to confer competitive advantages and differentiate themselves from competitors. Furthermore, dishonest firms face greater penalties in high-trust regions. Even in emerging markets with undeveloped formal institutions, social trust serves as a deterrent to managerial misconduct, including practices like greenwashing and the spread of misleading information (Dong et al. 2018). Thus, we posit that social trust serves as a deterrent against firms engaging in false disclosure, ensuring that their ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’.

Moreover, social trust can incentivize firms who engage in ESG disclosure to enhance their ESG practices and ensure alignment and consistency between their disclosures and actual practices. In high-trust regions, firms’ ESG disclosure is deemed more trustworthy, as previously discussed. Firms are thus inclined to disclose objective information that encompasses both strengths and concerns in their ESG practices. As social trust encourages consistent socially responsible behaviors, firms with ESG disclosure are motivated to engage in ESG practices to address concerns and maintain strengths, thereby improving their overall ESG performance. Consequently, social trust ensures that firms engaging in ESG disclosure ‘act with perseverance’.

In summary, social trust serves as a deterrent against firms engaging in false disclosure, ensuring that their ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’. Furthermore, social trust encourages firms with ESG disclosure to engage in ESG practices to address concerns and maintain strengths in ESG practice, ensuring that they ‘act with perseverance’. Therefore, social trust alleviates creditors’ concerns about firms’ ESG information quality and stabilizes creditors’ expectations of firms’ ESG performance, which reduces information asymmetry and estimation risk for investors. This ultimately reduces the investors’ required rate of return. This theoretical framework leads to our first hypothesis:

**H1: The financing effect of ESG disclosure increases with higher regional social trust levels.**

As a key component of informal institutions, social trust complements the functioning of formal mechanisms. In regions characterized by improved legal frameworks and efficient market structures, investors expect firms to follow legal norms and market rules. Consequently, the marginal contribution of social trust diminishes in such institutional environments. Conversely, in settings where institutional infrastructure is weaker, trust among economic agents becomes more critical in mitigating information asymmetry and governance risks (X. Chen and Wu 2023). Building on this analysis, we propose the second hypothesis:

**H2: The influence of social trust on the financing effect of ESG disclosure is stronger for firms operating in regions with weak institutional environment.**

There is less potential for firms to make unsubstantiated or misleading claims about environmental commitment in nonpolluting industries, increasing the likelihood that investors will trust the ESG information disclosed by nonpolluting firms in these industries. Consequently, the influence of social trust is less significant in these industries. In contrast, studies have shown that firms with greater pollution propensity tend to provide broader environmental disclosures (Bewley and Li 2000). Moreover, heavily polluting firms typically face greater pressure for environmental information disclosure due to regulatory obligations.$^2$ Under such circumstances, investors may question the authenticity of the disclosures, often viewing them as instances of greenwashing. However, in high-trust regions, false ESG disclosure could be costly even for polluting firms, leading these firms to disclose more truthful information. Accordingly, disclosures are more likely to be regarded as credible by investors. Based on this analysis, we propose the third hypothesis:

**H3: The influence of social trust on the financing effect of ESG disclosure is stronger for polluting firms.**

The extant literature establishes analyst coverage as both an information intermediary and corporate monitor (Ellul and Panayides 2018), with demonstrated effects on firms’ investments and financing decisions (Chang, Dasgupta, and Hilary 2006). Therefore, for firms with intensive analyst attention, their information disclosures and ESG practices are monitored by analysts (Benlemlih et al. 2023; Zhang and Wu 2023). Accordingly, the influence of social trust on these firms may be diminished, leading to our fourth hypothesis:

**H4: The influence of social trust on the financing effect of ESG disclosure is stronger for firms with weak analyst coverage.**

Over the past decade, firms have increasingly pursued ESG initiatives to enhance their reputational standing. A well-established ESG image, in turn, serves as a credible signal of a firm’s commitment to sustainability (Lee, Raschke, and Krishen 2022). Thus, firms with strong reputations tend to have their ESG disclosure perceived as more credible by investors; therefore, the significance of social trust might decrease. In contrast, if a firm has a poor reputation, investors may be reluctant to trust their ESG disclosure. However, investors perceive the information disclosed by firms in high-trust regions as containing more useful content that could support their decision-making. Therefore, we propose our fifth hypothesis:

**H5: The influence of social trust on the financing effect of ESG disclosure is stronger for firms with poor reputations.**

## 3. Research design

### 3.1. Sample and data
We construct our initial sample using data from Chinese listed companies covering the years 2012 to 2020. We exclude financial firms and remove observations with missing data, yielding a final sample of 1255 firms and 7687 firm-year observations. The data for constructing the social trust indicator is collected from the World Values Survey database (WVS) and the ESG disclosure data from Bloomberg.$^3$ Corporate information, financial information, and governance data are collected from the Chinese Research Data Services Platform (CNRDS), and the China Stock Market and Accounting Research database (CSMAR).

### 3.2. Measures of variables

#### 3.2.1. Social trust
Drawing on Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales (2003), we construct a province-level social trust indicator based on data from the WVS, which consists of nationally representative surveys on the values of residents in almost 100 countries. Within our sample period, these surveys in China were conducted in 2012 and 2018. We construct the social trust indicator using the question about respondents’ trust in other people. Specifically, the question is ‘Generally speaking, would you say that most people can be trusted, or that you need to be very careful in dealing with people?’. The options include the following: (1) most people can be trusted; (2) need to be very careful; (3) don’t know. First, we calculate the proportion of respondents in province $p$ who choose ‘most people can be trusted’, and obtain the social trust indicator of province $p$ in 2012 and 2018. Second, we match the social trust indicators of the corresponding provinces according to the years of the survey: the social trust indicators from 2012 to 2014 are matched to the survey data of 2012; and the social trust indicators from 2015 to 2020 are matched to the survey data of 2018.$^4$ Finally, each firm is matched with the social trust indicator corresponding to its headquarter province.

#### 3.2.2. ESG disclosure
Drawing on Christensen, Serafeim, and Sikochi (2022), we use Bloomberg’s ESG disclosure score to quantify each firm’s ESG disclosure level. This score is specifically designed to quantify the quantity and transparency of a firm’s ESG reporting, rather than its actual ESG performance. The score’s construction ensures objectivity, as it is based on publicly available information that Bloomberg analysts systematically collect from corporate sustainability and annual reports as well as corporate websites. The scale spans from 0.1 to 100, with higher values denoting more comprehensive transparency based on Bloomberg’s data coverage. To illustrate its comprehensive nature, the score is structured under the three main pillars of Environmental (E), Social (S), and Governance (G), which are further disaggregated into 19 specific issues (9 for E, 7 for S, and 3 for G) and 61 sub-issues (21 for E, 16 for S, and 24 for G).$^5$ A key strength that enhances the data’s reliability is its industry-aware evaluation framework (D. M. Christensen, Serafeim, and Sikochi 2022). Unlike a one-size-fits-all approach, Bloomberg assesses firms only on the ESG metrics considered material to their specific industry, thus ensuring the scores are comparable within a sector. The growing use of this score in academic research (e.g. Baldini et al. 2018; D. M. Christensen, Serafeim, and Sikochi 2022; Li et al. 2018) confirms its credibility. To mitigate potential issues arising from the skewed distribution of the score, we use the natural logarithm of Bloomberg’s overall ESG score (*ESG Disclosure*) to measure ESG disclosure level.

#### 3.2.3. Cost of capital
Since Chinese financial reports do not disclose interest expenses by debt type during most of sample period, we follow Zou and Adams (2008) and Minnis (2011) and define the cost of capital (*COC*) as the net financing expenses divided by total debt for firm $i$ in year $t$, multiplied by 100. Net financing expenses include interest expenses, capitalized interest, and other financing-related costs.

### 3.3. Empirical model
We investigate whether social trust enhances the financing effect associated with ESG disclosure through the following specification:

$$COC_{i,t} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 Social Trust_{i,t-1} \times ESG Disclosure_{i,t-1} + \alpha_2 Social Trust_{i,t-1} + \alpha_3 ESG Disclosure_{i,t-1} + \sum \beta Controls_{i,t-1} + Industry FE + Year FE + \epsilon_i \quad (1)$$

where *COC* represents firm-level debt financing costs. The independent variables include the regional social trust measured at firms’ headquarters location (*Social Trust*), Bloomberg’s overall ESG disclosure score (*ESG Disclosure*), and their interaction. All independent variables are lagged by 1 year. A significant negative coefficient on the interaction term would support our hypothesis that regional social trust amplifies ESG disclosure’s financing effect. Our regression incorporates multiple dimensions of controls (*Controls*): firm fundamentals including *Age, SOE, Independent, Dual, Audit*; financial conditions including *Size, ROA, Lev, Cash, Capex, BM*; and provincial characteristics including *GDP*. All variables are defined in Appendix A. To account for unobservable characteristics, our specification includes industry fixed effects (*Industry FE*) and year fixed effects (*Year FE*). Standard errors are clustered at the year level to address cross-sectional dependence in residuals when examining historical persistence, following Gu, Sun, and Zhou (2024). All continuous variables are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to mitigate outlier effects.

### 4.2. Does social trust enhance the financing effect of ESG disclosure
Table 2 examines how social trust affects ESG disclosure’s effect on debt financing costs. The dependent variable is the cost of debt capital (*COC*). Following prior research (e.g. Raimo et al. 2021; Xu, Xu, and Yu 2021), ESG disclosure exhibits a significant negative association with *COC* in column (1), suggesting it reduces financing costs. The interaction term *Social Trust* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* in column (3) is significant at 1%. This finding persists after including industry and year fixed effects (column (4)) and firm-level controls (column (5)). The interaction coefficients in columns (3) to (5) suggest that social trust strengthens ESG disclosure’s financing effect. Specifically, a one-standard-deviation increase in *Social Trust* (0.097) strengthens the negative effect of ESG disclosure on the cost of debt by about 0.171 percentage points (calculated as $1.761 \times 0.097$). Given the sample mean cost of debt of 2.108%, this effect accounts for about 8.1% of the average level, underscoring the economically significant role of social trust in amplifying the financing benefits of ESG disclosure. Collectively, these results support our hypothesis that social trust magnifies ESG disclosure’s impact on debt financing.

### 4.3. Difference-in-differences analysis
Our baseline results could suffer from endogeneity arising from omitted variables or reverse causality. Unobservable factors might jointly affect social trust, ESG disclosure, and financing costs. Alternatively, firms’ ESG disclosure behavior and financing decisions could shape regional trust. To address these endogeneity concerns, we adopt a DID approach using China’s social credit system implementation as an exogenous shock.

The National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC) and People’s Bank of China (PBOC) initiated the social credit system by designating 11 pilot cities in August 2015, followed by 32 additional cities in April 2016.$^6$ This setting provides a quasi-natural experiment to study changes in social trust for the following reasons. First, the selection of model cities is decided by the NDRC and PBOC and is not predicted by local governments or firms. Second, designated cities implemented credit information platforms and established reward-punishment mechanisms to enhance trustworthiness. Third, our untabulated city-year analyses confirm that regions experienced significantly higher social trust levels after their pilot program designation. This empirical evidence, combined with the exogenous selection process and the implementation of specific trust-building measures, strengthens the validity and reasonability of using pilot program implementation as the identification strategy.

To ensure a more rigorous and consistent comparison across the pre- and post-treatment periods, we exclude firms that only have observations after the policy implementation. This approach prevents our results from being biased by firm entries or exits around the event window and ensures that the estimated treatment effect is based on a stable panel of firms. This procedure results in a final sample of 7321 firm-year observations used in our DID model. We adopt the following regression model to examine how social trust causally influences ESG disclosure’s impact on debt financing costs:

$$COC_{i,t} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 Treatment_{i,t-1} \times ESG Disclosure_{i,t-1} + \alpha_2 Treatment_{i,t-1} + \alpha_3 ESG Disclosure_{i,t-1} + \sum \beta Controls_{i,t-1} + FirmFE + YearFE + \epsilon_{i,t} \quad (2)$$

where *COC* represents firm $i$’ cost of debt in year $t$. *Treatment* equals one if a firm’s city has been designated as a pilot city, and zero otherwise. *ESG Disclosure* captures a firm’s ESG disclosure level in year $t-1$. We include the control variables (*Controls*) as Model (1). A key concern is that the selection of pilot cities may not be random and could be correlated with regional characteristics that independently influence financing costs. To mitigate this potential selection bias and enhance the validity of our DID design, we augment our model with a set of city-level control variables: institutional maturity (*Marketization*), financial development (*FinDev*), and political importance (*PolImp*).$^7$ In addition, we include firm fixed effects to control for all time-invariant unobserved firm heterogeneity. The coefficient of *Treatment* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* measures how social credit system implementation affects ESG disclosure’s financing effect.

Table 3 presents our DID findings. In column (1) of Panel A, the significantly negative coefficient of *Treatment* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* ($p < 0.01$) suggests that social credit system implementation enhances ESG disclosure’s effect on reducing financing costs. In column (2) of Panel A, the coefficient of *Treatment* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* remains statistically significant after incorporating the city-level controls, which alleviates concerns that our DID findings are driven by systematic differences between cities.

As the parallel trend assumption is the fundamental prerequisite for a valid DID analysis, we further employ a dynamic DID model. Specifically, we replace the single *Treatment* variable with seven year-specific indicators. *Before$_{[-4]}$* to *Before$_{[-1]}$* equal one if the year is four, three, two, and one year before a firm’s headquarters city has been designated as a model city, respectively, and zero otherwise. *After$_{[1]}$*, *After$_{[2]}$* and *After$_{[3+]}$* equal one if the year is one, two, three and more years after the designation, respectively, and zero otherwise. The year of designation itself serves as the benchmark category. In column (3) of Panel A of Table 3, the results show that the coefficients for all pre-treatment periods are statistically insignificant and the coefficient becomes significantly negative one year after the social credit system’s construction. This indicates that, prior to the policy implementation, there was no differential trend in the ESG disclosure – cost of capital relationship between the treatment and control groups, confirming that the parallel trends assumption holds.

### 4.4. Propensity score matching (PSM) approach

Each firm in high-trust areas is then matched with its closest counterpart from low-trust areas using Model (1)’s control variables and fixed effects. Analysis of this matched sample (Table 4) confirms our baseline findings, indicating our results are not driven by self-selection or other omitted firm characteristics.

## 5. Mechanisms
Our analysis shows that social trust significantly enhances the financing benefits of ESG disclosure. Section 2 explains how social trust could impede false disclosure behavior, ensuring that its ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’. Furthermore, firms disclose their strengths and concerns in ESG practice. Regions with high social trust expect moral behaviors from firms, as social trust encourages them to engage in ESG practices to address these concerns, ensuring that they ‘act with perseverance’. As a result, social trust alleviates creditors’ concerns about firms’ ESG information quality and stabilizes creditors’ expectations of firms’ ESG performance, thus reducing creditors’ expectations of returns.

To examine this prediction, we conduct the following tests. First, if social trust can ensure that firms’ ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’, we predict that firms operating in high-trust regions demonstrate lower tendencies toward greenwashing through ESG reporting. Following Lin, Zhu, and Meng (2023) and Long, Wang, and Zhang (2024), we define greenwashing as the difference between a firm’s normalized ESG reporting rank and its normalized ESG performance rank among industry-province peers. We use Bloomberg ESG disclosure scores to capture ESG reporting levels and employ Sino-Securities ESG rating scores, which are composed of 17 key environmental indicators, 13 key social indicators, and 14 key governance indicators, to evaluate ESG performance. Specifically, we calculate the gap between a firm’s normalized Bloomberg ESG disclosure scores and normalized Sino-Securities ESG rating scores to measure the degree of greenwashing (*Greenwash*), which can be expressed as follows:

$$Greenwash_{i,t} = \left( \frac{ESG Disclosure_{i,t} - \overline{ESG Disclosure}}{\sigma_{ESG Disclosure}} \right) - \left( \frac{ESG Performance_{i,t} - \overline{ESG Performance}}{\sigma_{ESG Performance}} \right) \quad (3)$$

where *ESG Disclosure* represents firm $i$’s ESG reporting level, while *ESG Performance* captures ESG performance in year $t$. $\overline{ESG Disclosure}$ and $\overline{ESG Performance}$ denote the average ESG reporting scores and ESG performance scores of industry-province peers, respectively. $\sigma_{ESG Disclosure}$ and $\sigma_{ESG Performance}$ are the standard deviations of the peers’ ESG reporting scores and ESG performance scores, respectively. Column (1) of Table 7 reveals a negative *Social Trust* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* coefficient ($p < 0.05$), suggesting that firms from high-trust regions exhibit lower greenwashing tendencies in ESG disclosure.

Our analysis further demonstrates social trust’s role in enhancing the informative content of a firm’s disclosure. Following Gu, Sun, and Zhou (2024), information transparency is assessed using an indicator variable (3 = excellent, 2 = good, 1 = qualified, 0 = unqualified) based on disclosure quality ratings from China’s Shenzhen Stock Exchange and Shanghai Stock Exchange.$^{11}$ Column (2) documents a positive *Social Trust* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* coefficient ($p < 0.05$), suggesting stronger associations between ESG disclosure and information transparency within high-trust regions. Evidence from columns (1) and (2) of Table 7 indicate that social trust can impede a firm’s false disclosure and increase effective information disclosure, ensuring that the firm’s ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’.

Moreover, if social trust prompts firms with ESG disclosure to ‘act with perseverance’, we predict enhanced correlation between firms’ ESG disclosure and actual ESG performance in high-trust regions. Using Sino-Securities ESG ratings as our performance metric (*ESG Performance*), column (3) of Table 7 reveals a positive *Social Trust* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* coefficient ($p < 0.10$). This indicates stronger alignment of reporting practices and actual ESG performance under higher regional trust conditions. These findings imply that social trust incentivizes firms to follow through on ESG commitments, thereby improving ESG outcomes and stabilizing investor expectations regarding firms’ ESG performance.

### 6.3. Analyst coverage
Hypothesis 4 predicts that social trust exerts stronger effects in firms with weak analyst coverage. We measure analyst coverage by counting analyst teams following each firm annually. The sample is split at industry-year medians into high and low analyst coverage groups. Column (5) and (6) of Table 8 reveal a significantly negative *Social Trust* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* coefficient for the low analyst coverage group, while no statistical significance emerges for the high analyst coverage group. The intergroup tests confirm coefficient differences ($p = 0.002$). These results support our conjecture that social trust serves as an alternative governance mechanism when formal monitoring through analyst coverage is weak, significantly influencing the financing effect of corporate ESG disclosure.

### 6.4. Firm reputation
We lastly investigate whether firms’ reputation complements or substitutes social trust’s effect (Hypothesis 5). Following prior literature (Barnett and Pollock 2012; Roberts and Dowling 2002), we first selected 12 indicators to evaluate the firm reputation from the perspectives of consumers and society, creditors, shareholders, and the company itself.$^{12}$ Next, we employ factor analysis to calculate the firm reputation scores derived from these 12 indicators. We then partitioned these scores into deciles within a given year, with each score assigned a numerical value ranging from 1–10. Finally, the sample is divided at industry-year median ratings into good and poor reputation groups. Column (7) and (8) of Table 8 reveal a significantly negative *Social Trust* $\times$ *ESG Disclosure* coefficient in poor reputation group, but no statistical significance emerges in good reputation group. Coefficient differential across groups shows 10% significance ($p = 0.061$). This evidence suggests reputation substitutes for social trust, as trust mechanisms particularly benefit firms lacking reputational advantages.

## 7. Conclusion
This paper investigates how social trust shapes ESG disclosure’s effects on financing costs for Chinese listed firms during 2012–2020. Our evidence shows that ESG disclosure’s impact on financing costs strengthens in high-trust regions. These results hold across DID analysis, PSM, and a series of robustness checks. Mechanism analyses reveal firms within high-trust areas demonstrate lower greenwashing propensity, and their ESG disclosure contains more effective information, indicating that social trust ensures that their ESG disclosure ‘means what it says’. We also find that these firms’ ESG disclosure is more strongly correlated with their ESG performances, indicating that social trust urges firms with ESG disclosure to ‘act with perseverance’. Cross-sectional tests indicate enhanced effects for firms in less marketized regions, polluting industries, and firms with lower analyst coverage and worse reputation.

Our findings underscore how social trust shapes ESG practices within emerging markets. While growing numbers of firms are adopting ESG disclosure as a value-creation strategy, our results reveal that the economic benefits of such disclosures may be contingent upon the regional social trust levels. These results indicate that firms should consider informal institutions such as social trust when making ESG disclosure decisions. Furthermore, regulatory authorities in various countries have introduced corresponding regulations to guide firms in disclosing ESG information. Our research implies that regulatory authorities should focus not only on enforcing ESG regulations but also on fostering a conducive social trust environment, particularly within developing countries.

## Appendix A. Appendix Variable definitions

| Variable | Definitions |
| :--- | :--- |
| Social Trust | The level of social trust in the province where the firm is located, constructed based on the World Values Survey (WVS). |
| ESG Disclosure | Natural logarithm of the Bloomberg’s overall ESG disclosure scores. |
| COC | Sum of the firm’s interest expense, capitalized interests and other financing costs divided by total debt (%). |
| Size | Natural logarithm of total assets. |
| Age | Number of years up to the establishment year. |
| SOE | Indicator variable for state-owned enterprises equal to one if the ultimate controlling owner is either the central government or local governments, and zero otherwise |
| ROA | Net income divided by total assets. |
| Lev | Total debt divided by total assets. |
| Cash | Cash flow divided by total assets. |
| Capex | Capital expenditure divided by total assets, where capital expenditure is the sum of cash paid for the purchase and construction of fixed and intangible assets and other long-term assets minus the sum of cash recovered from disposal of fixed and intangible assets and other long-term assets. |
| BM | Total assets divided by market value. |
| Independent | Number of independent directors divided by the total number of all directors. |
| Dual | An indicator variable that equals one if the CEO is also the board chair, and zero otherwise. |
| Audit | We assign values of 4, 3, 2 and 1 to firms with audit opinions of standard unqualified opinions, qualified opinions, adverse opinions, and disclaimer of opinions, respectively. |
| GDP | Natural logarithm of gross domestic product. |
| Marketization | The province-level marketization index which represents the maturity of the institutional environment in different regions. The data are from the *Marketization Index of China’s Provinces: NERI Report* (Wang, Hu, and Fan 2021). |
| FinDev | The ratio of total loans from financial institutions to the city-level GDP. |
| PolImp | An indicator variable that equals one if the city is a municipality, a sub-provincial city, or a provincial capital, and zero otherwise. |
| CGSS | The level of social trust in the province where the firm is located, based on the China General Social Survey. |
| Reports | An indicator variable that equals one if the firm issue a CSR/ESG report, and zero otherwise. |
| COC2 | Net financing expenses divided by average of total liabilities at the end and beginning of the year (%). |
| Enforcement | The provincial-level ratio of closed economic cases to total cases received. |
| Legal Index | The ‘Development of Intermediary Organizations and the Legal Environment’ sub-index from the *Marketization Index of China’s Provinces: NERI Report* (Wang, Hu, and Fan 2021). |
| Greenwash | The difference between the normalized Bloomberg ESG disclosure scores and normalized Sino-Securities ESG rating scores. |
| Transparency | An indicator variable which is a four-tiered measure of listed firms’ disclosure quality published by the Shenzhen Stock Exchange and Shanghai Stock Exchange. We assign values of 3, 2, 1, and 0 to firms with excellent, good, qualified, and unqualified information transparency, respectively. |
| ESG Performance | Sino-Securities ESG rating scores. |
| Polluting Industries | We identify the following industries as polluting industries: mining, textile, oil and nuclear fuel processing, chemical raw materials and chemical manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, rubber and plastic products, non-ferrous metal metallurgy, electricity, heat production and supply. |
| Analyst Coverage | The number of analyst teams that have tracked and analyzed the firm in a given year. |
| Firm Reputation | Firm’s reputation score is constructed based on these 12 indicators: the indicators from the consumers and society perspective include asset, revenue, net profit, and market value; the indicators from the creditors’ perspective include leverage, current ratio, and long-term debt ratio; the indicators from the shareholders’ perspective include earnings per share, dividends per share, and whether the firm is audited by one of the Big 4 audits; and the indicators from the company’s perspective include sustainable growth rate and proportion of independent directors. |
